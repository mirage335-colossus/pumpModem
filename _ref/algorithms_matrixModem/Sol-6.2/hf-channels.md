# HF channel evidence for Matrix Modem

**Scope.** This chapter summarizes the propagation and noise conditions that a weak-signal HF modem should be designed and tested against. It emphasizes the difference between a useful design envelope and an assurance that every HF path will work. It does not specify a final waveform. Sources were checked against existing research on [signal levels and outages](../../highFidelity-triple_Robust_Modem/Fable-5.2/30-skywave-signal-level-and-outages.md), [mid-latitude and NVIS dispersion](../../highFidelity-triple_Robust_Modem/Fable-5.2/31-skywave-dispersion-midlatitude-nvis-longhaul.md), [high-latitude and equatorial dispersion](../../highFidelity-triple_Robust_Modem/Fable-5.2/32-skywave-dispersion-highlatitude-equatorial.md), [channel models](../../highFidelity-triple_Robust_Modem/Fable-5.2/33-hf-channel-models-and-simulators.md), [noise and interference](../../highFidelity-triple_Robust_Modem/Fable-5.2/50-noise-and-interference-mf-hf.md), and [nonstationary HF channels](../../resilience-triple_Robust_Modem/Fable-5.2/robust-nonstationary-channel-research.md), then against selected primary standards and measurement papers. Literature links below lead to the source paper or standard.

## Findings that constrain the design

1. **HF is a collection of changing propagation modes, not one fading law.** A receiver can see E- and F-region paths, one or more hops, ordinary and extraordinary magneto-ionic components, scatter, and ground-wave energy together. Each component can have its own delay, average gain, Doppler offset, and Doppler width. These parameters vary by circuit, frequency, local time, season, solar cycle, and geomagnetic state. A single equal-power two-ray model with a fixed offset is a useful controlled discriminator, but it is not a representative statistical channel by itself. ITU-R F.1487 describes this model structure and gives parameter sets for comparative modem testing. [ITU-R F.1487](https://www.itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-r/rec/f/R-REC-F.1487-0-200005-I%21%21PDF-E.pdf)

2. **Delay and Doppler must be specified separately.** A path may have a modest differential delay and severe fading-rate spread, or the reverse. Low and mid-latitude disturbed paths often challenge delay tolerance; high-latitude auroral and polar paths can have very large Doppler spreads. “6.25 Hz” therefore cannot by itself define a channel category. It could mean tone spacing, symbol rate, common carrier uncertainty, inter-ray Doppler offset, or a measured Doppler-spread statistic, and those have different consequences.

3. **A 160 ms symbol is much longer than typical HF differential delay, but it is not automatically immune to HF fading.** One path delayed by 1 ms is a small fraction of 160 ms, and even the common 7 ms disturbed NVIS delay benchmark is 4.4% of that duration. A 160 ms tone can therefore plausibly tolerate the usual multipath time scale when its detector treats unknown phase robustly and its pulse transitions do not create excessive intersymbol interference. But a 6.25 Hz tone separation is on the same order as ITU's low-latitude disturbed Doppler spread of 10 Hz and below its high-latitude 25 Hz approximate 95th-percentile exceedance boundary. Narrow frequency separation may then invite fading decorrelation and inter-tone leakage. The benchmark is a paired delay/spread test point, not proof that a modem fails precisely at the quoted number.

4. **Deep fades, propagation failure, and usable low C/N0 are distinct.** Even if a receiver handles within-symbol multipath, absorption, loss of ionospheric support, destructive path interference, external noise, or a storm can remove the signal for periods much longer than a symbol. ITU monthly median prediction is not a worst-case bound. Some physical outages are not fixed by additional correlation computation or a more efficient receiver.

5. **Recent regional measurements add nuance but do not replace the old standards.** A 2024 low-cost NVIS campaign over Java observed quiet-condition rms delay spreads from 0 to 1.3 ms (mean 0.4627 ms) and Doppler shift from 0.7 to 1.1 Hz over one week. The authors explicitly call for longer coverage, and their own ionogram showed an E-sporadic layer close to the sounder frequency. This supports real equatorial-region measurement and a useful quiet baseline, not a disturbed-channel percentile model. A 2026 published study of a mid-latitude 10 MHz signal reports non-Gaussian Doppler-residual distributions, with daytime Cauchy-like and nighttime skewed exponential-power or log-normal behavior. Its observable is common Doppler residual over a particular link, not the per-path Doppler spectrum used in F.1487, so it warns against unexamined Gaussian-tail assumptions without replacing the standard spread model. [Dear et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1029/2023RS007877), [Fernandes et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.3389/fspas.2026.1713968)

## Reference channel conditions

ITU-R F.1487 Annex 3 gives directly useful representative paired conditions. It defines differential time delay between two modes, and frequency spread as **2σ**, and advises quoting the actual parameters used. These categories are experimental reference points and are not universal geographical percentiles.

| Region and condition | Differential mode delay | Doppler spread | Interpretation for Matrix Modem |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Mid-latitude quiet | 0.5 ms | 0.1 Hz | Stable-channel baseline; most useful for validating sensitivity absent severe dispersion. |
| Mid-latitude moderate | 1 ms | 0.5 Hz | Routine impaired baseline. |
| Mid-latitude disturbed | 2 ms | 1 Hz | Disturbed long-path baseline. |
| Mid-latitude disturbed NVIS | 7 ms | 1 Hz | Long delay with modest spread. Include ground-wave component if in scope. |
| Low-latitude quiet | 0.5 ms | 0.5 Hz | Equatorial quiet baseline. |
| Low-latitude moderate | 2 ms | 1.5 Hz | Useful nominal difficult case. |
| Low-latitude disturbed | 6 ms | 10 Hz | Coupled delay and rapid fading. |
| High-latitude quiet | 1 ms | 0.5 Hz | Quiet auroral/polar path baseline. |
| High-latitude moderate | 3 ms | 10 Hz | Doppler-dominated challenge. |
| High-latitude disturbed | 7 ms | 30 Hz | Severe stress test. |

F.1487 additionally says differential delays of 4 ms at low latitudes and 5 ms at high latitudes, and Doppler spreads of 3 Hz and 25 Hz respectively, are exceeded approximately 5% of the time. Those are its stated indicative occurrence statistics, with the scope and dataset of that 2000 Recommendation; they should not be promoted to current universal regional distributions. Its suggested comparative sweep covers delays from 0–20 ms and spreads 0.1–40 Hz. The standard's 8-PSK example shows sharply rising error once delay and Doppler impairments become sufficiently severe, and states that extra S/N may no longer restore performance in its plateau region. That is evidence against assuming all channel impairments can be overcome merely by extra energy, but it is not a performance prediction for an orthogonal noncoherent Matrix waveform. [F.1487 Annex 3](https://www.itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-r/rec/f/R-REC-F.1487-0-200005-I%21%21PDF-E.pdf)

The same standard's comparative tables matter because they discourage choosing one “typical worst” point. Low-latitude distributed conditions often emphasize delay. High-latitude conditions can combine small delay with large Doppler. An architecture intended to help HF should at least preserve separate knobs for (a) common frequency shift/drift, (b) per-path Doppler center, (c) per-path Doppler width, (d) differential delay, and (e) time-varying path gain. Combining all of them into a single “Doppler” field loses physically important distinctions.

## What measured soundings say beyond the reference model

### Disturbed transauroral wideband measurements

Nissen and Bello measured an HF channel with a 1 MHz probe and reported per-mode statistics from about 100 measurements after excluding responses with significant energy from multiple propagation modes. Across that campaign, the reported Doppler spread had a median of 5 Hz, 10th and 90th percentiles of 1.75 and 13 Hz, and a maximum of 17 Hz. The quiet-geomagnetic floor in that experiment was about 2 Hz, already above the less than 0.4 Hz nominal mid-latitude condition cited by the authors. A 15.5 MHz disturbed snapshot had 7.5 Hz rms Doppler spread, about 100 μs rms delay spread, and a 6.6 Hz mean Doppler shift. Larger values coincided with M-class solar flares. The paper uses rms **2σ** spreads and narrow selections of individual modes, so these observations do not represent all composite HF channels or global occurrence rates. They establish that a few Hz of fading spread occurs in measured disturbed cases and that common shift, spread width, and delay dispersion are separate observables. [Nissen and Bello 2003](https://doi.org/10.1029/2002RS002746)

### Polar-cap, trough, and auroral measurements

Stocker et al. analyzed 4.6–18.4 MHz circuits spanning the 2001 solar maximum and 2009–2012 minimum-to-rising phase. Their high-latitude polar-cap path had 95th-percentile Doppler spreads that reached 13 Hz at 14.36 MHz in winter daytime. Several bins exceeded 7 Hz, but more than half of those observations came from only two days. The associated mid-latitude/subauroral trough circuit had much lower spread, often around 1–2 Hz, while multipath spread could be greater. Their observations across sunspot-number bins did not yield one simple monotonic relationship. Some bins showed thresholds, some approximately linear trends, some peaks, and some no clear pattern. Doppler resolution was 0.5 Hz on the newer records and 0.625 Hz in 2001. The Doppler measure is the narrowest band containing 80% of received power. The composite delay measure excludes weak modes, and the analysis retained only observations with at least 6 dB SNR in 3 kHz and removed interfered observations. These are conditional statistics for received signals, with a different width convention from F.1487. The study's historical paths and limited event counts are important: high percentiles may be event-dominated and solar-cycle response is circuit/season/frequency dependent. [Stocker et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1002/2013RS005264)

The older disturbed-channel measurements and F.1487's high-latitude 30 Hz reference together support a useful stress range from roughly 10 Hz to 30 Hz, not a claim that all polar operation sees these values. High latitude is not one homogeneous “bad channel”: geomagnetic disturbance, route position relative to trough/oval/cap, solar illumination, frequency, and event history matter. A harsh test should be labeled by channel parameters and conditions rather than only “auroral.”

**Extreme detectable returns.** The existing DAMSON source audit records a materially different 194 km Harstad–Kiruna path. Conditional on SNR between −5 and +5 dB in 3 kHz, its reported 95th-percentile composite 80%-power Doppler widths were 49.5 and 57 Hz in the two analysis periods, reaching 73 Hz in the 14.4–21.9 MHz daytime-inclusive group. The corresponding cells report first-to-last-mode delays in the 7.4–11.4 ms range. The Doppler and delay statistics are marginal and do not establish simultaneous extremes. Values approach the sounder's ±40 Hz and 12.5 ms observation limits. Off-great-circle auroral return is the thesis's interpretation, with no direction measurement to confirm it. Its journal abstract reports a smaller upper value of 55 Hz, with the difference in selection unresolved in the existing audit. Separately, NRL results found regimes where weak diffuse return coexisted with or replaced a stronger specular component. A source-derived severe diagnostic should include unequal gains, shifted components and wide diffuse spread. The 30 Hz Gaussian reference remains a repeatable screen, with neither a universal maximum nor a justified acquisition cap. [Project audit and source locators](../../highFidelity-triple_Robust_Modem/Fable-5.2/32-skywave-dispersion-highlatitude-equatorial.md#a-damson-doppler-and-multipath-sounding-network-northern-scandinavia), [Angling thesis, Table 6-6](https://figshare.le.ac.uk/articles/thesis/Measured_and_simulated_scattering_functions_and_their_impact_on_high_frequency_HF_systems/10162685), [Wagner et al. transauroral measurements](https://doi.org/10.1029/94RS03177).

### Low-latitude / equatorial NVIS

The Java campaign used a 5.4 MHz NVIS link with a measured one-week quiet interval. The observed rms delay range of 0–1.3 ms is consistent in scale with F.1487's low-latitude quiet 0.5 ms reference, but does not sample the 6 ms disturbed setting. A measurement at 13:30 local time showed multiple clusters and two groups of impulses, plausibly reflecting coexisting E and F paths when the sporadic-E critical frequency was about 7.1 MHz. This is concrete evidence that “quiet” conditions can still have distinct propagation components and that frequency-selective multipath can be mode-driven. The system sampled every 15 minutes and sought broad delay/Doppler coverage, but the authors say more measurements are needed for a comprehensive channel characterization. The values should be used as one regional quiet anchor, not an equatorial worst case. [Dear et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1029/2023RS007877)

Separate 2021 NVIS work on ordinary and extraordinary magneto-ionic modes reports the extraordinary mode had about 15% greater availability over its particular observation schedule, similar mean delay spread around 0.3 ms, and up to 4 dB improvement from polarization diversity. This cautions against treating the two magneto-ionic components as identical or assuming they always combine as equal-strength independent branches. Its one 5.4 MHz link does not establish gains available to a single-antenna, audio-interface modem. [Tartón et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.3390/s21062210)

### More recent statistics of common Doppler

The 2026 Doppler-residual paper analyzes a 10 MHz WWV path using high-rate observations and a long-term record. It finds daytime residual variability better described by Cauchy statistics and nighttime behavior by skewed fits including exponential-power and log-normal distributions. It connects diurnal and seasonal structure to solar zenith angle and ionospheric electron density and reports storm/flare cases with short attenuation events. These are common frequency residual statistics after the chosen processing and link geometry, not estimates of the scattering-function width for every independent path. Heavy-tailed residuals imply Gaussian-derived search margins may under-cover shifts if this observable applies to the receiver's acquisition problem. Verify against the actual waveform, receiver oscillator error, and a multicomponent path before using the distribution quantitatively. The study is newly published and its single link limits generalization. [Fernandes et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.3389/fspas.2026.1713968)

## Propagation loss, fading, and genuine outage

ITU-R P.533 predicts monthly-median skywave circuit performance and explicitly adds day-to-day and within-hour variations to estimate time percentages. Its comparison to historical D1 observations has approximately 10.5 dB standard deviation overall, with larger error for some long paths and at high geomagnetic latitudes. Monthly medians and a channel's short-term Doppler/delay profile answer different questions. A design can pass a F.1487 dispersion test and still fail because the path is in an absorption or support outage. Conversely, a high path loss does not establish high Doppler spread. [ITU-R P.533-14](https://www.itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-r/rec/p/R-REC-P.533-14-201908-I%21%21PDF-E.pdf)

The existing project research gives material examples that prevent “worst case” from being reduced to a finite delay/Doppler pair:

- X-class flares increase sunlit D-region absorption, with stronger effects at lower HF. The existing source review documents modeled 20–40 dB absorption at 6.4 MHz in a specific X1.3 event and modeled/observed discrepancies relative to operational D-RAP prediction.
- Polar-cap absorption from solar energetic particles can exceed 100 dB at HF and persist for days. This is effectively an outage for a low-power single-hop modem under many link budgets.
- Geomagnetic storms can lower the F2 critical frequency and degrade usable frequency, while auroral precipitation can increase Doppler spread and absorption. Storm onset, main phase, and recovery phase can impose different conditions. A single stationary sample cannot represent the episode.
- Fading can remain correlated over several seconds or minutes. Independent-symbol Bernoulli erasures are therefore optimistic unless the code/interleaver spans the measured fade-correlation time.

These are conditions to report separately from symbol-level resilience. For store-and-forward communications without retransmission or adaptive configuration, the practical question is a probability of completing the whole message under a stated distribution of link outages and fade duration, not only an average BER in a stationary channel. There is no finite universal “worst HF channel”: a long-lived outage can make any finite-power, one-way modem fail.

### Independent 2026 checks on route and event effects

- An Alaskan network at 2.9 and 3.4 MHz observed a mixture of concentrated echoes and range–Doppler broadening around a November 2023 subauroral/auroral trough crossing. Broadening depended on ray path and interval, with some Doppler spreading before a corresponding range spread appeared. The researchers report Gaussian FWHM only on adequate fits, after 10 s coherent processing and 1 min incoherent averaging. They explicitly leave secular motion within a minute as a possible confound. This is evidence to keep both observables and path identity, with no 160 ms modem calibration implied by their 10 W sounding waveform. [Alaska trough study, §§2 and 4](https://doi.org/10.3389/fspas.2026.1848834).
- Observations of the November 2025 geomagnetic storm show decreases and increases in received WSPR reports depending on path, time and frequency. For 10 MHz Shiraz transmissions the counts went from 29 to 1 at one matched time and from 3 to 42 at another. Reporting stations and decode thresholds condition these counts. They establish that a storm can suppress or open paths, with neither a universal gain penalty nor an unbiased distribution of outage duration. [Mahmoudian et al. 2026, §3](https://doi.org/10.1029/2025SW004917).
- European ionosondes observed different durations for enhanced absorption associated with a flare and with particle precipitation. The paper's radio-quiet spectrogram procedure uses HF broadcasters as opportunistic illuminators and establishes a qualitative signature, leaving quantitative absorption prediction for later work. Keep this slow loss process separate from 10–30 Hz modem stress profiles. [Verhulst et al. 2026, §§4–5](https://doi.org/10.1029/2026RS008688).
- A September 2026 comparison of REC533, ICEPAC and VOACAP reports overall REC533 median-field prediction error with 0.28 dB bias and 9.81 dB standard deviation over selected CCIR D1 observations. The archive chiefly covers historic solar cycles 19–21. This is a field-strength residual across observation conditions, separate from short-term fading, and does not give a reliable Gaussian margin for a particular antenna pair. A 10 dB error in assumed received carrier power changes the ideal very-low-SNR airtime estimate by about a factor of ten. [Zhu et al. 2026, §§2–4](https://doi.org/10.1029/2025SW004907).

## Noise and interference environment

At HF, the relevant noise is often external to the receiver. ITU-R P.372 separates atmospheric, man-made, and galactic noise and provides frequency-, location-, and time-dependent statistical models. Atmospheric noise from lightning varies with season, local time, and geography. Man-made noise depends strongly on site and can dominate in populated/electrically noisy settings. Galactic noise becomes more important where atmospheric/man-made noise is low and near the upper HF band. Receiver noise figure alone cannot define the noise floor, and a quiet intended band does not mean a quiet path or a stationary Gaussian noise process.

Impulsive lightning and intermittent co-channel signals matter to a weak-signal modem because matched integration and many-hypothesis searches can turn a short transient into a large false score if thresholds assume only white Gaussian noise. Interference rejection, clipping, robust statistics, and null-hypothesis calibration must be evaluated against measured ambient captures with the same bandwidth, antenna/location class, and observing time as the intended receiver. Do not infer acoustic room-noise performance or a local SDR spectrum survey from ITU external-noise medians. P.533 itself cautions that P.372 monopole-ground noise factors are first approximations for other antennas and does not supply within-hour variability for man-made noise.

Current recommendation: [ITU-R P.372-18 (September 2026)](https://www.itu.int/rec/R-REC-P.372-18-202609-I/en). The detailed project review of P.372-17 datasets, noise transformations, HF site caveats, impulse noise, receiver/front-end effects, and a measured replay is in [_ref/highFidelity-triple_Robust_Modem/Fable-5.2/50-noise-and-interference-mf-hf.md](../../highFidelity-triple_Robust_Modem/Fable-5.2/50-noise-and-interference-mf-hf.md).

## Translating channel scales into waveform design questions

For a proposed 160 ms symbol and nominal 6.25 Hz spacing/rate:

- A 160 ms observation has a Fourier-bin spacing of 6.25 Hz. This is a transform resolution fact, not a guarantee of orthogonality under unknown carrier offset, Doppler spread, transmitter/receiver clock error, multipath, or window/pulse shaping.
- ITU mid-latitude quiet/moderate spreads (0.1–0.5 Hz) are small relative to 6.25 Hz, while low-latitude disturbed 10 Hz and high-latitude disturbed 30 Hz are larger. If an implementation needs each tone to remain spectrally isolated over an entire symbol, those severe categories challenge that assumption.
- A fixed inter-path Doppler **center** can be searched or tracked. A stochastic Doppler **spread** is energy distributed around a path's center, which causes time-selective fading and cannot be corrected by simply widening a common frequency-offset grid.
- A path delay of a few milliseconds is short relative to the symbol, but it creates frequency-selective constructive/destructive addition across tone frequencies. The tone spacing and the frequency dependence of channel gain jointly determine whether some alphabet symbols are deeply faded. Simple long-duration tones solve neither frequency-selective nulls nor prolonged fades.
- Pure tone energy detection is naturally insensitive to constant phase. It is not invariant to within-symbol phase rotation, rapid fading through a null, neighboring-tone leakage, common frequency error, or unknown frequency-selective gain. A no-data/null hypothesis also needs explicit noise/interference statistics; cryptographic randomization of tone selection does not remove the physical energy of a transmitted signal.

These are physical deductions from the cited channel ranges, not measured Matrix Modem results. They argue for a staged comparison: a simple orthogonal/noncoherent baseline on ITU channels; then measured or stochastic path gain evolution and non-Gaussian offset stress; then actual ambient HF captures. The matrix calculation should implement a stated detector or likelihood over many candidate templates, while the channel abstraction remains traceable to the measured delay-Doppler structure.

## A representative evidence plan

The following small set covers materially different channel mechanisms without implying exhaustive global qualification.

1. **Sensitivity baseline:** AWGN and quiet mid-latitude F.1487 (0.5 ms, 0.1 Hz), with constant phase and frequency offsets swept separately. Establish false alarm, miss probability, message completion, and required received (E_s/N_0) or (C/N_0).
2. **Ordinary impaired path:** mid-latitude moderate (1 ms, 0.5 Hz) and low-latitude moderate (2 ms, 1.5 Hz). Use both static unequal-gain two-mode and stochastic fading paths. Include frequency-selective fades across the complete configured band.
3. **Delay-heavy case:** disturbed NVIS (7 ms, 1 Hz) with an optional direct/groundwave component and E/F-mode-like taps. Add longer tails only where source data supports them; keep any artificial longer values labeled as stress tests.
4. **Doppler-heavy case:** high-latitude moderate/disturbed (3/7 ms and 10/30 Hz) with nonzero mode-specific Doppler centers. Add time-varying path gains and a common offset/drift separately. Compare Gaussian spectrum assumptions with heavy-tailed/common-residual alternatives where evidence applies.
5. **Event/outage case:** measured or physically parameterized flare/auroral/absorption episodes with fades correlated over realistic duration. Report burst or message completion probability and residual delivery after the outage. A stationary BER curve is insufficient.
6. **Noise/interference case:** calibrated P.372-shaped background plus measured ambient captures, impulsive events, and narrowband/co-channel interference. Run the receiver's null test against signal-free windows from the same captures.

For modem-level confidence rather than a few illustrative points, follow F.1487's advice to state the paired delay/spread values and map BER or error outcomes over the grid. Its old rule of testing at least the larger of `3000/B_D` seconds or `100/(BER × R_b)` illustrates why high-confidence BER evidence can be expensive. Matrix work can use bounded hierarchical sampling and separate channel-path strata, but simulations should preserve temporal correlation and the relevant event mixture. Randomizing independent symbol fades would erase the feature being tested.

For experiment portability, record link route/latitude zone, frequency, local time/season, solar/geomagnetic classification, whether spreads are rms/2σ/percentile or maximum, multipath weighting and tap correlation, noise reference plane, and receiver search bounds. Never compare “Doppler = 10 Hz” measurements without identifying whether the number is a common shift, spread width, 2σ, or bound.

## Sources and limits

- **ITU-R F.1487-0 (2000), “Testing of HF modems with bandwidths of up to about 12 kHz using ionospheric channel simulators.”** Primary international recommendation. Defines stationary Gaussian-scatter channel models and conventional comparative categories. Its convenient parameter sets are not contemporary global statistics and its stationarity assumptions omit event-level outages.
- **ITU-R P.533-14 (2019), “Method for the prediction of the performance of HF circuits.”** Primary monthly-median circuit method with time-percentage treatment. Useful link-budget and variability framework, not exact local real-time prediction.
- **ITU-R P.372-18 (2026), “Radio noise.”** Current primary external-noise recommendation. Statistical and antenna reference-plane assumptions must be retained.
- **Nissen and Bello (2003), “Measured channel parameters for the disturbed wide-bandwidth HF channel,” Radio Science 38, 1023.** Direct measured scattering functions. Historical campaign; selected individual-mode records, not globally representative composites.
- **Stocker et al. (2013), “Observations of Doppler and delay spreads on HF signals received over polar cap and trough paths at various stages of the solar cycle,” Radio Science 48, 638–645.** Multi-year polar-cap and trough data. Some upper percentiles are event-dominated; path selection matters.
- **Dear et al. (2024), “Ionospheric Channel Impulse Response Measurement System for NVIS Propagation Mode Over Java Island Based on Low-Cost SDR Platform,” Radio Science 59, e2023RS007877.** Direct equatorial-region NVIS sounding and published data. One week of quiet conditions, with authors explicitly requesting more measurements.
- **Tartón et al. (2021), “Analysis of the Ordinary and Extraordinary Ionospheric Modes for NVIS Digital Communications Channels,” Sensors 21, 2210.** Regional mode-specific availability and polarization combining result. Single frequency and link; not a generic single-antenna benefit.
- **Fernandes et al. (2026), “Characterizing ionospheric variability through HF Doppler measurements: a statistical and numerical ray tracing analysis,” Frontiers in Astronomy and Space Sciences 13, 1713968.** Recent long-term 10 MHz path result, useful counterpoint on non-Gaussian residuals. Common Doppler residuals should not be conflated with per-ray Doppler spread.

The literature supports useful conditional channel categories, significant regional/temporal diversity, long correlated fades and true outage states, and no universal 6.25 Hz channel cutoff. It does not establish a Matrix waveform's sensitivity, message duration, required compute, or probability of completion on HF. Those require a detector, coding, message-size and link-budget definition followed by matched simulation and measured-capture evidence.
