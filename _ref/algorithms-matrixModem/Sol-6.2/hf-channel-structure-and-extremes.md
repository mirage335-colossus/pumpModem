# HF channel structure and extremes for the coherence limit

25 November 2026 · Literature review focused on the assumptions of the fading bound

The [fading-limit study](fading-limit-and-mfsk-alternatives.md) shows that the peak-limited noncoherent bound depends on four properties of the channel: the shape of the Doppler power spectrum, whether part of the power is specular, how long the statistics stay stationary, and whether the noise is Gaussian. This review collects measured evidence on those four properties from moderate to worst-case HF skywave conditions. It complements, and does not repeat, the [HF channel evidence](hf-channels.md) and [propagation envelope](channel-envelope-review.md), which cover F.1487, Nissen and Bello, Stocker et al., DAMSON, the Java NVIS campaign and the 2026 WWV Doppler residual study.

Access is marked **[full]** (primary text read), **[abs]** (abstract or metadata), **[sec]** (secondary source) and **[derived]** (arithmetic for this review). Many important sources were reachable only as abstracts; they are listed at the end. The measured values come from specific paths, seasons and selection rules and are not occurrence statistics for all HF links.

## Findings that change the bound's inputs

1. **Quiet mid-latitude one-hop paths are much more coherent than the Watterson moderate profile.** FST4W measurements show received mode widths near 0.06 Hz at 14 MHz over 1535 km, including equipment, and spectral widths that differ by two orders of magnitude between a 20 km and a 5291 km path. Stable-carrier Doppler records show discrete traces that wander slowly. On such paths the Gaussian–Rayleigh bound at 0.5–1 Hz is pessimistic, provided the receiver tracks the drift.
2. **Measured spectra are often peaked with heavy tails, multi-peaked and slanted rather than single Gaussians.** The Vogler–Hoffmeyer model uses an exponential autocorrelation (Lorentzian spectrum) because it matched data better than a Gaussian. Equatorial spread-F tails are roughly exponential. Applying the bound needs the effective bandwidth `B_eff = 1/∫S²`, not a width quoted at an arbitrary level ([conversion table](fading-limit-and-mfsk-alternatives.md#is-it-a-worst-case)).
3. **Within an hour, amplitude statistics are usually Rayleigh, but single stable modes and sporadic E are often Rician, and two comparable rays are worse than Rayleigh.** The Rician cases make the bound pessimistic. The two-ray cases (O and X modes on north–south or NVIS paths, high and low rays near the MUF, short and long path) make outage worse than the bound suggests.
4. **The worst mid- and low-latitude regime is evening equatorial spread-F.** On half of evenings a Marshall Islands path exceeded 6 Hz of Doppler spread at −10 dB and 15 Hz at −30 dB, with more than 20 Hz not uncommon. High-latitude extremes reach tens of Hz, as covered in the [existing review](hf-channels.md#polar-cap-trough-and-auroral-measurements).
5. **Availability is dominated by events outside any stationary fading model.** Shortwave fadeouts last a mean of 13–39 minutes for C1 to X10 flares, polar cap absorption can last about ten days on polar paths, storms can close the F2 layer overnight, and day-to-day signal deciles span 8–17 dB.
6. **HF noise is impulsive, varies by about 10 dB between sites and deciles, and includes narrowband carriers.** For a fixed noise power this raises the information limit, but energy detectors must be protected to benefit.

## Doppler spectrum shape

- **Vogler–Hoffmeyer stochastic model.** The Watterson Gaussian tap spectrum was validated on one path with a few minutes of data from one day, and CCIR Report 549-2 states the Gaussian-scatter model is not valid for all HF channels. The model offers a Gaussian shape and a “peak” shape from an exponential autocorrelation (a Lorentzian spectrum), motivated by SRI measurements in which a sharp narrow peak was characteristic, for example on a polar long path. Delay-dependent Doppler is modeled as a linear slant, and a 126 km mid-latitude path showed curved delay dependence from nonlinear ionospheric change. In its figures the exponential simulation looks closer to the data than the Gaussian; formal tests were deferred. [Vogler and Hoffmeyer, NTIA Report 90-255, 1990, §§ 1.2, 2.1.2–2.1.3, Figs. 5–9, full](https://its.ntia.gov/publications/download/90-255_ocr.pdf)
- **Transfer-function model.** The follow-on report models the random process as having an exponential autocorrelation and states that slant is observed frequently in measured scattering functions. On an 80 km near-vertical auroral path near Fairbanks, quiet conditions gave a scattering function that looks more deterministic, 5.3 MHz showed separate O and X modes, and intense spread-F split the return into two regions. Comparisons are qualitative. [Vogler and Hoffmeyer, NTIA Report 92-284, 1992, §§ 2–3, full](https://www.its.ntia.gov/publications/download/92-284.pdf)
- **Equatorial spread-F tails.** On a 700 km Marshall Islands path, 50% of evenings exceeded 6 Hz of Doppler spread at −10 dB and 15 Hz at −30 dB [Fitzgerald, Argo and Carlos, Radio Science 34(1), 1999, abs](https://doi.org/10.1029/1998RS900013). The −30/−10 dB width ratio of about 2.5 lies between a Gaussian (1.73) and a two-sided exponential (3.0) and far below a Lorentzian (10.5), so the tails are heavier than Gaussian [derived; the two widths are median statistics, not necessarily from the same spectra].
- **Backscatter analogs.** SuperDARN autocorrelations are better fitted by Lorentzian spectra in high-width regions and Gaussian spectra in low-width regions, with double-peaked spectra more common at high width [Parkinson, Hannah and Dyson, Ann. Geophys. 26:877, 2008, abs](https://doi.org/10.5194/angeo-26-877-2008); [Ponomarenko and Waters, Ann. Geophys. 24:115, 2006, abs](https://doi.org/10.5194/angeo-24-115-2006). These are backscatter measurements and transfer only by analogy.

**Implication.** For the bound, a Gaussian spectrum matched at the −10 dB width is about 1 dB optimistic against a Lorentzian of the same −10 dB width, and about 3 dB optimistic if matched at the half-power width [derived]. Separated narrow components are the opposite case: they concentrate power and make the bound pessimistic. For the receiver, heavy tails mainly add leakage into neighboring tones, which argues for tone spacing set by a low-level (−20 to −30 dB) width rather than a nominal spread.

## Amplitude statistics

- **Historical survey.** On MF/HF paths up to 1500 km, fading was specular plus random with distributions from Rayleigh to near-Gaussian (Rician with large `K`). Rapid fading was Rayleigh while slow quasi-periodic fading followed Nakagami-type laws. Long auroral paths were essentially Rayleigh. Sporadic-E returns followed Rice distributions with a regular reflected component. [Salaman, *Historical survey of fading at medium and high radio frequencies*, NBS Technical Note 133, pp. 7–12, full](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/Legacy/TN/nbstechnicalnote133.pdf)
- **Engineering default.** ITU-R P.842 uses within-the-hour deciles of +5 and −8 dB, which match Rayleigh power deciles about the median (+5.2/−8.2 dB) [ITU-R P.842-3, Table 1, full; derived comparison](https://www.itu.int/rec/R-REC-P.842/en).
- **Single-ray links.** A 2022 Russian study states that single-ray shortwave link amplitudes follow Rice or generalized Rayleigh rather than Nakagami laws in 90% of cases [Pashintsev et al., *Izv. VUZov Rossii: Radioelektronika* 25(6), 2022, abs](https://doi.org/10.32603/1993-8985-2022-25-6-22-39). The `K` values were not accessible.
- **More severe than Rayleigh.** Nakagami `m` between 0.5 and 1 has been observed on long HF links [Nakagami 1960 via Yip and Ng, IEEE TCOM 48(2), 2000, sec]. Mechanisms with HF evidence include equal O and X amplitudes on nearly field-aligned paths, high/low-ray interference near the MUF ([Deacon et al. 2021](#sporadic-e)), and focusing cusps from traveling disturbances with 20–30 dB mode amplitude fluctuations [Vertogradov et al., *Radiophys. Quantum Electron.*, 2005, abs].

**Implication.** Rayleigh within the hour is the right default. Rician single modes make the bound pessimistic and reward a long-coherence receiver branch. Two near-equal rays create deterministic deep nulls whose outage exceeds Rayleigh, even though their spectrum is line-like; polarization matching, frequency hopping and interleaving over the beat period address that case.

## Coherence on quiet paths

- **Long-symbol amateur measurements.** FST4W submodes use tone spacings of 1.46, 0.56, 0.18 and 0.089 Hz with symbols of 0.683, 1.792, 5.547 and 11.2 s. Spectral widths differed by two orders of magnitude between a 20 km and a 5291 km path; the mode width was about 0.06 Hz at 14 MHz over 1535 km, including equipment. Mid-latitude path-spread histograms were right-skewed with their mode in the lowest bin, while a 12,254 km trans-equatorial path was near-Gaussian with its mode away from zero. At 20 m the 900 s mode was practical mainly on one-hop paths and the 1800 s mode only rarely. Equipment spread can exceed ionospheric spread on one-hop paths. [Griffiths, Elmore, Robinett, Rhymes and Watrous, *FST4W on the HF Bands*, TAPR DCC 2022, Table 1, §§ 4.1–4.2, Figs. 8–10, full](https://web.tapr.org/tech_docs/DCCpapers/2022%2002.pdf)
- **Stable-carrier Doppler.** HamSCI Grape receivers on WWV show Doppler generally within ±1 Hz, with traveling-disturbance oscillations of 0.25 Hz or more at periods of 15 minutes or longer, daily sunrise peaks and sunset drops, and possible O, X and co-channel WWVH components that break a single-carrier assumption [Collins et al., ESSD 15:1403, 2023, full](https://doi.org/10.5194/essd-15-1403-2023); [Romanek et al., SARA 2021, full](https://hamsci.org/sites/default/files/publications/2021_SARA/SARA%202021%20Romanek%20Final%20Paper.pdf). An 810 kHz study resolved about 0.1 Hz of disturbance Doppler at 0.016 Hz resolution [Trop et al., Atmos. Meas. Tech. 18:1909, 2025, full](https://doi.org/10.5194/amt-18-1909-2025). Spectrogram atlases catalogue one- and two-hop modes, sporadic E, high and low rays, dawn Doppler and flare Doppler flashes.
- **NVIS.** A 170 km mid-latitude NVIS sounding measured Doppler spreads of ±0.5, ±5 and ±2 Hz at 3.9, 5.7 and 6.7 MHz with delay spreads of 4, 10 and 6 ms, and channels at 3.9 and 6.7 MHz nearly static over 140 s [Hervás et al., *Sensors* 20(9):2486, 2020, § 3.4.5, full](https://doi.org/10.3390/s20092486).

**Implication.** Quiet one-hop paths can provide tens of seconds of coherence per mode, which is far more energy per coherence interval than the Gaussian moderate profile suggests. Collecting it needs dwells or coherent segments of many seconds and drift tracking of at least ±1 Hz per day and 0.25–1 Hz per traveling disturbance. Beyond about 5–10 s, long symbols begin to resolve the separation between modes and the drift, so the receiver should treat a quiet path as a few drifting narrow lines rather than one wide Gaussian process.

## Polarization and magneto-ionic fading

- O and X beating causes polarization fading even on a single hop. Near sunrise and sunset only the X wave may propagate, giving less fading and circular polarization. A matched circularly polarized antenna can isolate O from X by more than 25 dB on NVIS, and dual-circular reception is recommended as diversity [Witvliet, *Near Vertical Incidence Skywave: propagation mechanism, impact of antenna, backscatter and solar flares*, Nordic HF conference paper, 2022, §§ 3.1.3 and 3.2.4, full](https://ris.utwente.nl/ws/portalfiles/portal/284844584/2022_Witvliet_NVIS_Nordic_HF.pdf), citing [Witvliet et al., “Measuring the isolation of the circularly polarized characteristic waves in NVIS propagation,” IEEE AP Magazine 57(3):120–145, 2015](https://doi.org/10.1109/MAP.2015.2445633).
- An 850 km link with two complementary circular polarizations showed low correlation between them [Erhel, Lemur, Oger, Le Masson and Marie, IEEE AP Magazine 58(6):38–48, 2016, abs](https://doi.org/10.1109/MAP.2016.2609799). In ray-tracing simulation over 300–1500 km and 3–15 MHz, two orthogonal horizontal dipoles or vertical loops gave about 3.1 times the outage capacity of a vertical-dipole reference [Erhel et al., IES 2015, full; simulation only].
- An NVIS study reported up to 4 dB from polarization diversity and about 15% greater availability for the extraordinary mode ([existing review](hf-channels.md#low-latitude--equatorial-nvis)).
- Polarization fades last from a fraction of a second to a few seconds; focusing fades from traveling disturbances last ten minutes or more [IPS/Bureau of Meteorology, *Introduction to HF Radio*, § 2.9, full, educational].

**Implication.** Polarization is the most practical second receive branch at HF. A dual-polarized receiver turns a two-ray magneto-ionic channel into one or two more Rician branches and adds energy that a single linear antenna discards. Isolation on oblique east–west paths, where the characteristic waves are more elliptical, was not found quantified.

## Nonstationarity and outages

- **Stationarity intervals.** Watterson's validation used three samples of 10–13 minutes selected as nearly stationary [Watterson et al., ERL 112-ITS 80, 1969, sec]. Vogler and Hoffmeyer limit the Gaussian model to stationary, low-ray, non-dispersive channels. Averaging over at least 8 minutes has been recommended because moving irregularities make statistics nonstationary [Jull 1968, sec]. Equalized channels in a MITRE wideband test were time-invariant for about 10 s [Weber et al., arXiv 2609.05811, § IV-B1, full](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.05811). Little 2015–2026 empirical work on stationarity intervals was found.
- **Traveling ionospheric disturbances.** Medium-scale disturbances appear in the 14–33 minute band and enhanced 15–60 minute power around 03:30–12:00 UT on WWV paths ([Collins et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.5194/essd-15-1403-2023)); large-scale disturbances with periods of 30–180 minutes and one example of about 2.5 hours modulated amateur skip distances [Frissell et al., GRL 2022, abs](https://doi.org/10.1029/2022GL097879). Excursions are under 1 Hz at 2.5–15 MHz.
- **Shortwave fadeouts.** From more than 25,000 flares, mean durations of HF-impacting absorption were 13, 18, 27 and 39 minutes for C1, M1, X1 and X10 flares, with 90th percentiles of 30, 48, 77 and 123 minutes [Fiori et al., JASTP 252:106148, 2023, abs](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jastp.2023.106148). An X1.6 flare attenuated 7 MHz NVIS signals by more than 40 dB while background noise fell 12 dB ([Witvliet 2022](https://ris.utwente.nl/ws/portalfiles/portal/284844584/2022_Witvliet_NVIS_Nordic_HF.pdf), § 3.1.4).
- **Polar cap absorption and storms.** Polar cap absorption may last up to about ten days on transpolar paths [IPS/BoM, full, educational]. During the November 2012 storm the F2 layer fell below detectability overnight at several European mid-latitude ionosondes [Berényi et al., Front. Astron. Space Sci. 10:1092850, 2023, § 3.1.1, full](https://doi.org/10.3389/fspas.2023.1092850).
- **Day-to-day variability.** Signal lower deciles span 8–13 dB below 60° geomagnetic latitude and 11–17 dB above, largest near `f/MUF` of 1.0–1.2 [ITU-R P.842-3, Table 2, full](https://www.itu.int/rec/R-REC-P.842/en).

**Implication.** Model the channel as piecewise stationary over seconds to minutes, with Doppler centroid and power drifting over tens of minutes to hours, and with an availability layer of minute- to day-scale outages. The receiver must track and reacquire, and codes must treat outages as erasures across a deadline that spans them.

## Long delays

Round-the-world delays are about 133–140 ms; short-path/long-path differences are `(40,000 km − 2d)/c`, about 67 ms at 10,000 km and 33 ms at 15,000 km [derived]. A 28 MHz amateur study observed a long-path echo only about 3 dB weaker than the short path at about 140 ms, and another echo at about 270 ms [Egano, “A study of long path echoes,” *QEX*, January/February 2017, full, anecdotal; no stable link found]. A chirp-sounding network detected round-the-world delays only on a few occasions [ZL1BPU, full]. Russian sounding found round-the-world attenuation governed mainly by the low-latitude ionosphere [Kurkin et al. c. 2001, abs; Ponyatov et al., RPQE 57:417, 2014, abs]. No occurrence statistics were found. Long echoes matter most for symbols near 160 ms, where they fall into the next symbol; multi-second tones overlap mostly their own symbol.

## Equatorial spread-F

Besides the Doppler widths above, 75% of evenings on the Marshall Islands path had coherence bandwidth under 1.5 kHz and 40% had spatial coherence under 180 m across the path and 75 m along it; a companion report notes Doppler spreads above 20 Hz were not uncommon and most degradation came from bottomside spread-F ([Fitzgerald et al. 1999](https://doi.org/10.1029/1998RS900013), abs). Continuous Doppler sounding in Tucumán shows spread structures drifting eastward at about 70–200 m/s, mainly at night from about September to March [Chum et al., JGR 119:6954, 2014, abs](https://doi.org/10.1002/2014JA020184). For a tone receiver this is the regime that needs the widest spacing, a short coherent segment and the wide-spread hypothesis; otherwise these hours behave as an outage.

## Sporadic E

A review of mostly VHF sporadic-E measurements reports fades up to 60 dB, 40% of events shorter than 2 minutes, fading periods of about 5 s (2–20 s oblique), three or more components with different Doppler shifts and arrival angles, polarization-ellipse fading of about 10 s, and a May–August peak; the authors' 50 MHz data show magneto-ionic double refraction [Deacon, Witvliet, Steendam and Mitchell, IEEE TAP 69(7):4033, 2021, full](https://doi.org/10.1109/TAP.2020.3044654). At HF, sporadic E is often partly specular (Rician), which is favorable for the bound, but patchy, short-lived and able to blanket F-layer modes. Treat it as an opportunistic mode, not a reliable one.

## Noise and interference

- **Model coefficients.** ITU-R P.372-17 man-made noise medians `F_am = c − d·log₁₀ f` use `(76.8, 27.7)` city, `(72.5, 27.7)` residential, `(67.2, 27.7)` rural and `(53.6, 28.6)` quiet rural, with upper/lower deciles of about 11.0/6.7, 10.6/5.3 and 9.2/4.6 dB for the first three; the impulsive component is excluded [Weber et al., arXiv 2609.05811, § II-C, Table I, full](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.05811). The [link-budget review](link-budget-and-operation-review.md#received-power-and-the-noise-reference-plane) already records P.372-18.
- **Site measurements.** Measurements at more than 100 German locations put median man-made noise below P.372 at 20 MHz but 3–7 dB above it at 12 MHz and above it for rural 5 MHz; the impulse method detects only impulses more than 13 dB above the Gaussian level [ITU-R Report SM.2155, § 8.3, Table 4, full](https://www.itu.int/pub/R-REP-SM.2155).
- **Non-Gaussian statistics.** HF noise probability densities are generally non-Gaussian and were fitted by a bi-kappa law that varies with time of day, environment and ionospheric state [Giesbrecht, Clarke and Abbott, “An empirical study of the probability density function of HF noise,” *Fluct. Noise Lett.* 6(2):L117–L125, 2006, abs](https://doi.org/10.1142/S0219477506003203). Wideband HF noise models combine Gaussian noise, narrowband sinusoidal interferers and impulsive noise, with impulsiveness depending on bandwidth [Lemmon and Behm, NTIA TR-91-277, abs; Lemmon, Radio Science 2001, abs](https://doi.org/10.1029/2000RS002364).
- **Not found:** numeric `V_d` impulsiveness values at the modem's bandwidth, and quantitative 2015–2026 narrowband occupancy statistics for amateur bands.

**Implication.** Site and decile variation of about 10 dB is as large as most waveform choices, so link margins should use deciles. A Gaussian-noise bound is conservative for a fixed noise power, but an energy detector without clipping or blanking can lose that headroom and more ([theoretical weakness](fading-limit-and-mfsk-alternatives.md#where-reality-is-worse-than-the-bound)). Narrowband carriers are a separate hazard for narrow tone bins and argue for keyed hopping and erasure of contaminated bins.

## Regime map for the bound and the receiver

| Regime | What the evidence shows | Effect on the Rayleigh–Gaussian bound | Receiver requirement |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Quiet mid-latitude one hop | Few narrow modes, about 0.06 Hz wide, slow drift | Pessimistic | Long coherent segments, drift tracking, narrow-line branch |
| Sporadic E or single stable mode | Often Rician | Pessimistic | Same, plus fast reacquisition when the mode disappears |
| Moderate and disturbed mid-latitude, NVIS | 0.5–5 Hz, several modes, slant | Near correct when converted through `B_eff` | Spread-matched weighting, adaptive per message |
| Two comparable rays | Deterministic nulls, `m` < 1 | Ergodically favorable, worse in outage | Polarization or frequency diversity, interleaving over the beat period |
| Equatorial evening spread-F | 6 Hz at −10 dB, 15 Hz at −30 dB on half of evenings; >20 Hz not uncommon | About right to slightly optimistic | Widest spacing, short coherent segments, or treat as outage |
| High-latitude disturbed and auroral | 10–70 Hz composite widths ([existing review](hf-channels.md#polar-cap-trough-and-auroral-measurements)) | About right to optimistic | Same as above |
| Flares, polar cap absorption, storm nights | Minutes to days of 40 dB-class loss or closure | Outside the model | Erasure handling, deadline and frequency planning |
| Impulsive or interfered noise | Non-Gaussian, about 10 dB site spread | Conservative for fixed noise power | Clipping or blanking, robust bin statistics, hopping |
