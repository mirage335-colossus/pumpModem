# Information limits under Doppler fading


Research date: 24 November 2026. This note audits the often repeated claim that a peak-limited transmitter in a Rayleigh channel with a smooth Doppler spectrum faces a hard rate ceiling, and that long-symbol MFSK reaches 97–99.8% of that ceiling. The result is valuable for Matrix Modem because it identifies a regime where receiver computation cannot recover information erased by rapid fading. It is conditional on a sharply specified stochastic channel and signaling constraint. It is not a universal bound for every disturbed HF channel.

## Conclusion

The quoted table is reproducible for a particular infinite-bandwidth, frequency-flat, zero-mean complex Gaussian Rayleigh fading model with a Gaussian Doppler power spectrum, unit mean channel power, no channel state information at either end, and a peak constraint. Crucially, for the frequency-flat time-selective model, Sethuraman and Hajek's Proposition 3.3 gives the **exact continuous-time capacity per unit energy** under a fixed peak, and their general converse turns it into a capacity upper bound at any allowed average power. When average power equals peak power, that upper bound is exactly the rate expression used in the table. The quoted numbers are therefore a genuine conditional asymptotic capacity ceiling in that model, not merely an FSK lower bound. The reported numbers use the Gaussian spectrum's **full width parameter `2σ`** as shown. At received `C/N₀ = −10 dB-Hz`, the rate upper bound is about 0.0289, 0.00390, 0.000405, and 0.000135 bit/s for `2σ = 0.1, 1, 10, 30 Hz`. Converting those rates to `(C/N₀)/R` gives 5.39, 14.09, 23.92, and 28.68 dB per information bit. Those reproduce the prompt's rounded 5.4, 14, 24, and 29 dB values.

For broader time- and frequency-selective WSSUS channels, the status is more nuanced. Viterbi's result and Durisi et al.'s 2007 result give an achievable infinite-bandwidth FSK lower bound under a time-peak constraint. Durisi et al.'s 2010 paper derives both upper and lower bounds, and identifies flat-in-delay scattering functions for which they coincide at peak-to-average parameter `β=1` in their discrete time-frequency model. Thus the formula is also an exact capacity for that stated class and model. For general delay-dependent scattering, the FSK expression remains the lower bound and the upper bound can be larger. None of these results is a universal ceiling for every disturbed HF link. A finite 2.2 kHz modem, a nonstationary or non-Gaussian channel, a specular component, genuinely additional receiver side information, multiple antennas, a different peak/average-power budget, or a different spectrum can change the answer.

The 97–99.8% claim is a result from the repository's own Monte Carlo, not a published theorem or field measurement. It compares a symbol-wise mutual-information calculation for an idealized finite-band MFSK bank against the conditional infinite-bandwidth capacity ceiling. It is a useful computational result for the tested assumptions. It does not demonstrate a coded receiver, reliable message completion, a physical HF link, or performance under acquisition and oscillator uncertainty. A crucial caveat is that the calculator assumes ideal interleaving makes successive symbols independently faded. Real HF fading can be strongly correlated over the interleaver span, and deep fades can persist beyond a codeword.

The practical design implication is to use the expression as a **conditional stress benchmark and falsification target**, alongside other channel families. A waveform or receiver claim that beats it should first be checked for changed assumptions, resource constraints, or model error. A waveform should not be rejected merely for failing to match it under changed assumptions.

## Definition and units

Take a complex baseband model `Y(t) = H(t) X(t) + N(t)`, with `H` a zero-mean, proper complex, stationary Gaussian fading process normalized to unit average power, and `N` complex white Gaussian noise. The transmitter and receiver know the law of `H`, but not its realization. Let `P_r` be average received signal power and `N₀` the one-sided noise power spectral density used by the cited continuous-time convention. Then `P = P_r/N₀` has units hertz and is the linear `C/N₀`. Be explicit about whether reported `N₀` is per real dimension, one-sided RF, or complex-baseband PSD before comparing sources. A factor-of-two convention changes powers expressed in dB by 3.01 dB.

For a frequency-flat, time-varying Rayleigh channel, let `S_H(f)` be the normalized Doppler power spectral density with integral one. Sethuraman and Hajek's Proposition 3.3 gives the continuous-time capacity per unit energy `η(P_peak)` for a fixed input peak and no bandwidth constraint. Let `P_peak` and `P_avg` denote the peak and average reference signal powers divided by `N₀`, in hertz. The reference is before multiplication by the unit-power random fade. In this complex-baseband normalization,

\[
\eta(P_{\mathrm{peak}}) = 1 - \frac{1}{P_{\mathrm{peak}}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\ln\left(1+P_{\mathrm{peak}} S_H(f)\right)\,df \quad \text{nats per normalized energy unit}.
\]

Their general capacity-per-unit-energy converse gives `R ≤ P_avg η(P_peak)` nats/s at any allowed average power, with the corresponding time/energy cost definitions in that paper. In physical joules at the reference plane, the efficiency is `η(P_peak)/N₀`. At `P_avg=P_peak=P`, this becomes `R ≤ P - ∫ln(1+P S_H(f))df`. Dividing by `ln 2` gives the bit/s expression. Since the lower bound for frequency-flat fading is achievable in the infinite-bandwidth limit, this upper bound is tight there. A larger peak-to-average ratio changes the bound through `P_peak` and the factor `P_avg` and cannot be represented by substituting the average `C/N₀` into the equal-peak formula.

The 2007 WSSUS theorem is distinct. It gives a lower bound on the general underspread time- and frequency-selective channel's infinite-bandwidth capacity in terms of the power-Doppler profile, achieved by an FSK-like construction. Durisi et al.'s 2010 Theorem 8 gives an upper bound for that model. At `β=1`, the upper and lower bounds coincide for scattering functions flat in delay, yielding exact capacity within the paper's discrete time-frequency model. For general delay profiles, they need not coincide. None of these infinite-bandwidth results by itself establishes the capacity of a finite-bandwidth modem with acquisition, finite blocklength, synchronization error, or additional implementation constraints.

For a Gaussian Doppler PSD

\[
S_H(f)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}\exp\left(-\frac{f^2}{2\sigma^2}\right), \qquad \int S_H(f)\,df=1,
\]

the 2007 paper and ITU model convention use `2σ` for the specified Doppler spread. The code named `peak_limited_bound.py` integrates this normalized spectrum numerically. The table can be independently reproduced with 200,001-point Simpson quadrature over `±12σ`. The result is insensitive to increasing the integration range or grid at the shown precision.

`Eb/N₀` in the table means `(C/N₀)/R_b` for the corresponding ideal information rate `R_b`, not an uncoded symbol-energy threshold, not transmit-side energy, and not a practical coded-modem sensitivity. The −10 dB-Hz input means `C/N₀ = 0.1 Hz`. For example, the 0.1-Hz spread model gives `R_b = 0.028906 bit/s`, so `Eb/N₀ = 0.1/0.028906 = 3.459` or 5.39 dB. Fixed transmitter peak power and average power equal to that peak correspond to a constant-envelope waveform. If a duty-cycled transmitter is allowed, its peak-to-average ratio must be included explicitly.

| Gaussian Doppler `2σ` | Bound rate at −10 dB-Hz | `(C/N₀)/R` |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 0.1 Hz | 0.028906 bit/s | 5.39 dB |
| 1 Hz | 0.003902 bit/s | 14.09 dB |
| 10 Hz | 0.0004052 bit/s | 23.92 dB |
| 30 Hz | 0.0001355 bit/s | 28.68 dB |

The table records energy efficiency on an asymptotic rate axis. At fixed received `C/N₀`, a very low bound means a very long ideal code duration per bit. It is not an outage probability, a guaranteed service rate, or a prediction that a channel remains in this regime for a message.

## What the theory establishes

### Stationary stochastic fading and peak limits

Viterbi's 1967 paper, *Performance of an M-ary orthogonal communication system using stationary stochastic signals*, treats orthogonal signaling through stationary stochastic fading and derives an asymptotic FSK result. The accessible source trail in the 2007 paper identifies this work as the source of the infinite-bandwidth expression. This is **not** Viterbi's better-known convolutional-code/Viterbi-decoder paper. The distinction matters because that frequently repeated citation is incorrect.

Sethuraman and Hajek's 2005 paper gives capacity per unit energy for temporally correlated Rayleigh fading under a hard peak constraint. The fading correlation then appears in the expression. Under a receiver-CSI assumption, the capacity-per-energy is one nat per joule, independent of peak level. The no-CSI, peak-constrained result is different because the receiver must learn a random channel from the same signal. It is a capacity-per-unit-energy theorem with specified limits, not a universal finite-frame outage bound.

Durisi, Bölcskei, and Shamai (ISIT 2007) consider noncoherent underspread WSSUS Rayleigh fading with peak constraints. Their time-only peak-constraint theorem supplies an infinite-bandwidth capacity **lower bound** in terms of the power-Doppler profile. They state that it coincides with Viterbi's lower bound for the same setup. Their distinct time-and-frequency peak constraint yields upper and lower bounds and can produce overspreading, with a capacity-maximizing finite bandwidth. Therefore, saying “any waveform or computation cannot do better” is too broad unless the same peak convention, channel class, frequency-peaky allowance, infinite-bandwidth limit, and CSI assumptions are retained.

At low `P S_H(f)`, expand `ln(1+u) = u-u²/2+O(u³)`. Since `∫S_H=1`, the first-order `P` terms cancel, leaving

\[
R_{\mathrm{FSK},\infty} \approx \frac{P^2}{2}\int S_H(f)^2\,df \quad \text{nats/s}.
\]

This quadratic low-power behavior explains the apparently severe energy penalties. For the normalized Gaussian PSD, `∫S_H² df = 1/(2√π σ)`, so its effective Doppler bandwidth is `B_eff = 1/∫S_H² = 2√πσ = √π(2σ)`. This is not simply the support width or a universal coherence bandwidth. It is a spectrum-shape-dependent effective bandwidth. At low signal levels, a 10 dB reduction in `C/N₀` produces roughly a 20 dB reduction in rate, while the required `Eb/N₀` at a fixed Doppler spectrum rises by roughly 10 dB.

### Peakiness and coherent channel knowledge change the conclusion

With only an average-power constraint and arbitrarily large peaks allowed, rare, energetic pulses can recover the AWGN capacity-per-unit-energy slope in several noncoherent fading models. This flash-signaling limit does not apply to a transmitter held to constant envelope, fixed peak power, a spectral mask, or strict duty-cycle and bandwidth limits. A practical coded FSK signal with known key schedule is generally not free to take the mathematical limit of infinitely rare, infinitely energetic events.

At the other extreme, if the receiver knows the channel perfectly, coherent low-rate energy efficiency can approach the AWGN minimum `Eb/N₀ = ln(2)`, or −1.59 dB under the usual complex-noise convention. This does not remove quasi-static fades or path outages. Tracking, pilot-aided estimation and joint decoding computed from the same received samples are already allowed by the no-side-information converse, with pilot airtime and energy charged to the same budget. Better estimation may help approach that bound where practical algorithms leave a gap. Externally supplied channel state, an additional receive observation or a changed propagation law defines a different channel or resource model. A time or frequency reference that removes oscillator uncertainty alone does not reveal random propagation coefficients.

The classic AWGN −1.59 dB number itself is an asymptotic low-spectral-efficiency limit. In a finite codeword, the target error probability, blocklength, synchronization, and operational energy constraint matter. Polyanskiy, Poor, and Verdú's finite-blocklength bounds give the right framework for an AWGN reference, but those formulas do not directly model noncoherent, time-correlated HF fading.

### Ergodic rates do not protect against a long fade

The spectral-density expression assumes a stationary ergodic process and asymptotically long coding over its statistics. It describes long-run mutual information under that model. A quasi-static Rayleigh channel can remain in a deep fade for the entire frame. The decoder then experiences an outage, and extending the code while the channel stays faded does not create independent fades. Longer coding helps random noise only when the channel supplies useful additional observations. Time/frequency interleaving helps when it spans decorrelated channel states and the receiver combines them correctly. No fixed-rate code can guarantee success through an arbitrarily deep or arbitrarily persistent propagation outage.

HF ionospheric links can depart from the assumed law through multiple modes, changing path weights, specular components, nonstationarity, impulsive atmospherics, strong narrowband interference, oscillator drift, and sudden absorption or blackout. ITU-R F.1487 describes the stationary Gaussian-scatter Watterson model and its intended 3 kHz use, with possible application up to 12 kHz. The recommendation itself cautions that wider-band use lacks adequate channel information and validation. Its provision for a non-fading specular tap also shows that specular and diffuse components can coexist. A smooth Gaussian Doppler spectrum is a convenient and important test model, not an exhaustive worst case. An inference from the papers is that isolated nonstationarity or a strong specular component could improve reception relative to zero-mean Rayleigh fading. That cannot be presumed: abrupt mode changes and path cancellation may instead make tracking and decoding worse. Such cases need separate channel models or measured traces.

## Audit of the repository's 97–99.8% calculation

The supporting calculator is `_appraisal/algorithms-matrixModem/calc/mfsk_capacity.cpp`; its assumptions and commands are recorded in that directory's README. The stored capacity sweep and summary are under `_appraisal/algorithms-matrixModem/calc/results/`. The percentage compares its best symbol-duration mutual-information rate with the Gaussian-spectrum integral above at selected low `C/N₀` points. It is not an independent academic replication.

The computation models noncoherent MFSK with one tone per symbol, a 2.2 kHz band, tone spacing `1/T + 4σ`, a Gaussian-Doppler Rayleigh fading law, and a Gaussian-signal estimator-correlator using Karhunen–Loève weights. It numerically estimates symbol-wise mutual information and divides by the symbol duration, searching a finite grid of durations. It assumes perfect timing and frequency knowledge, no inter-tone leakage, and an ideal outer code achieving that symbol-wise mutual information. It assumes ideal interleaving yields independent fading across symbols. The code and README state approximately ±0.05–0.1 dB Monte Carlo uncertainty for the reported `Eb/N₀` values, a 0.05-decade duration grid, and a Doppler sweep cap of 60 seconds.

At −10 dB-Hz the stored best-duration MI ratios for 0.1, 1, 10 and 30 Hz are 0.974, 0.998, 0.994 and 0.987. The separate fixed-duration summary helps expose what that result does and does not transfer to. At the same `C/N₀`, fixing the modeled symbol at 160 ms lost 13.9, 5.5, 0.5 and 0.2 dB of effective `Eb/N₀` relative to its optimized duration in each row. Those optima in the scanned grid were 56.5, 50.4, 3.18 and 1.26 seconds. Crucially, the 160 ms comparison kept the model's extra spacing `4σ`, giving 6.45, 8.25, 26.25 and 66.25 Hz. Its small penalties in the faster cases do not predict a 6.25 Hz-spaced modem. The large slow-channel differences give a concrete reason to keep long integration and coding branches available. [Stored rate ratios](../../../_appraisal/algorithms-matrixModem/calc/results/peak_limited_bound.md), [duration comparison](../../../_appraisal/algorithms-matrixModem/calc/results/mfsk_summary.md).

Those assumptions make the result useful in two ways. First, they show that a finite but wide tone bank can approach the infinite-bandwidth benchmark in the tested low-`C/N₀` parameter points. Second, they suggest the benchmark is attainable by a tractable family of tone signaling in those parameters, rather than merely a pessimistic upper limit. They do **not** imply that every codeword decoder or physical implementation achieves those rates, nor that it completes real messages at that service rate.

Important interpretation limits:

- `97–99.8%` is a **rate ratio**, not a measured success probability and not a universal lower bound on receiver sensitivity. The result varies by Doppler value, received `C/N₀`, numerical optimizer, and trial uncertainty. Consult the stored result table rather than repeating the percentage without its parameter row.
- The computation's infinite-bandwidth expression is approached from a finite 2.2 kHz tone bank. The finite-band capacity need not equal its infinite-band counterpart. The ratio is evidence about this particular input family and numerical model, not a theorem that all finite bands meet the limit.
- Perfect synchronization and known noise/fading statistics omit a costly part of broad acquisition. CFO, drift, clock offset, delay spread, unknown epoch, channel mismatch, and multi-hypothesis false alarms can reduce end-to-end performance.
- Independent fading per symbol is a strong assumption. If symbols are contiguous or the interleaver does not span a coherence time, the rate calculation can overstate usable diversity. A correlation-aware sequence decoder may recover some loss, but that receiver was not evaluated by this program.
- Ideal outer coding at a mutual-information rate is asymptotic. Short frames, finite error probability, headers, key/schedule overhead, code termination, pilots, interleaving, guard intervals, retries, and quiet-slot completion reduce net delivered information rate.
- No inference about GPU utilization, Tensor Core speed, native receiver throughput, or end-to-end DSP workspace capacity follows from this scalar Monte Carlo. Those require a separate implementation and measurement.

The stored values and assumptions are useful for prioritizing a native experiment. Before treating them as a Matrix Modem performance claim, implement at least one representative modulator/detector in the canonical native stack and validate predicted curves against matched simulation inputs. Carry channel memory between symbols, include acquisition and null decisions, and report a codeword-level error/erasure curve plus complete processing cost. That future experiment should preserve the zero-mean Gaussian model as a reference stratum while adding held-out channel cases.

## Boundaries for waveform decisions

The theory gives no universal winning waveform under simultaneously fixed latency, power, peak, occupied bandwidth, receiver complexity, and LPI constraints. For the narrow stationary Rayleigh case, long orthogonal FSK is an unusually strong analytical candidate because frequency peaky signaling uses the available peak constraint in a favorable way. When time peakiness is allowed, on-off/flash FSK may gain energy efficiency at the cost of instantaneous detectability and peak power. With reliable CSI or sufficiently stable tracking, coherent or differentially assisted schemes can do better in practical bandwidth. In fast fading, known or estimated channel subspaces, interleaving, sequence decoding, and diversity may help, but none can recover absent energy or eliminate an outage.

For the prompt's 6.25 Hz spacing, ideal rectangular-window orthogonality gives `T = 1/6.25 = 0.16 s`. That geometry does not optimize the low-`C/N₀` cases in the repository's sweep. The old MFSK calculation finds that much longer symbol periods are often better at low received power, while a wider tone spacing is needed when Doppler broadening is large. A 6.25 Hz choice should therefore be evaluated as one operational point, not as a consequence of the information limit. Equal-symbol-duration, equal elapsed time, equal energy, and equal peak power comparisons answer different questions and should be reported separately.

An invertible relabeling known to the receiving endpoint preserves mutual information for a fixed channel and waveform ensemble. Changing the actual hop schedule can change the sampled channel correlation and usable diversity. Label/key knowledge and hopping can also change an observer's prior uncertainty and detection problem. That is a different metric from receiver sensitivity. Coding and repetition should be compared on equal energy and latency budgets, with interleaver span and fading correlation stated. Repetition can simplify acquisition or channel estimation, but repeating the same symbols consumes airtime and energy unless it replaces other overhead or combines independent diversity. The rate bound is not an argument that repetitions are free.

## Evidence strength and remaining uncertainty

The mathematical expression and its conditional assumptions have strong primary-source grounding in the papers below. The numerical reproduction of the −10 dB-Hz table is straightforward and robust to numerical integration precision. The claimed 97–99.8% closeness has moderate evidence as a repository-specific model calculation, with its ideal synchronization and interleaver assumptions materially limiting its scope. Applicability to actual ionospheric links remains uncertain until a channel regime, RF reference plane, stationarity interval, path-delay/Doppler spectrum, and receiver acquisition model are selected and compared with measurements.

The 2026 finite-blocklength publication by Qi and Koch, titled “On Noncoherent Multiple-Antenna Rayleigh Block-Fading Channels at Finite Blocklength,” concerns noncoherent multiple-antenna Rayleigh block fading and a high-SNR asymptotic regime. It is relevant evidence that the number of independent coherence intervals and finite frame length matter, but it cannot be used as a low-`C/N₀` single-antenna HF prediction. The 2024 noncoherent MFSK Doppler-uncertainty paper studies Doppler-frequency uncertainty and soft decoding for space communications. It motivates including uncertainty and coded performance, but its channel and space-link assumptions differ from HF Gaussian Doppler spread. Neither publication revises the conditional peak-constrained formula above.
