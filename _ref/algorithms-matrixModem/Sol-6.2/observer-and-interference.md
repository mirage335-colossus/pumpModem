# Observer detection and perceived interference

## Scope and definitions

Low probability of intercept (LPI) is a statement about a specified observer, its observation point, receiver, prior knowledge, noise and interference population, observation time, and decision rule. It is not an intrinsic property conferred by encryption, cryptographic phase or amplitude choices, or frequency hopping. Low probability of detection (LPD) literature formalizes the problem as testing (H_0), transmission absent, against (H_1), transmission present, with a chosen constraint on the observer's false-alarm and miss probabilities. Perceived interference is a separate outcome. It depends on whether a listener or radio service experiences a material change in its receiver output or decisions. A waveform can be recognizable to a specialized detector but inaudible to a service, or be annoying interference without being attributable to Matrix Modem.

This distinction fits the project's priority order. Evaluate intended-link reception and any interference effect at the actual receive location, then characterize how long plausible observers need to recognize presence under stated conditions. The user's specific interest in weak received signal means an observer at the receive site can be useful as a conservative shared-path comparison. It does not represent every possible observer. A nearby observer, a different antenna or a clean elevated site may have a much higher signal-to-noise ratio. An observer beyond the receiver can be affected by a different propagation path and noise population.

## What the theory does and does not say

For an idealized discrete-time AWGN channel with fixed nonzero noise powers, Bash, Goeckel and Towsley show the square-root law: keeping an optimal warden's sum of false-alarm and missed-detection probabilities close to one limits reliable covert information to order √n over n channel uses. The constant depends on the channel and the chosen covertness criterion. Encryption alone does not enforce this output-distribution condition. Their model presumes an observation channel and specified noise statistics. It is not a prediction that a particular modem transmission is detectable after a given number of seconds over HF.

Later literature qualifies the simple slogan. Noise or channel uncertainty can allow positive asymptotic covert rates in some discrete-time models, but such conclusions depend on what uncertainty the warden truly cannot learn and the assumed observation model. He, Yan, Zhou and Lau's 2017 treatment points out that worst-case and average covertness metrics differ. A 2024 multi-antenna analysis shows why a positive-rate result that assumes only a radiometer can be fragile: a correlation detector using two or more antennas restores square-root scaling in its model without requiring knowledge of the transmitter's codebook or channel coefficients. Lee et al. show that transmitter channel-state information can change the covert-capacity conclusion for some state-dependent AWGN models. None of these models is a direct HF link budget.

Continuous-time results are particularly relevant to very long messages. Bash et al.'s continuous-time AWGN work relates the limit to the time-bandwidth product, and later work on environmental uncertainty finds that transferring discrete-time noise-uncertainty intuition into continuous-time can fail. Thus do not infer that slow symbol timing, many hop frequencies, a large nominal key, or a changing HF noise floor guarantees positive-rate stealth. The emitted waveform, total transmitted energy, warden's observation bandwidth and interval, and the stochastic model jointly determine distinguishability.

The Tandra-Sahai SNR-wall work addresses a different but related question: robust signal detection when the receiver has uncertainty in noise power and fading/channel statistics. For energy detection, uncertainty creates a nonzero SNR region where increasing observation time does not ensure reliable detection. Their extensions show that matched filters and cyclostationary detectors also have robustness limits under particular finite-coherence and calibration uncertainties. This is not a universal threshold for Matrix Modem. Tandra and Sahai also show a counterexample to treating every SNR wall as unavoidable: waveforms with deliberately designed large-scale features can remain robustly detectable under their bounded-delay uncertainty models. Thus a receiver can trade waveform degrees of freedom for robustness. With an accurately known waveform and well-calibrated channel, a keyed matched filter has information unavailable to a blind radiometer. With large unknown timing, frequency offset, fading and model mismatch, that advantage erodes. Conversely, cyclostationary or repeated timing structure may allow a capable observer to detect a signal that is hidden from a simple energy detector. Any reported “observation time” must identify which observer is meant.

### An ideal difference in observation-time scaling

Let `g=C/N₀` in Hz, `B` be the observation bandwidth, and `d` the mean signal-induced score shift in noise-only standard deviations. A calibrated square-law radiometer with `B T` independent complex AWGN dimensions and low total-band SNR `g/B` has `d≈(g/B)√(BT)`, so `T≈B d²/g²`. A known deterministic waveform, phase, timing and channel give a real matched projection with `d=√(2gT)`, so `T=d²/(2g)`. For the illustrative 2.2 kHz, −13 dB-Hz budget, `d=3` gives roughly 91 days in the radiometer model and 90 s in the perfectly known waveform model. This compares one ideal statistic from each class, with fixed calibrated noise and continuous average received power. A tuned channelized observer, periodic feature, Helper, or side information can shorten the first time substantially. Unknown phase, messages, timing, changing paths and a wider search can lengthen the second. Use experimentally calibrated ROC curves and temporal uncertainty for the actual comparison. [Link-budget assumptions and detector bounds](information-limits.md).

## Implications for candidate waveform choices

- **Constant-envelope or sparse keyed tones.** A signal below the surrounding noise in a narrow tone bin can remain difficult for a radiometer if the observer has no stable noise baseline. A known or estimated repeated tone schedule, symbol clock, hop dwell, framing cadence, or helper reference can create exploitable structure. Longer integration improves tests only while the desired statistical contrast remains coherent or the noncoherent statistic combines independent evidence correctly.
- **Frequency hopping.** Hopping can reduce energy in any one narrow channel, but it redistributes the emitted energy across the hop set and can leave a clear wideband energy rise, occupancy pattern, dwell-time structure, or periodicity. A wideband observer need not decode the hop sequence. Secret hop schedules complicate a sequence-matched test but do not hide an increase in total in-band energy. Whether hopping reduces a particular service's interference is a separate per-channel occupancy and receiver-filter question.
- **Keyed schedule and a public zero key.** A published amateur key is public protocol information. For that mode, evaluate the observer's informed case as knowing the effective schedule derivation, including its clock/nonce assumptions where applicable. For a secret-key mode, evaluate both a blind observer and an informed observer that knows the waveform family and performs timing/frequency/channel searches without the key. Do not attribute concealment to an unknown key where the key is public.
- **Helper Carrier.** If a helper/reference tone is transmitted, it is an emission and an additional possible detection feature. Even if Matrix reception treats its guidance independently, an observer can combine it with the data-band observations. Measure helper-on and helper-off cases separately and include their total radiated energy. A helper that improves intended timing or Doppler acquisition may also make an observer's acquisition easier.
- **Masking and natural HF noise.** Elevated or impulsive natural/man-made noise can reduce a simple detector's reliability in some samples, but a quiet interval, another bandwidth, a longer record, or a second antenna may restore detectability. Noise from ITU-R P.372 is a planning reference and does not prescribe a site's time-varying distribution. The existing HF noise research describes large geographic, time, site, and bandwidth variation, and warns against treating a marginal noise level as a complete temporal model. An observer's false-alarm rate must therefore be calibrated on held-out local noise episodes, including quiet intervals and representative interference, rather than inferred from one AWGN draw.
- **LPI and LPI claims.** A keyed waveform's cryptographic unpredictability can make sequence reconstruction difficult. That is not equivalent to statistically indistinguishable emission. State a probability of detection only relative to an observer class, observation interval, per-hour false-alarm budget, noise/channel population and detector set. Call simulations a detector comparison under their stated model, not an absolute LPI guarantee.

These implications are inferences from the cited signal-detection and covert-channel models. The current Matrix Modem waveform has not been specified or measured, so there is no evidentiary basis yet to rank its LPI performance against Fast, single-carrier Robust, triple Robust or other modem families.

## A bounded, fair observation-time study

The desired comparison is the time needed to meet a predeclared presence-detection operating point at the intended receiver and at explicitly chosen observer locations, after each location's path, channel and noise. It should also report the signal's effect on incumbent or representative service receivers when perceived interference is in scope. Keep these as separate outcomes.

### 1. Fix the observer and operating point

Record each observer's location, antenna, polarization, analog/RF filter, gain, ADC, clock, usable bandwidth and dynamic range. Define the allowed detector knowledge, including whether protocol, center frequency, hop range, helper, symbol timing, code family or key is known. Evaluate at least a blind-energy detector and a stronger detector set containing energy over several bandwidths, spectral occupancy, cyclic/timing features where applicable, and keyed matched-filter or generalized-likelihood searches for an informed observer. Limit each search family and report its compute budget. An unlimited optimal detector is a theoretical reference, not a software benchmark.

Predeclare the target false alarms per hour and required detection probability. For example, one could measure the observation duration required to reach a lower confidence bound on (P_D\geq0.90) at a family-wise (P_{FA}\leq0.01) per hour; those numbers are a study choice, not a standard or user-specified acceptance threshold. Include the full false-alarm burden from all scanned frequencies, timing offsets, Doppler bins, detectors, and repeated looks. If M independent decisions are made in one hour, a per-look false-alarm rate p gives an hourly false-alarm probability (1-(1-p)^M). Sliding windows and HF samples are generally correlated, so calibrate hourly false alarms on full held-out noise episodes or block-resampled episodes instead of trusting the independence formula.

### 2. Use matched noise and channel evidence

For each target site/band/season or measured HF capture, preserve receiver calibration and the full unclipped record. Construct paired (H_0) and (H_1) observations from the same captured noise where possible, applying an independently validated received-signal channel realization to the (H_1) arm. This isolates the waveform's contribution while preserving each noise sample. A replayed or software-added signal only tests that signal path and receiver model. Separately capture actual RF/baseband loopback or over-the-air trials to check analog emissions, filtering, and simulator fidelity.

Use multiple independently selected episodes as the statistical units. Split episodes, by time/site/event, into detector-development, threshold-calibration, and held-out evaluation sets. Keep all windows from one lightning storm, interference event, or long noise episode in one partition. Otherwise overlapping windows leak effectively identical noise across training and test sets and make false-alarm confidence look unrealistically strong. Include both typical and unusually quiet intervals, with the exact selection rule retained. For HF, stratify at minimum by frequency, local time, season/solar or geomagnetic context, path type and disturbance category when source data supports those labels. Do not call a synthetic category a measured percentile.

### 3. Plot full performance, not just a threshold crossing

For every observer/detector and signal condition, report the ROC curve and (P_D(T)) versus observation duration T at fixed false-alarm budgets. Include a confidence interval for (P_D) and (P_{FA}), number of independent episodes, and cases where the threshold was never met during the available record. Report the earliest T at which the confidence requirement is met, plus the received signal power and energy, noise/interference distribution, channel state, code/key knowledge, detector family and total test multiplicity. Also report detection delay under an actual sequential test if real-time observation time matters. A threshold chosen and evaluated on the same data is exploratory only.

For equal-prior binary decisions, the optimal Bayes error is (P_e=(1-\mathrm{TV}(P_0,P_1))/2), where TV is total variation distance between the complete (H_0) and (H_1) observation distributions. This is useful as a definition, but estimating TV in high-dimensional real captures is difficult. Empirical detector ROC curves give a lower bound on how distinguishable the data are to the tested detector class, not a proof that no better detector exists. KL divergence or a Gaussian energy approximation can guide analysis when assumptions hold. It should not replace measured false alarms under nonstationary noise.

### 4. Compare reception and interference with the same signal budget

At the intended receiver, determine keyed data-recovery and presence-detection performance separately. At observer locations, run detectors with the knowledge each observer actually has. Normalize comparisons by the same transmitted energy, emission spectrum, channel/path loss, receiver bandwidth, observation interval and environmental record. If a waveform uses helper power, pilot energy or retransmissions, include them in the total. Report both total in-band energy and power spectral density/occupied-channel energy. A per-bin improvement from hopping is not a total-energy reduction.

For perceived interference, choose concrete incumbent receivers or service detectors and measure changes such as occupied-time probability, in-band noise rise, packet/bit-error rate, desensitization, audio audibility, or false squelch. Set a baseline condition and a detection threshold in advance. These are service-specific outcomes. “No energy rise” in a wideband radiometer does not imply no interference to a narrowband receiver, and detectability by a software observer does not imply a harmful service effect.

### 5. Keep uncertainty and selection honest

Use binomial confidence limits only for independent episodes/trials, not each highly overlapping window. Cluster-bootstrap by episode/site/day where appropriate. Report threshold-selection uncertainty and detector multiplicity. Preserve failed detections, detector-specific observations and condition-specific findings. State exact source provenance and whether each channel/noise episode is measured, replayed, statistically synthesized, or idealized. The broad user's “observer/receiver time” goal is experimentally tractable for a chosen population; no single scalar is valid across every HF observer, noise state and season.
