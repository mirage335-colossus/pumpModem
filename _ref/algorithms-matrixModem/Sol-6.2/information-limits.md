# Information and detection limits for Matrix Modem

Research date: 24 November 2026. This note supplies checkable information-theoretic reference points for a very weak HF signal. It does not predict a field link, prove a receiver implementation, or select a final waveform. Numerical examples are derived from the explicit assumptions below and should be read alongside the repository's HF propagation and noise chapters.

## Findings that constrain the design

1. **A carrier below the noise integrated over the full receive band can still carry information.** In white Gaussian noise, coherent integration or a matched filter trades observation time and energy for detection probability. The relevant ideal first-order quantity is received C/N_0, with dimensions Hz. It is not enough to state transmit power and path loss: receiver-site noise spectral density, antennas, mismatch, interference, fades, and the receiver's actual integration must also be specified.
2. **The ideal AWGN energy limit is an optimistic ceiling.** At low spectral efficiency, the Shannon limit is $E_b/N_0=-1.59$ dB, so an AWGN channel with received $C/N_0=x$ dB-Hz has low-rate capacity approximately $10^{x/10}\log_2 e$ bit/s. Unknown fading, finite duration, acquisition, coding overhead, tone-bank false alarms, quantization, and outages consume margin. For an unquantized noncoherent memoryless Rayleigh channel, average-power-only signaling with no peak constraint can recover this energy slope through arbitrarily peaky inputs; fixed peak power or peak-to-average constraints can change it sharply. If a fading process is slowly varying, underspread, or tracked, results can also approach the AWGN benchmark under the relevant models. The phrase “noncoherent” alone does not determine the penalty.
3. **An unrestricted average-power model gives unrealistic flash-signaling freedom.** Low-rate capacity-per-unit-energy results often use arbitrarily rare, arbitrarily energetic pulses. A real transmitter has peak power, occupied bandwidth, spectral-mask, keying, amplifier-linearity, observation-time, and LPI/LPD constraints. Pulse-position or sparse orthogonal signals are relevant, but their peakiness, duration, acquisition burden, and detection visibility must be costed explicitly.
4. **A long code can overcome random noise, not an arbitrarily persistent fade or propagation outage.** In a quasi-static block fade, increasing code duration inside the same fade does not create independent fade diversity. A finite-reliability target needs a channel-state distribution and a definition of unacceptable outage, or an explicit erasure outcome. HF blackout episodes can last much longer than any plausible modem frame.
5. **INT4 is not a communication theorem.** Information-theoretic bounds assume a real-valued channel statistic. Input quantization before the search, output quantization after each correlation, four-bit accumulator saturation, and a four-bit matrix operand with wider exact accumulation are different channels. A dense matrix multiply can implement many candidate correlations, but the false-alarm threshold, nuisance marginalization, and error-correcting decoder still determine communication performance.

## Reference-plane assumptions and reproducible link arithmetic

The illustration treats 2 mW, 10 mW, or 100 mW as conducted transmit power into a matched antenna, assumes 0 dBi transmit/receive gains and exactly 150 dB basic path loss, then takes $P_r=P_t10^{-15}$. This yields -147.0, -140.0, and -130.0 dBm. Real EIRP, antenna efficiency, polarization, feed loss, orientation, ground coupling, ionospheric focusing, and receiver reference plane can change these values substantially. The 150 dB input is the user's challenging case, not an HF propagation prediction. Compare the scenario-specific skywave loss and uncertainty discussion in [the repository's skywave link-budget chapter](../../highFidelity-triple_Robust_Modem/Fable-5.2/30-skywave-signal-level-and-outages.md).

Use three **illustrative** receiver-input noise PSDs: -174 dBm/Hz (290 K thermal reference, before external antenna noise and receiver noise figure), -164 dBm/Hz (10 dB above that reference), and -154 dBm/Hz (20 dB above). They are not claimed to be universal HF quiet, median, or disturbed values. The repository's [noise review](../../highFidelity-triple_Robust_Modem/Fable-5.2/50-noise-and-interference-mf-hf.md) and [measurement requirements](../../highFidelity-triple_Robust_Modem/Astra-6/50-noise-interference-and-frontends.md) emphasize that actual HF noise is directional, geographic, time-varying, often impulsive, and includes receiver/front-end effects. Replace these example PSDs with calibrated measurements at the relevant antenna plane.

For a flat PSD, C/N_0=P_r/N_0. Ideal AWGN capacity over bandwidth (B) is

\[
C=B\log_2\left(1+\frac{P_r}{N_0B}\right).
\]

The table computes that expression for $B=2{,}200$ Hz. The low-rate approximation $(C/N_0)\log_2 e$ becomes accurate when the signal power is small relative to the integrated noise.

These are **capacity upper bounds**, not demonstrated data rates. They optimistically treat the entire average received signal power as usefully coded, with known noise law, no acquisition, no preamble, no fading, no spectral overhead, and arbitrarily long coding. At 2 mW and -154 dBm/Hz, received average power is about 1 dB below the noise power in 6.25 Hz, and about 26 dB below noise power in 2.2 kHz. This is not a contradiction: $C/N_0=7$ dB-Hz remains the quantity governing an ideal known-signal detector's energy accumulation. It does mean that a single short symbol has little energy: for $T=0.16$ s, $E_s/N_0=(C/N_0)T=0.80$, or approximately -1.0 dB under this scenario. Detection over one such symbol is not equivalent to reliable coded communication.

At 6.25 information bit/s, the bit lasts 160 ms only for an uncoded one-bit-per-symbol scheme. A robust code, pilot, interleaving, synchronization and quiet-slot completion lower net information rate. Conversely, a 6.25-Hz tone spacing does not itself mean a 6.25 bit/s information rate. If one chose 6.25 symbols/s and $M=352$ ideal orthogonal tones in 2.2 kHz, the raw alphabet ceiling would be $6.25\log_2 352\approx52.9$ uncoded bit/s, before guard bands, coding, guards, synchronization, nonideal orthogonality or channel uncertainty. This is an alphabet count, not an achievable low-SNR rate. To have complex tone orthogonality over a rectangular 160-ms interval requires tone spacing an integer multiple of $1/T=6.25$ Hz in the ideal channel. Delay spread, frequency error, Doppler spread, windowing and the transceiver passband alter the cross-correlations.

### How to reproduce the table

Convert dBm to watts with $P[W]=10^{(P_{dBm}-30)/10}$, and dBm/Hz similarly for $N_0$. For each transmit power set $P_r=P_t10^{-150/10}$. Compute $C/N_0=P_r/N_0$, $C=2200\log_2(1+(C/N_0)/2200)$, and $E_b/N_0=(C/N_0)/R_b$ for a selected net rate $R_b$. The entries above were recomputed with Python's standard `math` functions; the assumptions are explicit so other link budgets can substitute their measured values.

### High-loss duration and the 160 ms reference

At the same illustrative −164 dBm/Hz, 0 dBi antennas and 2.2 kHz bandwidth, increasing loss with the same transmitter power gives the following controlled link arithmetic. The duration columns divide payload bits by constant-gain AWGN asymptotic capacity, before acquisition, coding loss, fade, quiet completion or identification. The power is all available to DATA.

| Basic loss | Conducted power | `C/N₀` | Ideal `C` (bit/s) | 128 bits | 1 KiB |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 150 dB | 2 mW | +17.01 dB-Hz | 71.66 | 1.79 s | 1.91 min |
| 150 dB | 100 mW | +34.00 dB-Hz | 2417 | 0.053 s | 3.39 s |
| 165 dB | 2 mW | +2.01 dB-Hz | 2.291 | 55.9 s | 59.6 min |
| 165 dB | 100 mW | +19.00 dB-Hz | 112.58 | 1.14 s | 72.8 s |

| 180 dB | 100 mW | +4.00 dB-Hz | 3.622 | 35.3 s | 37.7 min |

For a uniform `k`-bit message with error probability at most `ε`, Fano's inequality gives `I ≥ (1−ε)k−h₂(ε)` bits. For a known constant-gain Gaussian observation with mean received signal energy `E`, comparing the conditional output laws with the noise-only law gives `I ≤ (E/N₀) log₂e`. Hence `T ≥ [(1−ε)k−h₂(ε)] ln 2 / (C/N₀)` when the numerator is positive and received mean power stays constant over `T`. This energy-only finite-error converse gives about 29.4 min for 128 bits and `ε=0.001` at 2 mW/180 dB under these assumptions. It applies even if synchronization is known. An HF-specific converse or reliability forecast requires a model for the actual channel and observation deadline.

For a narrower comparison, synchronized orthogonal binary FSK with unknown constant phase in AWGN has ideal uncoded error probability `0.5 exp(−E_s/(2N₀))`, obtained by comparing the occupied-bin energy with an independent exponential empty-bin energy. For a 160 ms label this is 0.90% at +17.01 dB-Hz, 33.5% at +7.01 dB-Hz, and 49.8% at −12.99 dB-Hz. In a flat circular-Gaussian fade with the same mean symbol energy, averaging over the exponential gain gives `1/(2+E_s/N₀)`, about 10.0%, 35.7%, and 49.8% respectively. These separate effects explain why fixed-rate uncoded tones can be nearly useless at the weakest point, even though an ideal low-rate channel carries information. [Orthogonal FSK under a nonselective Rician model](https://stark.engin.umich.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/384/2018/04/1985D.pdf).

## Shannon bound, fading and observation duration

Verdú's wideband result gives a general capacity-per-unit-cost treatment and identifies on-off signaling with vanishing duty cycle as optimal in broad average-cost settings. For complex AWGN with average power and two-sided complex noise convention $N_0$, the limiting minimum energy per information bit is $\ln 2$, or -1.59 dB. At fixed $C/N_0$, the associated low spectral-efficiency rate bound is $R\lesssim(C/N_0)\log_2 e$. This is a limiting slope, not a finite-frame promise. [Verdú, “Spectral Efficiency in the Wideband Regime,” IEEE TIT (2002)](https://doi.org/10.1109/TIT.2002.1003824).

The AWGN finite-blocklength normal approximation estimates a coding rate for a known memoryless channel. For $n=BT$ complex channel uses at SNR per complex use $\rho=P_r/(N_0B)$, capacity per use is $c=\log_2(1+\rho)$, dispersion is $v=\frac{\rho(\rho+2)}{(1+\rho)^2}(\log_2e)^2$, and the approximation is $nc-\sqrt{nv}\,Q^{-1}(\epsilon)$ information bits at block error probability $\epsilon$, plus a smaller-order term. The approximation is distinct from the underlying nonasymptotic bounds. It does not include HF fading or unknown synchronization. Using $B=2{,}200$ Hz, $\epsilon=10^{-3}$, and the example $C/N_0=7$ dB-Hz gives roughly 28 bits in 10 seconds and 25,200 bits in one hour under this ideal AWGN approximation. For 17 dB-Hz it gives roughly 576 bits and 254,700 bits. These values illustrate how long duration helps against random AWGN when the channel remains usable; they do not imply field capacity or outage protection. Polyanskiy, Poor and Verdú provide nonasymptotic achievability/converse bounds and the normal approximation framework. [“Channel Coding Rate in the Finite Blocklength Regime,” IEEE TIT 56(5), 2010](https://doi.org/10.1109/TIT.2010.2043769).

That table treats samples as independent across code dimensions and does not model fade correlation. For a flat quasi-static Rayleigh example, let $\gamma$ be the received average SNR per complex use and instantaneous gain $G\sim\operatorname{Exp}(1)$. Outage at target spectral efficiency $r=R/B$ is

\[
P_{out}=\Pr[\log_2(1+\gamma G)<r]=1-\exp[-(2^r-1)/\gamma].
\]

At 2 mW, 150 dB path loss, $N_0=-154$ dBm/Hz and 2.2 kHz, mean $\gamma$ is -26.4 dB. This toy Rayleigh law gives approximately 13% outage at a 1 bit/s target, and 58% at 6.25 bit/s. At $N_0=-164$ dBm/Hz, the same setup has mean $\gamma=-16.4$ dB and the corresponding examples are about 1.4% and 8.3%. They are calculations under a quasi-static block-Rayleigh gain drawn once per frame, not HF predictions or actual Matrix Modem success rates. Increasing $T$ without channel change does not reduce this quasi-static outage. Frequency/time diversity can reduce outage if fades decorrelate and the code combines independently faded observations, at the cost of spreading energy over that diversity, requiring a calibrated channel model and occupying a longer interval. An outage lasting hours cannot be remedied with receiver compute.

The low-SNR AWGN limit needs a peak-constraint qualification. For an unquantized memoryless noncoherent complex Rayleigh channel with **average power only and no peak constraint**, arbitrarily peaky signaling can recover the AWGN capacity-per-unit-energy slope. If the received noise has unit variance and a nonzero input has energy $s$, the output laws are $\mathcal{CN}(0,1+s)$ under transmission and $\mathcal{CN}(0,1)$ under silence, with divergence $D=s-\log(1+s)$ nats; dividing by $s$ tends to 1 nat per unit energy as $s\to\infty$. At any finite peak-to-average ratio or fixed absolute peak, this flash limit is unavailable and the low-power behavior can be worse. Sethuraman, Wang, Hajek and Lapidoth analyze stationary temporally correlated fading under peak and average constraints and make the peak constraint explicit. [“Low SNR Capacity of Noncoherent Fading Channels,” IEEE TIT 55(4), 2009](https://doi.org/10.1109/TIT.2009.2012995); [Koch & Lapidoth, “At Low SNR Asymmetric Quantizers Are Better,” IEEE TIT 59(9), 2013](https://doi.org/10.1109/TIT.2013.2262919). Matrix Modem's fixed peak and occupied bandwidth make the no-peak asymptote an unattainable bound, not an achievable claim.

There is a useful counterpoint: Durisi, Morgenshtern and Bölcskei show that for continuous-time WSSUS **underspread** Rayleigh channels, noncoherent capacity remains close to equal-bandwidth AWGN capacity at practically relevant SNR over their modeled class, largely independent of fine scattering-function support. This is mathematical support for not declaring all unknown HF phase fatal. It does not cover every severe, nonstationary, non-WSSUS ionospheric episode or establish that a specific tone's coherence is long enough. The arXiv record is [here](https://arxiv.org/abs/1107.2527); it is identified as the author manuscript of “On the Sensitivity of Continuous-Time Noncoherent Fading Channel Capacity,” IEEE TIT 58(10), 2012. The underlying message is conditional: slow/underspread channel uncertainty can be manageable, whereas severe fast fading and correlated outages need explicit treatment.

Recent finite-blocklength work is still model-specific. Qi and Koch (published in IEEE TIT in 2026 after its 2025 preprint) derive a high-SNR normal approximation for **noncoherent multiple-antenna Rayleigh block fading**, accurate as SNR and the number of coherence intervals tend to infinity. That asymptotic regime is not the low-C/N_0, single-receiver HF case, but their paper confirms that coherence interval count and finite frame size are core terms, and that the more general nonasymptotic bounds can be computationally demanding. [Qi & Koch, DOI 10.1109/TIT.2025.3641371](https://doi.org/10.1109/TIT.2025.3641371); [author accepted manuscript](https://www.tsc.uc3m.es/~koch/files/MIMO_NA_TIT.pdf).

## Orthogonal energy detection and sparse signaling

Noncoherent orthogonal FSK is a credible reference family because each symbol conveys information in the selected frequency while avoiding absolute carrier phase. For independent equal-energy orthogonal waveforms in complex AWGN with unknown phase, the ideal receiver forms one energy statistic per tone and chooses among them; the transmitted bin has a noncentral chi-square energy distribution and the other $M-1$ bins have noise-only exponential energies. Increasing $M$ lowers required $E_b/N_0$ toward the wideband energy limit in classical unconstrained models, but increases occupied bandwidth, hypothesis count, synchronization sensitivity, and peak-to-average structure. Soft energy metrics and coding across symbols matter. A hard argmax or 1-bit threshold throws information away. Primary treatments include Stark's capacity and cutoff-rate calculation for noncoherent FSK under nonselective Rician fading, which explicitly compares soft and hard decisions, and NASA's coded hard-decision MFSK report. These models are narrower than time-varying HF. [Stark, “Capacity and Cutoff Rate of Noncoherent FSK with Nonselective Rician Fading,” IEEE Transactions on Communications 33(11), 1985](https://doi.org/10.1109/TCOM.1985.1096232); [NASA report, Performance of Coded, Noncoherent Hard-Decision MFSK Systems](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19730008433).

For candidate 1 transmitted, let $y=a s_1+n$ under $H_1$ and $y=n$ under $H_0$. For unit-norm template columns $S=[s_1,\ldots,s_K]$, the bank $z=S^Hy$ contains the complex projections. With known amplitude and phase in circular AWGN of complex variance $\nu$, the log likelihood ratio for candidate $k$ is $2\operatorname{Re}(a^*z_k)/\nu-|a|^2/\nu$. Averaging a uniform unknown phase at known $|a|$ gives $\log I_0(2|az_k|/\nu)-|a|^2/\nu$, monotone in $|z_k|^2$, with $I_0$ the modified Bessel function. Maximizing over a free deterministic complex gain gives log GLR $|z_k|^2/\nu$. A random fading model instead uses its prior and time correlation. A null decision needs a calibrated threshold. The Gram matrix $S^H S$ determines how nonorthogonal tones and shifted templates correlate under white noise.

If the receiver tests $K$ plausible frequencies, times, shifts, drift rates, channel templates and keys, per-template $P_{FA}$ cannot be held fixed while claiming a fixed global false-alarm probability. For $K$ tests the union bound $P_{FA,global}\le\sum_jP_{FA,j}$ is safe but may be loose; use the actual joint null distribution or a held-out empirical maximum statistic for correlated templates. Sequential repeated looks add another multiple-testing penalty unless the stopping rule is calibrated. A strong GPU candidate-bank throughput does not itself solve this detection-statistics issue. Preserve the null output and independent noise-only trials in every sensitivity report.

Pulse-position and on-off modulation are canonical ways to approach average-energy bounds by making rare pulses recognizable among noise-only positions. In a finite-duration bandlimited channel, the number of useful time-frequency slots is finite, however. If each energetic slot exceeds the transmitter's peak constraint, one cannot take the asymptotic sparse limit for free. A fixed mean transmit power combined with fixed peak power bounds duty-cycle sparsity. LPI and receive sensitivity also pull in opposite directions: a highly energetic rare pulse can improve a known receiver's detection while making an unknown observer's energy or cyclostationary test easier, especially after a long observation. Verdú's result is a theoretical model result, not an LPI guarantee.

For scale, if there are $K$ ideal unit-exponential null scores, a threshold of $\ln(K/\alpha)$ controls family-wise false alarm at $\alpha$ by the union bound even with correlation between the scores. Looking at up to 1,409 frequencies at each of 125 starts/s gives about 634 million looks in an hour. The illustrative threshold for $\alpha=0.01$ per hour is then 24.87 in units of mean noise energy. This counts every look under the stated noise law. Real null scores may involve colored or impulsive noise, clipping and adaptive selection. Those cases require distinct calibration.

## Four-bit arithmetic and quantization caveat

“INT4 arithmetic suffices” can refer to four-bit sample quantization, four-bit template elements, four-bit products, four-bit accumulated correlation values, or four-bit final likelihood scores. These choices are not equivalent and no source above implies that four-bit accumulation preserves channel information. A length-$L$ dot product of signed values in $[-7,7]$ can reach magnitude $49L$; even four-bit sample inputs with bounded quantization require more than four accumulator bits to represent a long exact sum. Saturation or early clipping biases the energy and correlation tails used for threshold decisions. A viable design can keep input and multiply values at four bits while accumulating exact partial sums in a wider representation, or can keep several low-precision limbs and combine them exactly. Either choice is a software/hardware contract to verify against actual accelerator instructions and candidate-tail error rates.

Koch and Lapidoth show that a symmetric one-bit quantizer retains $2/\pi$ of the low-SNR capacity slope for a real Gaussian channel (about 1.96 dB penalty). An asymmetric quantizer with flash signaling can recover the slope. In the **noncoherent Rayleigh** case they prove an unavoidable low-SNR loss for one-bit output quantization. In the coherent case a channel-dependent quantizer can avoid the loss. These findings depend on the specific channel and quantizer assumptions. [Koch and Lapidoth, *At Low SNR Asymmetric Quantizers Are Better*, IEEE TIT 59(9), 2013](https://doi.org/10.1109/TIT.2013.2262919), [open manuscript](https://arxiv.org/abs/1201.2523).

A favorable four-bit example follows from a local calculation. For independent real `N(μ,1)` samples and a weak mean shift, use 16 bins with interior thresholds `jΔ`, `j=−7,…,7`, and infinite outer endpoints. For `[l,u)`, its probability at zero mean is `Φ(u)−Φ(l)` and its first derivative is `φ(l)−φ(u)`. Fisher information about the mean from the quantized observation is `Σ(φ(l)−φ(u))²/(Φ(u)−Φ(l))`, relative to one for the unquantized sample. At `Δ=0.35` noise standard deviations this is 0.9887, or about 0.049 dB local information loss, using the exact code-domain score. This is one gain-normalized weak mean detector with known signal shape and independent Gaussian noise. Quantized template coefficients, linear integer scoring, unknown-phase covariance, colored or impulsive noise, a poor gain setting and bounded accumulators impose other potential losses. Recent few-bit line-spectrum work analyzes related effects under its own sinusoid and noise assumptions. [Zhu et al., author version](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.00491).

At matched signal energy, noise, channel, code, bank and global false-alarm target, compare analysis-only reference scores with the actual proposed post-boundary four-bit paths. Measure detection, false alarms, erasures, complete-message errors, input levels and clipping. Verify exact arithmetic against the chosen representation. A single favorable reference match cannot establish performance in this broader comparison.

## Distinguish observable detection from decoding

For a known template $s$ with $\|s\|^2=1$, circular complex Gaussian noise $n\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,\sigma^2 I)$ and signal amplitude $a$, $z=s^H y$ is complex Gaussian. Under $H_0$, $|z|^2/\sigma^2$ is unit-exponential. Under $H_1$, $2|z|^2/\sigma^2$ has a noncentral chi-square law with two real degrees of freedom and noncentrality $2|a|^2/\sigma^2$. This gives the ROC for an energy test at a known template. Unknown time, frequency, drift, fading or impulsive noise changes the distribution and calls for separate calibration.

Three different performance questions must remain separate:

* **Known-template detectability:** given the correct synchronization and one candidate, what energy gives a stated P_D at a stated P_{FA}?
* **Acquisition/admission:** over all unknown public signal hypotheses, which candidate is likely correct and how many false admissions occur per listening hour or per complete stream?
* **Reliable information delivery:** with a fixed total waveform duration/energy and a public message/framing contract, what is the probability the entire stream is recovered without false bytes?

The first can look very good while the second and third remain poor. A vast search can manufacture high scores in background noise. Cryptographic pseudorandom symbol schedules can conceal obvious structure from a casual observer, but they do not improve the matched receiver's C/N_0 and they do not remove multiple testing. Measure LPI separately using specified observer observation time, location, antenna, receiver bandwidth, channel, false-alarm criterion and pattern knowledge. Receiver-side decode success is not the observer's detection threshold.

## What is supportable before a modem experiment

The literature supports these initial constraints and comparisons:

* Build a **known-synchronization AWGN ceiling** from measured C/N_0, selected net rate, occupied bandwidth, and exact duration. Keep Shannon/finite-blocklength bounds labeled as theoretical.
* Retain noncoherent orthogonal FSK or sparse energy signaling as plausible simple baseline families. Compare them against coherent/short-coherent blocks only where acquisition or phase continuity is credible, and against chirp/spread methods under matched bandwidth, energy, time, channel, and search burden. No family is eliminated by the earlier GPU implementation's utilization.
* Test a matrix hypothesis bank as an implementation of batched likelihood projections, while separately implementing/calibrating the noise null, template normalization, global false alarm, and bit/message likelihood combination. The matrix multiply is a workhorse, not the whole decoder.
* Keep a clean, stationary AWGN scenario separate from noncoherent slow block fade, faster frequency-selective fading, measured replay, disturbed nonstationary episodes, burst interference, wrong-key search, and no-path outage. Report lower-tail signal/error/outage behavior rather than only mean PSD or average BER.
* Treat 6.25 Hz / 160 ms as a first test point: 1/T=6.25 Hz orthogonal-bin spacing only for an ideal rectangular symbol. Sweep spacing and duration independently. Do not conflate Doppler spectrum width, delay spread, symbol duration, occupied bandwidth, and data rate.
* Use hardware-faithful 4-bit quantization only after the floating point statistical reference is frozen. Stress scale variation and extreme likelihood tails. A faster per-hypothesis operation helps the complete receiver only if its output fidelity and candidate throughput survive the full search and code.

The research here is not an exhaustive systematic review and it is not a numerical qualification of Matrix Modem. Most exact probabilistic bounds above assume Gaussian noise or Rayleigh fading with precise stationarity/CSI assumptions. Their applicability to HF needs the calibrated/measured channel and noise work documented in the repository research chapters. Several design choices remain unsettled, especially the allowable transmit peak-to-average ratio, target complete-message failure probability, minimum/maximum message durations, and whether the “INT4” requirement allows exact wider accumulators for four-bit products. These are research/contract questions; the calculations do not decide them.

* Li, Sboui, Rezki & Alouini, “On the Capacity of Fading Channels with Peak and Average Power Constraints at Low SNR,” *IEEE TVT* 68(1), 2019, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1109/TVT.2018.2882684); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1804.06861). Low-SNR peak-to-average constraint consequences.
* Qi & Koch, “On Noncoherent Multiple-Antenna Rayleigh Block-Fading Channels at Finite Blocklength,” *IEEE TIT* 72(3), 2026, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1109/TIT.2025.3641371); [accepted author manuscript](https://www.tsc.uc3m.es/~koch/files/MIMO_NA_TIT.pdf). Recent, high-SNR asymptotic result with explicit limits on applying it to weak single-antenna HF.

Publication date and evidence scope matter. The 2026 Qi/Koch result postdates the 2025 preprint but remains high-SNR MIMO block-fading theory. The repository's measured/reconstructed propagation evidence is geographically and temporally bounded. The table's three noise densities are scenarios for arithmetic only, not empirical claims.
