# Waveform, repetition, and coding evidence


This chapter reviews the waveform hypotheses for Matrix Modem. It is a research basis for controlled comparison, not a selected or qualified design. The strongest immediate lesson from FT8 is that a successful weak-signal mode is a system of framing, synchronization, coding, waveform shaping, and decoder decisions. Its 6.25 Hz spacing is a consequence of its 160 ms symbol interval and chosen waveform. It does not establish that this spacing is generally robust to an HF Doppler spread of 6.25 Hz, nor that repeated tones and received-history correlation will retain their benefit in a changing multipath channel.

## What FT8 does and what transfers

The original FT4/FT8 protocol paper describes FT8 as a 79-symbol, continuous-phase frequency-shift-keyed waveform. Its 58 data symbols each carry one of eight tone indices, with three Costas synchronization arrays separating the data sections. FT8 uses a 160 ms symbol interval, so 6.25 Hz is the reciprocal interval and the tone increment. Its frequency-deviation pulse is Gaussian smoothed. The envelope is constant over the body of the transmission and ramps at the ends. The protocol protects a 77-bit message plus 14-bit CRC with an LDPC(174,91) code, then maps 174 bits to 58 three-bit tone symbols. Its decoder combines synchronization and code constraints, with belief propagation and ordered-statistics decoding. These details matter when interpreting familiar weak-signal performance. The authors report that shortening the earlier JT65 transmission schedule to FT8's 15-second schedule accepts roughly 4 dB sensitivity loss relative to JT65. Their simulation also finds benefit from block detection and a hybrid decoder, with results conditioned on their AWGN and frequency-spread test channels. A raw bank of eight independent symbol-energy tests is not an FT8-equivalent receiver. [Franke, Somerville, and Taylor, “The FT4 and FT8 Communication Protocols,” QEX, 2020](https://wsjt.sourceforge.io/FT4_FT8_QEX.pdf).

For Matrix Modem, the transferable ideas are testable engineering choices: constant-envelope continuous phase, shaped frequency transitions, known synchronization structure, a short-packet code, soft information into the decoder, and a receiver that tests timing and frequency uncertainty. The message format, scheduled slot, oscillator assumptions, channel conditions, sync placement, and decoding procedure differ, so FT8's threshold cannot be carried over as a Matrix Modem performance prediction. Its approximately 50 Hz occupied signal also says little about a proposed 2.8 kHz search or hop span. The full 2.8 kHz may be a receiver observation region, while a transmitted single-tone waveform at any instant occupies a much narrower spectrum. The observer, transmitter, and receiver bandwidths must be separately specified.

FT8 is also not simply a bank of rectangular, phase-discontinuous tones. The QEX description explicitly models phase as the integral of a shaped instantaneous frequency deviation. Smoothing lowers keying transients and changes spectral occupancy and inter-symbol behavior. A test that uses rectangular tones can still be a helpful mathematical baseline, but should not be described as simulating FT8 or a spectrally shaped candidate. The actual sampled waveform and passband filtering must enter the comparison.

## Tone spacing, duration, and disturbed channels

For rectangular complex tones observed over a common interval `T`, the inner product of two tones separated by an integer multiple of `1/T` is zero in the ideal synchronized, constant-gain model. Thus `T = 160 ms` gives a nominal first orthogonal spacing of 6.25 Hz. This is a property of the integration interval and signal pair, not a rule that the channel itself must have a spread below that number. Residual frequency offset, timing error, pulse shaping, a channel that changes during the symbol, multiple delayed paths, and front-end filtering all perturb the ideal correlations. In practical tests, compute the actual cross-ambiguity or Gram matrix after passing each waveform through the channel and receive filter. Do not infer orthogonality solely from tone labels.

Delay spread and Doppler spread cause different problems. A sufficiently delayed echo produces inter-symbol interference or a frequency-selective response across the waveform. A time-varying channel changes the complex path gains during observation and can spread energy in frequency. A common Doppler shift or oscillator error translates all tones, while Doppler spread describes time variation across components. Searching a common offset can address the first effect, but cannot undo arbitrary within-symbol variation or resolve unresolved paths. ITU-R F.1487's Watterson model uses delayed propagation paths with time-varying complex gains, and its own description identifies the model as a stationary Gaussian-scatter model. Treat it as an important reproducible channel family, not a complete description of disturbed, transient, or nonstationary ionospheric propagation. [ITU-R F.1487](https://www.itu.int/rec/R-REC-F.1487/en).

The practical trade is coupled. Longer symbols provide more integration time and narrower nominal tone spacing, but expose each decision to more channel evolution, lower raw throughput, and lengthen acquisition and message completion. Wider tone spacing or more rapid frequency changes can reduce sensitivity to a particular offset or allow frequency diversity, but consume spectrum and may move a symbol into a different frequency-selective fade. Neither tone spacing nor hop rate should be set directly equal to a single quoted Doppler or delay-spread number. Build a parameter sweep from measured, source-limited channel strata, then look for regimes where the ranking reverses.

The proposal of roughly 6.25 Hz tones across 2.8 kHz corresponds arithmetically to about 448 nominal intervals. This is not automatically 448 statistically independent channel samples or 448 independent hypotheses. Adjacent frequencies can have strongly correlated propagation and receiver noise, frequency uncertainty can leak energy into adjacent templates, and selecting the maximum from a larger bank raises the noise-only false-alarm statistic. Conversely, using more tones in an FSK alphabet can represent more labels per symbol, but its energy-per-bit advantage depends on the exact power constraint, code, receiver, and latency. The tone count across an observation band and the number of data choices in one symbol are separate design parameters.

## Repeating a pattern and correlating received history

Repeating a short keyed tone sequence has a plausible acquisition advantage. If the channel and oscillator change slowly relative to the repetition interval, repeated observations may support timing refinement, consistency checks, channel estimation, or combining. When a known transmitted sequence is available, a matched filter against candidate time/frequency/channel shifts has a well-defined likelihood interpretation under its assumed noise and channel model. A reference waveform built from an earlier received segment can also carry channel information that an independently generated ideal reference lacks.

Those advantages are conditional. A received-history template contains noise and interference as well as signal. Correlating a noisy observation with a second noisy observation includes signal-signal, signal-noise, noise-signal, and noise-noise terms. The shared reference also makes scores for later candidates statistically dependent. A reference corrupted by a deep fade, interferer, wrong alignment, or clipping can mislead every comparison that reuses it. Repetition does not create independent received energy or remove the noise in its reference. At low SNR, a transmitted reference or differential detector can pay a sensitivity cost for spending energy on the reference and for multiplying observations that each include noise. Such schemes require direct comparison with a known-template detector at the same total energy and observation time.

Frequency hopping can mitigate a persistent narrowband interferer or expose different frequency-selective gains when frequencies are sufficiently decorrelated. It can also decorrelate the very channel response that a received-history reference was meant to reuse. A differential detector that carries a reference across a hop may be particularly fragile if the channel coefficients at the two frequencies are weakly correlated. Analysis of frequency-hopped noncoherent GFSK in correlated Rayleigh fading explicitly treats a separate first-symbol error at a hop because its previous-bit reference comes from another frequency; the predicted cross-hop channel correlation falls with frequency separation under that paper's model. This is useful counterevidence to the idea that a hop automatically preserves differential correlation. Its land-mobile channel model is not an HF ionospheric model, so use it to motivate an HF-specific test, not to predict HF error rates. [Liu and coauthors, “Performance of Frequency Hopped Noncoherent GFSK in Correlated Rayleigh Fading Channels,” IEEE ICC 2003](https://web.engr.oregonstate.edu/~hliu/papers/FHGFSK-ICC03.pdf).

The number of repetitions has a direct airtime and energy consequence. At fixed radiated power, `r` identical repetitions cost about `r` times the energy and airtime before overhead. At fixed total message energy, dividing energy across repetitions does not supply a free `r`-fold sensitivity gain. Repetition can still improve a finite detector's robustness through diversity, averaging, or a better estimate, but that gain depends on the joint distribution of the channel and noise across repeats. If the repeats fit within one fade, they may add little diversity. If separated far enough to see independent fades, the message latency and outage exposure increase. If hopping gives frequency diversity, it does not ensure independent fading across the hop set. Estimate these correlations from the channel corpus or a declared channel model.

Autocorrelation alone also has limits of identifiability. A periodic pattern can generate several plausible delay peaks. Its autocorrelation can locate a repeated structure without identifying the absolute symbol labels, hop schedule, or codeword. CFO, sample-clock error, changing path delays, and multipath can shift or split peaks. A keyed sequence can lower an uninformed observer's prior probability of predicting the sequence, but encryption does not remove waveform repetition or its second-order spectral structure. Receiver acquisition and observer detectability are different evaluations.

## Coding, interleaving, and short messages

Noncoherent orthogonal FSK has a mature coded-receiver literature. Cheng, Valenti, and Torrieri study bit-interleaved coded orthogonal FSK on block-fading channels with soft demapping, iterative channel/noise estimation, deinterleaving, and decoding. Their paper supports soft metrics and joint estimation as credible options when phase is not tracked. It assumes blocks over which the gain and noise statistics are modeled as stationary, and it evaluates a simulated block-fading system. That is not evidence for a single fixed code on arbitrary ionospheric recordings. The paper also states the basic spectral-efficiency cost of larger orthogonal alphabets. [Cheng, Valenti, and Torrieri, IEEE Transactions on Wireless Communications, 2007](https://doi.org/10.1109/TWC.2007.06040154).

A more recent 2024 IEEE JSAC paper explicitly studies noncoherent MFSK with receiver Doppler-frequency uncertainty. It derives soft-decoder metrics and evaluates LDPC decoding, including cases with and without average signal/noise power estimates. This strengthens the case for separating a known-CFO detector from a detector that integrates or marginalizes over CFO uncertainty. Its abstract describes non-fading models and spacecraft communications, so the paper does not establish performance under HF time-selective multipath or nonstationarity. It is a recent algorithmic comparator, not an HF qualification. [“On Noncoherent FSK Reception With Doppler Frequency Uncertainty for Space Communications,” IEEE JSAC, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1109/JSAC.2024.3365881).

Interleaving helps when coded symbols can be distributed across distinct channel states. The relevant diversity is set by the actual temporal and frequency correlation, not by the number of transmitted symbols or nominal hops. In block-fading theory, rate, codeword length, and number of independently faded blocks jointly bound diversity. Very slow fading can leave an entire short packet inside one bad state, and no interleaver shorter than the channel's correlation scale can manufacture independent fades. Deep interleaving can also add unacceptable message latency. [Guillén i Fàbregas and Caire, “Coded Modulation in the Block-Fading Channel,” IEEE Transactions on Information Theory, 2006](https://doi.org/10.1109/TIT.2005.860414).

For short messages, choose the code and frame together. Synchronization, pilot or known-reference symbols, ramp time, CRC, code termination, interleaver padding, hop transitions, and quiet-slot completion all consume resources. A longer code or deeper interleaver may improve performance on selected fading realizations but increase time-to-decision and the chance that a complete message crosses a changing channel regime. Conversely, omitting explicit known symbols could save time if joint synchronization and decoding can search them reliably, but expands the joint hypothesis space and the calibrated false-alarm burden. FT8 offers one demonstrated packet architecture, while short-block and finite-energy performance must be tested for Matrix Modem's own payload, channel and service contract.

Do not compare schemes only by uncoded BER or energy per coded bit. Report at least three matched operating constraints:

1. **Equal total radiated energy and equal payload.** Include synchronization, pilots, ramps, references, parity, guard intervals, all repetitions, and retries. Report complete-message error probability and false acceptance.
2. **Equal elapsed time and equal payload.** This reveals the throughput and deadline consequences of long symbols, interleaving, and repetition. State whether idle/hop gaps count.
3. **Equal average power and equal payload.** This reflects a power-limited transmitter and makes extra airtime consume extra energy. Also record peak envelope and duty cycle where hardware limits them.

Keep synchronization and acquisition costs inside the receiver comparison. A candidate that needs a dense start-time, frequency, hop-phase, and code-phase search should be compared with the same covered uncertainty for its alternatives. A lab result with the correct start time and known hop index is a mechanism check, not an end-to-end acquisition result.

## Bounded comparison that can discriminate the choices

Use the current project evaluation plan and channel evidence as the starting point. It already separates moderate, disturbed, delay-heavy, and adverse HF cases and requires matched total energy, explicit search coverage, physical completion, and native receiver workload. The following focused sequence addresses the waveform/repetition question without presuming a winner.

1. **Verify ideal waveform behavior first.** Generate rectangular orthogonal FSK and a shaped continuous-phase FSK candidate with identical label sequence, symbol interval, total energy, and passband. Measure the sampled waveforms' spectrum, autocorrelation, cross-ambiguity over timing/CFO, peak envelope, and inter-symbol memory. Confirm symbol decisions on noise-free flat channels, then on AWGN and a single delayed path. Retain the ideal rectangular result as an analytical control.
2. **Separate detection from coding.** Compare coherent known-template, unknown-constant-phase noncoherent energy, segmented coherent/noncoherent combining, and history-based repetition scores using identical noisy received windows and coverage. Add soft symbol likelihoods to one fixed short code. Preserve noise-only, wrong-key, wrong-ordinal and ambiguous-period controls. Calibrate false detection after searching the full stated hypothesis bank.
3. **Test channel memory explicitly.** For every candidate, measure repeats with the same frequency and with a fixed pseudo-random hop pattern at several predeclared hop intervals. Include a schedule-aware known-transmit template, an ideal channel-free template, and a template constructed from received history. Sweep symbol/repetition spacing across the measured channel correlation times and frequency correlations. Include path delay changes and fades, not only independent Rayleigh samples.
4. **Compare coded diversity.** Compare no interleaving, a short interleaver, and an interleaver long enough to span the measured correlation scale, subject to a declared message latency. Count parity, pilot, and padding overhead in total energy and time. Retain an outage condition where the entire packet is attenuated, since coding cannot recover absent information without a diversity resource outside that fade.
5. **Repeat on source-informed holdouts.** Tune parameters on one subset of channel episodes, then evaluate frozen candidates on separate episodes and noise/interference controls. Use channel episodes, not adjacent symbols cut from one episode, as the independent sampling unit. Keep moderate and rare severe strata separate so an easy majority cannot hide a broken channel regime.

The key outcomes are complete-message delivery and false acceptance, not peak matrix throughput alone. For computational feasibility, measure the complete candidate search including template preparation, correlation, soft-metric generation, decoder, retries or fallbacks, and input accumulation. A fixed-template correlation matrix may use GPUs well, but efficient FFT/filter-bank structure may dominate when tones lie on a regular grid. Compare both while preserving the same public hypothesis coverage. Tensor Core utilization is an implementation result, not proof of waveform merit.

## Research position

The literature supports taking orthogonal or near-orthogonal FSK, shaped CPFSK, noncoherent or segmented-coherent detection, soft decoding, and appropriately matched interleaving seriously. It also supports questioning a fixed spacing, assuming repetitions provide independent diversity, or treating received-history correlation as a noiseless channel reference. FT8 supplies a useful packet-design precedent, not a performance guarantee. The frequency-hopped differential-detection study supplies a concrete reason to test how hopping damages cross-observation channel correlation. The HF model and measurement literature supplies the need to go beyond one stationary two-path benchmark.

No waveform family is established here as best for Matrix Modem. A better combination may emerge from joint synchronization and decoding, a different pulse, a shorter acquisition prefix, a robust fallback, another detector, or a code designed around measured HF correlation. Any conclusion remains conditional on the operating band, path class, oscillator and front-end errors, power/latency limits, transmission schedule, and receiver's public hypothesis and resource contract.
