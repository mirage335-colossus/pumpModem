# Keys, stream semantics, and U.S. amateur spectrum rules

Research date: 2026-11-24. Regulatory text was checked against the eCFR pages current through 2026-11-20, with the 2023 FCC data-emission order and the 2026 60 m amendment checked as history. This is a technical research note, not a legal opinion. The rule application depends on the actual service, emission, station, band, jurisdiction, and transmitted content.

## Findings that constrain the design

1. A secret-key AES/Serpent payload mode is confidentiality encryption. U.S. amateur stations generally may not transmit messages encoded to obscure meaning under 47 CFR 97.113(a)(4). The exception is narrow. Section 97.211(b) allows a designated telecommand station to send special codes intended to obscure telecommand messages to the space station. Section 97.309(b) independently limits unspecified digital codes and says those codes must not be transmitted to obscure a communication's meaning. Therefore, the loaded-key file-transfer mode cannot be represented as an ordinary amateur-radio operating mode. A published all-zero key has no confidentiality and is publicly reversible, but the legal status of a particular transformed code still depends on its purpose and characteristics. This finding does not resolve whether every publicly decodable transform is permissible.

2. The current 2.8 kHz figure is an authorized-bandwidth limit for specified data bands in §97.307(f)(3), and appears separately for 60 m in §97.307(f)(14). It is not a 26 dB emission mask. Part 97 instead requires no more bandwidth than necessary, confinement to the band or segment, and avoidance of splatter/keyclick interference (§97.307(a)–(c)). Below 30 MHz, transmitter spurious emissions have a separate requirement, generally at least 43 dB below mean fundamental power for transmitters installed after 2003 (§97.307(d)). A 2.2 kHz Matrix profile would be an engineering margin choice, not the text of the 2.8 kHz rule.

3. Part 2 defines occupied bandwidth by the two frequencies outside which each side contains 0.5% of the emission's total mean power (§2.202(a)). This is a measurement definition, not a 26 dB point. The exact 26 dB wording found in FCC rules belongs to other services, including cellular §22.917, and must not be imported into Part 97. Measure the emitted RF signal through the intended transmitter chain. Audio passband width alone does not certify the RF occupied bandwidth or out-of-band emissions.

4. Existing code already has a versioned named keyring with separate 64-byte AES and Serpent roots, HKDF purpose labels, an audio collection path, optional OS-only fallback, cleansing, confidentiality and MAC APIs, and physical message-completion logic. The selected built-in Amateur roots are all zero and provide neither confidentiality nor sender authentication. The code comments and maintained docs explicitly describe audio as supplementary input, not an entropy estimate. These are useful shared foundations for file interoperability, but Matrix-specific key-domain changes should be derived in new Matrix-scoped domains rather than changing the existing modem constructions. One terminology distinction matters: current Robust's random-access payload stream XORs independent Serpent-CTR and AES-CTR keystreams; it is not sequential cipher composition. Fast's protected-file path sequentially encrypts Serpent-CBC then AES-CBC and authenticates with encrypt-then-HMAC. “AES + Serpent cascade” therefore needs a precise Matrix mode definition.

5. A clock or non-transmitted counter can avoid adding a nonce field to the RF stream only if sender and receiver derive identical coordinates and the coordinates never repeat under a key. Current Robust streams intentionally repeat when key, purpose, epoch, and offset repeat; confidentiality streams likewise repeat for a repeated key, timestamp, and offset. The timestamp is a whole-second epoch. Two operators using the same key in the same second can therefore reuse stream bytes at matching offsets. For CTR encryption, this exposes the XOR of the corresponding plaintexts. A keyed waveform schedule can also repeat, which risks recognizability and wrong-key admission assumptions. Different frequency-domain keys mitigate only cases where the configured shift buckets differ.

6. CTR encryption is length-preserving and adds no padding, but does not authenticate or detect active modification. Authentication is a separate construction and normally consumes tag bits. If Matrix must transmit no additional bits for confidentiality, that is achievable with a stream construction; if it also needs cryptographic integrity, the tag must use reserved existing redundancy or consume additional wire capacity. FEC success, a public checksum, a recognizable pattern, or a wrong-key score is not a substitute for a secret-key authenticator.

7. Physical quiet-slot completion can establish that the receiver has observed an end of transmission under its channel and detector assumptions. It cannot prove that the transmitter intended no further data. In a stream with no declared total length, a complete authenticated block prefix may still be a valid shorter stream after truncation. Whole-stream completion needs a final authenticated indication, a committed fixed-length contract, or an explicit application-level limit. None should let received packet lengths control DSP geometry or allocate unbounded memory.

## Federal spectrum and message rules

### What the 2.8 kHz rule says

The FCC's 2023 order removed specified baud-rate limits and adopted a 2.8 kHz authorized bandwidth for data/RTTY emissions in listed amateur bands, effective 2024-01-08. The current §97.307(f)(3) applies to the bands listed through §97.305(c), with separate 300 baud / 1 kHz FSK provisions at 2200 m and 630 m. In 60 m, §97.307(f)(14) independently sets 2.8 kHz and says data/RTTY must meet §97.309. The currently listed HF data allocations and service-specific restrictions should be checked for each intended frequency; “2.8 kHz HF” is not a license to transmit that width anywhere in HF.

Section 97.307(a) says an amateur transmission must not occupy more bandwidth than necessary for its information rate and emission type, according to good amateur practice. Paragraph (b) requires emissions from modulation to remain in the band/segment available to the control operator and forbids splatter/keyclick interference beyond the necessary bandwidth. Paragraph (c) addresses spurious emissions and harmful interference. For transmitters installed after 2003 below 30 MHz, paragraph (d) sets a distinct 43 dB spurious-emission attenuation requirement. These obligations remain relevant even if a waveform's nominal occupied-bandwidth estimate is below 2.8 kHz. In 60 m, §97.307(f)(14)(ii) additionally directs operators to limit transmission length to avoid harmful interference to U.S. Government stations.

47 CFR §2.202(a) defines occupied bandwidth using 0.5% of total mean emitted power outside each band edge. §2.202(b) defines necessary bandwidth by the class of emission and information rate. The 2.8 kHz authorized limit and the Part 2 occupied-bandwidth measurement are related but distinct concepts. Regulatory measurement details may depend on the applicable authorization and equipment procedure. The 2.2 kHz example (450–2650 Hz) is a sound engineering profile to leave radio-filter margin, but it does not itself guarantee that the RF output meets an authorized limit. A transmitter's SSB filter, IF response, audio frequency mapping, modulation products, amplifier linearity, and measurement window matter. For FHSS, verify whether the selected implementation is legally classified as a data emission or spread-spectrum emission and whether the particular band authorizes it; do not assume that a narrow hop span by itself answers the classification.

### Confidentiality and amateur operation

Section 97.113(a)(4) prohibits amateur stations from transmitting “messages encoded for the purpose of obscuring their meaning,” subject to exceptions in Part 97. The directly relevant exception in §97.211(b) is for special codes sent by an eligible telecommand station to the space station it controls. It is not a general encrypted-file exception. Section 97.309(b) allows an unspecified digital code only subject to its international-agreement condition and explicitly disallows using it for the purpose of obscuring meaning. Section 97.309(a)(4), by contrast, allows techniques with publicly documented technical characteristics when the underlying digital code is one specified in (a). Public documentation of a waveform does not make secret-key ciphertext public in meaning.

Consequently, the Matrix product concept needs clearly separate operating profiles if it is intended for both amateur and non-amateur links. For amateur operation, keep the waveform and bit transform publicly decodable and do not market or enable the secret-key confidential mode as a Part 97 option. A fixed published all-zero root is not secret encryption and is already described in repository docs as providing no confidentiality/authentication. Whether even a public, reversible keyed pattern is being used “for the purpose” of obscuring meaning is a facts-and-purpose question. For other licensed services or non-radio paths, encryption still must be checked against that service's rules. One-way operation also engages §97.113(b), which prohibits one-way communications except as provided in Part 97. The user's safe-file-transfer purpose does not create a regulatory exception.

### FHSS, LPI and detectability

The authorized-emission table includes data/RTTY in selected HF segments and lists `SS` as a distinct emission type in certain higher-frequency bands. Section 97.3(c)(8) defines `SS` through bandwidth expansion and specific emission designators. If the proposed hopping or DSSS implementation falls in this class, a 2.2 kHz occupied width alone cannot authorize it on HF. Section 97.309(a)(4) addresses a publicly documented *technique* used with a code specified in that paragraph. A public key, by itself, establishes neither emission classification nor the applicable code. [§97.3](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/section-97.3), [§97.305](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/section-97.305), [§97.309](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/section-97.309), [§97.311](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/section-97.311).

The amateur emission table in §97.305 identifies where spread-spectrum emissions are authorized. Part 97's §97.305(b) says a spread-spectrum test emission may be sent only on frequencies where SS is specifically authorized. It is not safe to infer that a pseudorandom hopping pattern is authorized because its total span is under 2.8 kHz or because a radio can generate it. The applicable band, classification, equipment and operating rules should be verified before an amateur implementation uses FHSS.

LPI is not a legal conclusion and encryption does not make an RF signal unobservable. A public sequence, pseudorandom sequence, repeated calendar, tone occupancy, or hopping process can be detected statistically without recovering payload bits. Conversely, operating below the local noise floor in one receiver bandwidth does not bound detectability at every receiver, because the observer can integrate over time or use prior knowledge. Measure the emitted waveform at RF, then compare fixed false-alarm / missed-detection operating points for at least an energy detector and a known-structure detector against representative receiver-side background records. State antenna, front end, bandwidth, observation duration, channel and observer prior knowledge. The intended receiver and a third-party observer need separate detection experiments.

### One-way operation and station identification

The modem can operate without an acknowledgement channel. Whether one-way *communications* are authorized is a separate question. Section 97.113(b) limits them to specifically permitted uses. Section 97.111(b) lists examples such as emergency communications, information bulletins and telemetry, and brief station adjustment or two-way-establishment transmissions. The existence of a one-way file-transfer feature and use of the public root do not establish that general one-way file delivery is an allowed amateur use. Keep a lab comparator and identify the applicable authorized use before radiating a test profile. [§97.111](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/section-97.111), [§97.113](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/section-97.113).

Section 97.119 normally requires station identification at the end of a communication and at least every ten minutes during it, using an authorized identification emission. A Helper identifier field or nominal callsign choice cannot by itself meet the permitted format and timing. If an on-air profile uses separate identification, account for its airtime, energy, effective spectrum and observability, and keep its occupancy distinct from the DATA quiet-slot evidence. This becomes material for transfers lasting hours. [§97.119](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/section-97.119).

## Key material, derivation and reuse

### Existing repository contract

The current shared keyfile format is `DPMKEY04`. Each entry stores independent 64-byte Serpent and AES roots. Production key generation uses the operating-system CSPRNG in separate rounds and can mix a short physical audio digest into the Serpent lane. The implementation requires audio by default and reports an explicitly enabled fallback. The code does not estimate or certify audio min-entropy, and the additional delay between OS draws is not a proof of source independence. The keyfile's optional pad is additional wrapping material, not a password; the unpadded file itself contains key material. Secret buffers are cleansed where practical, while process memory, swap, device drivers, crash dumps and caller buffers remain beyond that guarantee. See the maintained [cryptography reference](../../../docs/crypto.md#keyfile-version-4) and [security boundary](../../../docs/security.md#cryptography-and-format-limits).

In `Crypto`, the first 32 AES-root bytes seed the existing AES-only waveform purpose keys. HKDF labels distinguish DATA, DSSS, scrambler, FHSS and MAC purposes. That is key separation between purposes, not freshness between transmissions. The current `stream(purpose, timestamp, offset, count)` derives an epoch key using the whole-second timestamp. CTR blocks use `floor(offset/16)` and byte offsets are allowed to resume inside blocks. Same key, purpose, timestamp and offset reproduce the same stream. The separate confidentiality stream uses lane-specific derived keys, timestamp and block offset for its AES-CTR and Serpent-CTR lanes, XORing their byte outputs. Its API comment also states reuse of the same address repeats bytes. The repository's security guide explicitly says same-key/canonical-address reuse is unsafe.

Current Robust configuration also has a per-configured-carrier keyed child context, documented as using a rounded nominal physical frequency. That existing frequency quantization is not the user's proposed Matrix rule. Matrix's explicit candidate is the configured shift rounded to a manually configured 10 Hz grid, excluding actual/measured frequency and FHSS hop. Implementing it would require Matrix-specific key derivation in its own domain, and a format version. It should not be silently inherited from the current Robust formula, whose quantum and context include other configuration. Specify signed frequency representation, units, nearest rounding, half-step tie behavior, negative frequencies, zero, and range before interoperability vectors are written. Integer mHz or a scaled integer avoids cross-platform floating-point boundary ambiguity.

### Uniqueness and simultaneous operators

NIST SP 800-38A requires every CTR counter block to be unique for a given key across all messages. Repeated counter blocks expose relationships between plaintexts encrypted with the matching keystream. A whole-second timestamp plus a local byte offset guarantees uniqueness within a single coordinated stream only if the sender never restarts/repeats that address. It does not enforce uniqueness across independent operators sharing a key. Two transfers started in the same second with the same key and alignment can collide. An explicit non-transmitted counter can solve this only if both ends share a deterministic, non-repeating counter state; a reset, restored backup, clock rollback, duplicated keyfile, or separately started sender can reuse it. A transmitted random nonce would resolve receiver coordination more robustly but conflicts with the no-added-bit request. Alternatives include a preallocated per-operator/session counter domain or a sufficiently long, reliably shared epoch coordinate, with a documented collision and rollback policy. No solution can guarantee cross-operator uniqueness if operators may concurrently reuse the same key and have no unique shared input.

The user accepts occasional information leakage under simultaneous key reuse. That is a conscious confidentiality trade-off, not protection supplied by AES-256 + Serpent. At a minimum, preserve the collision as an explicit residual risk and test adjacent same-second and same-shift transfers. One-way reception does not make the repetition problem disappear. A reused stream can reveal XORs even if the receiver never responds.

### Entropy claims and key sizes

“512 bits of entropy” must mean assessed min-entropy or an explicit entropy-source assurance, not a 512-bit buffer, a 512-bit hash output, or a user's subjective count of independent samples. NIST SP 800-90B describes entropy-source design and estimation, including health tests and conditioning. Hashing weak or correlated audio cannot raise its entropy above the input source's entropy. If audio is additional to secret high-quality OS randomness and mixed so it cannot replace that randomness, it may provide defense in depth, but it does not justify claiming a known 512-bit security strength without source assessment. The current code makes precisely this supplementary-input distinction.

AES-256 and Serpent-256 are two 256-bit block ciphers, but using them in a cascade does not establish 512-bit security. Security depends on the composition, key independence, nonce/counter use, implementation, and adversary model. Keep independent roots and domain separation, but state a conservative security strength and cite a construction analysis rather than adding key lengths. Schedule generation and confidentiality should have distinct purpose labels, and file encryption, tags, and hopping schedules should not share raw key bytes or counter domains. HKDF's `info` field is designed to bind derived keys to application context; it cannot repair weak source entropy or repeated stream coordinates.

### Frequency-domain schedule keys

Proposed Matrix key derivation should derive a Matrix root from the shared file-backed roots and then derive separate keys for at least payload confidentiality, integrity, symbol alphabet/schedule, and FHSS schedule. To meet the stated 10 Hz shift rule, include the rounded *configured shift* in the derivation context and omit the changing hop frequency. One interoperable sketch is:

```text
shift_bucket = round_to_nearest_configured_10_Hz(integer_shift_hz)
K_matrix   = HKDF-Extract(fixed Matrix salt, selected AES/Serpent root material)
K_payload  = HKDF-Expand(K_matrix, "Matrix/v1/payload" || shift_bucket, length)
K_auth     = HKDF-Expand(K_matrix, "Matrix/v1/auth"    || shift_bucket, length)
K_schedule = HKDF-Expand(K_matrix, "Matrix/v1/schedule" || shift_bucket, length)
K_hops     = HKDF-Expand(K_matrix, "Matrix/v1/hops" || shift_bucket, length)
```

This is a design sketch, not a settled wire contract. Use explicit framing and signed integer units. If transmitter and receiver choose different local shift values, rounded-key mismatch rejects reception. If two operators share the same key and shift bucket, their derived streams still collide if epoch and counter also match. Do not add hop frequency to the key context if the explicit compatibility requirement says keys must remain stable through hopping. Do include a protocol/version label so Matrix changes cannot perturb another modem or consume its key domain.

## Authentication, wrong-key admission, and safe streaming

CTR and CBC in NIST SP 800-38A supply confidentiality. HMAC is a separate keyed message-authentication construction (RFC 2104). NIST's authenticated-encryption work and Bellare–Namprempre's analysis show how the composition order affects authenticity. If a separately budgeted authentication design is selected, its tag should bind payload, protocol version, configured profile, shift bucket, stream epoch/ordinal and coding metadata as applicable. Validate a block's tag before release and bind its stream position to resist replay, reordering and splicing.

There is a direct policy conflict between *no encryption overhead* and *a cryptographic tag for arbitrary content*. A fixed-length tag adds redundancy. If the transmission's already-required FEC/checking budget is used for a tag, it is still taking capacity from that budget and must be analyzed for both decoding and forgery probability. A public CRC only detects accidental errors; it does not stop intentional changes. A public deterministic key authenticates nobody. A wrong-key test based on energy/correlation scores is useful to limit false admission but is not a cryptographic identity check. Existing Robust code describes its selectable wrong-key policies as nested evidence filters, not calibrated wrong-key probabilities. Keep this distinction explicit.

For continuous streaming, successful physical completion requires scored quiet observations with fixed physical geometry. Treat EOF, UI closure and local quotas as incomplete stops. Keep missing physical blocks as erasures in position. A final authenticated stream commitment could detect truncation, but would consume capacity and cannot supply physical completion evidence. Without a length or final commitment, a valid authenticated block prefix can remain valid when every later block is omitted. Physical quiet plus a valid prefix therefore leaves authenticated stream completeness unresolved. [Integrity and endpoint limits](../../../docs/fast-capacity-integrity.md), [physical-completion contract](../../../docs/robust-manual-capacity.md#physical-completion-and-security).

## Candidate implementation constraints (not a final design)

- Keep the common keyfile loader/generator as the interoperability boundary. Derive Matrix-only child roots with a versioned protocol domain; leave Fast, Robust, Helper and Legacy key derivations unchanged.
- Keep waveform schedules and payload confidentiality in different labeled key domains. Derive shift-bucket context from the manually configured shift, with deterministic integer rounding. Exclude FHSS hop from derivation to preserve the stated property.
- Treat the public Amateur root as publicly computable. It can select a public schedule or public checksum profile, but cannot provide confidentiality, sender authentication, or protection against intentional forgery.
- Pick and specify the complete transfer address before relying on deterministic CTR. Include a no-repeat policy that survives clock rollback/restart and describes accepted concurrent-key reuse. A wall clock alone is not a uniqueness guarantee.
- Use a length-preserving confidentiality transform if no encryption bytes may be added. Specify integrity/tag accounting separately. Do not imply a cascade means 512-bit security or derive an entropy claim from keyfile sizes.
- Measure RF at the antenna port. Keep 2.2 kHz as a tunable filter-margin profile. For the requested 26 dB engineering target, define the reference as a specified average in-band power density and compare out-of-band power density using the same resolution bandwidth, detector and observation window. Separately report integrated occupied bandwidth, discrete spurs and the applicable regulatory measurement. Include bursts, the worst hop/transition profile, Helper and identification, the tested radio/audio chain, and the instrument filters and observation windows.
- Gate amateur transmit profiles against the actual applicable emission authorization. Secret-key data mode and potentially FHSS need a separate regulatory decision; do not let a 2.8 kHz GUI setting imply permission to transmit.
- For LPI evidence, compare observer detection at fixed false-alarm rate, not only receiver decode probability or in-band power. Include known-pattern, known-key, and key-unknown observer conditions.
