# Groundwave evidence for 1 MHz and 30 MHz scenarios

**Research note for high-fidelity Robust Modem experiments.** This synthesis separates the 1 MHz medium-frequency case from the 30 MHz upper-HF case. It supports physically informed *scenario families*, not a universal field-strength law or a promised radio range. Measurements made for radar, broadcast, navigation, or urban-interference studies do not directly predict a Robust modem bit-error rate: they inform propagation mechanisms, field variability, and model error. Convert them into modem conditions only after defining the transmitter/receiver antennas, path, bandwidth, receiver noise/interference, waveform, and decision threshold.

## 1. What “groundwave” means here

The useful propagation calculation is the complex electric field at the receiving antenna, including its amplitude and phase. “Groundwave” is often used for a field that couples to a lossy earth or water boundary and remains useful beyond the free-space horizon. Textbook decompositions of that field into a space wave, a Norton surface-wave term, and diffraction terms depend on the mathematical representation and asymptotic regime. They should not be mistaken for independent packets that a modem can identify. At low receiving heights near a homogeneous lossy surface, direct and geometrical-optics terms can nearly cancel, leaving higher-order terms dominant; above the surface, direct, reflected, and diffracted components can instead produce delayed interference. A 1930s dispute over “surface-wave” components is a reminder to validate the *total field*, not to ascribe every received sample to a uniquely separable Zenneck mode. [Norton’s corrected plane-earth treatment](https://doi.org/10.1038/135954a0), [Burrows’s contemporary experimental discussion](https://doi.org/10.1038/138284a0), and the later [Norton surface-wave propagation paper](https://doi.org/10.1109/jrproc.1937.228544) show the historical distinction; the practical spherical-earth calculation is represented by the later groundwave methods below.

Recommendation ITU-R P.368-10 is in force for 10 kHz–30 MHz. It predicts the vertical far-field component for antennas on or near a smooth, homogeneous spherical Earth, under a linearly decreasing tropospheric refractive index, referenced to a short vertical monopole over a perfectly conducting plane. Its Annex 1 software is included. The method is a baseline, not a complete real-path model: terrain, vegetation, urban clutter, coastline transitions, sea-state scattering, specific antenna systems, and anomalous atmosphere need separate treatment. It also documents location and seasonal variability, so one nominal curve is not a reception guarantee. [ITU-R P.368-10 text and software](https://www.itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-r/rec/p/R-REC-P.368-10-202208-I%21%21PDF-E.pdf); [ITU’s method applicability table](https://www.itu.int/en/ITU-R/study-groups/rsg3/rwp3m/Pages/recp1144tables.aspx).

At 1 MHz, free-space wavelength is about 300 m. A surface-based vertical radiator and a path with substantial wet soil, fresh water, or seawater can support the conventional MF groundwave setting over ranges from local to many tens or hundreds of kilometres, subject to loss and skywave contamination. At 30 MHz, wavelength is about 10 m, ground conductivity and surface roughness matter strongly, and the case sits exactly at P.368’s upper frequency boundary. A groundwave is physically possible and is used by HF surface-wave radar, but propagation is more mixed: antenna heights of even a few wavelengths make direct/reflected space-wave and coastline diffraction contributions important, while at distance tropospheric refraction/ducting and ionospheric skywave can masquerade as “too little loss.” P.368’s 30 MHz endpoint does not establish that a received 30 MHz signal on an arbitrary path is groundwave-dominated.

### Ground constants, season, and terrain

The effective surface impedance is not determined by a short soil sample alone. The field interacts with a lateral region around the great-circle path, penetrates below the surface, and depends on moisture, temperature, salinity, geological strata, terrain irregularity, vegetation, and frequency. ITU’s 2014 handbook gives an instructive scale contrast: seawater penetration depth is around 25 cm at 1 MHz while medium dry ground is around 25 m, so underlying geology can alter MF loss even when the topsoil looks alike. It recommends path measurements for useful conductivity estimates, warns that global conductivity maps are broad planning aids, and uses roughly 1 and 5 S/m as low and typical sea conductivities. At 30 MHz, an older CCIR/ITU review reports frequency-dependent soil properties above 10 MHz and strong vegetation-related attenuation around/above 30 MHz, especially wet foliage or snow-covered trees. Do not transfer these scalar examples directly to a particular site. [ITU Ground Wave Propagation Handbook](https://www.itu.int/dms_pub/itu-r/opb/hdb/R-HDB-59-2014-PDF-E.pdf); [CCIR Report 229-6 source collection](https://search.itu.int/history/HistoryDigitalCollectionDocLibrary/4.283.43.en.1012.pdf).

The empirical literature does not establish one universal seasonal correction. The CCIR handbook summarizes 1–2 dB MF seasonal differences in Finland and almost none in an Indian rainy season. The 2014 groundwave experiment reports deep local fades likely due to buildings/mountains; a 2022 urban study finds systematically more loss than the standard prediction. These are not mutually reducible to conductivity alone: season, terrain, clutter, height, transmitter pattern, antenna coupling, noise, and skywave differ. Use a declared local parameter set and paired sensitivity brackets rather than a global “wet ground” switch.

## 3. Separate scenario families

Spatial variation measured along a mobile route is not temporal fading at a fixed station. Convert position to time only with a specified motion trajectory or a separately supported changing environment; otherwise retain it as an ensemble of locations.

The following families are suitable for a controlled Robust modem propagation study. They are *scenario design*; most numeric picks below are explicit placeholders pending measured geometry and channel calibration. They are intentionally separated by frequency because one shared path-loss curve would hide the high-frequency roughness, antenna-height, and propagation-mode distinctions.

### 1 MHz (λ ≈ 300 m): MF groundwave and mixed-path family

1. **Moderate reference — smooth, homogeneous groundwave.** Run a vertical, low-height, surface-coupled transmitter/receiver over a spherical homogeneous earth using the P.368-10 integral implementation/GRWAVE-compatible calculation. Pair at least a moist/moderate soil and a low-conductivity soil condition, and a seawater baseline where a marine route is relevant. Use measured or explicitly declared effective conductivity/permittivity and antenna heights. For a regional propagation test, the measured 1.242-MHz mixed route (Kawano et al.) is an appropriate comparison in *relative field versus distance*, but its absolute level was fitted and its land fades were scattered; it is not an exact reference realization. A useful modem “moderate” label should be chosen only after this baseline field is translated to calibrated received SNR/noise in the specified bandwidth.

2. **Adverse, still groundwave-dominated — resistive/heterogeneous land and shoreline.** Add a path with low-conductivity dry/rocky land, then an explicit land-to-sea or sea-to-land boundary. Bracket conductivity with site-specific estimates; absent data, use declared design points (for example σ = 10⁻⁴, 10⁻³, 10⁻² S/m for poor/ordinary/better land, εr reported alongside) rather than claim universal soil classes. These are *placeholders*, not values claimed by the cited field papers. Predict uniform segments with the same curved-earth engine and compare a simple Millington mixed-path envelope with a numerical inhomogeneous-ground solution. Include a slow spatial log-amplitude/phase perturbation for urban/mountain scatter only as a separately labeled clutter channel, calibrated from measured data where available. Ensure the recovery effect is not encoded as a discontinuous arbitrary gain step: compare exact/approximate boundary methods and preserve both forward and reversed path behavior.

3. **Severe stress / outage — high loss plus local fades and contaminated reception.** Compose a poor-ground or mixed-path baseline with bounded slow lognormal shadowing and a small number of narrowband, spatially correlated fades. Treat observed location spread as a sensitivity input, not a tail bound. Add independent co-channel/interference or man-made noise as a distinct receiver-boundary factor, and, if the geometry/time warrants it, add a separately identified skywave component rather than folding it into groundwave loss. Declare physical unavailability from the specified propagation, received-energy and interference conditions; report decoding/completion failure separately. A receiver failure alone does not establish that the physical link was unavailable. Do not force all weak inputs to decode as a null, or use a long-range nominal coverage calculation as an assurance of reliable digital reception.

### 30 MHz (λ ≈ 10 m): upper-HF surface-wave with mixed-mode family

1. **Moderate reference — low antenna height, conductive path, measured sea state.** Use P.368-10 at 30 MHz as its in-scope upper endpoint, with no extra validity beyond its assumptions; it supplies a smooth homogeneous groundwave baseline, not a complete coastal link prediction. For a sea route, specify vertical polarization, actual antenna height/ground plane, sea conductivity/salinity/temperature, and an explicit smooth-surface reference. Add a rough-surface impedance model as a separate condition. Barrick’s sea-state theory indicates roughness sensitivity peaks below 30 MHz (about 10–15 MHz) and is not a monotonic “more wind = fixed extra dB” law; the 25-MHz Strait of Georgia observations include a working-range increase at modest wind speeds. Preserve two outcomes: propagation loss (field/power) and operational range/decodability.

2. **Adverse — poor land path, shoreline transition, rough sea, vegetation or terrain.** Use actual path profile and measured site electrical constants if possible. The NTIA 3–30-MHz measurements provide an unusually relevant precedent for mobile path loss over differing terrain, forest, suburban obstructions, and snow, with ground constants determined by wave tilt. Compare P.368 baseline with a terrain-aware model (parabolic-equation, integral-equation/method-of-moments, or a suitable UTD impedance-wedge/shoreline model) and with site observations. At 30 MHz, a few metres is a significant fraction of a wavelength; don't silently invoke “both antennas on the surface.” A 30-MHz near-coast field model should include the direct/reflected wave where geometry allows and shoreline/cliff diffraction where appropriate. The Casciato idealized examples show why: for elevated terminals, space-wave and total field differ, while near the boundary multiple field terms interfere. Include wet foliage/snow or dry sand only as measured/stated conditional impairments.

3. **Severe stress / ambiguous-mode or outage — do not call this a groundwave penalty.** Create separate branches for (a) groundwave below decoding threshold over adverse land/sea, (b) intermittent multipath/space-wave nulls near a shoreline or elevated receive antenna, (c) anomalous tropospheric ducting/superrefraction producing unexpected enhancement or fading, and (d) ionospheric skywave where path length, frequency, time, and solar/geomagnetic conditions support it. Hansen’s 235-km study saw 32-MHz signals as much as 25 dB above smooth-earth predictions and attributed the discrepancy to ducting; 2023 long-term HF ocean data found atmosphere dominated a 7-km raised site, wind-driven waves dominated a 45-km island path, and detailed models were not consistently predictive. A high field reading at 30 MHz is therefore not a universally “good groundwave” case. Assign mechanism labels to the synthetic branches and never merge their loss statistics into a single worst-case scalar.

## 4. Candidate native model and validation ladder

An efficient research implementation can layer the following models without pretending that all are equivalent:

1. **Reference kernel:** complex attenuation and field for a vertical source over homogeneous lossy plane earth at short range, transitioning to the spherical-earth residue-series/GRWAVE solution as curvature matters. Keep conductivity, permittivity, polarization, atmosphere, and antenna reference explicit. Hill & Wait generalized spherical-earth attenuation to arbitrary complex surface impedance for 1–30 MHz, including cases with trapped-wave terms; it supplies a useful theoretical cross-check. [Hill & Wait, 1980](https://doi.org/10.1029/RS015i003p00637).
2. **Layered and mixed ground:** represent each path segment as a complex surface impedance. For amplitude-only screening, implement the ITU/Millington forward-and-reverse geometric mean, and state that it is a semi-empirical recovery approximation. For phase-sensitive or sharply layered paths, use a validated spectral/integral-equation or Monteath-style segmented solver; compare convergence and boundary cases against uniform-ground controls. The 2024 MF R-Mode article demonstrates an operational numerical integration approach with conductivity-map segments and highlights the map’s errors and temporal-change omissions. [Hehenkamp et al.](https://doi.org/10.3390/s24010282).
3. **30-MHz rough sea and coastline:** expose a smooth sea impedance baseline plus a roughness-derived effective impedance driven by an explicitly named directional sea-wave spectrum; keep incident/refracted atmospheric profile separate. For cliffs and abrupt shoreline geometry, use a suitable impedance-boundary diffraction treatment such as UTD, or a full-wave/numerical method where assumptions fail. Avoid double-counting “surface wave” loss with a second empirically imposed ground loss if the field solver already predicts that field.
4. **Observed residual channel:** only after the deterministic field baseline, introduce low-rate spatial/time variation (fading), local scatterers, antenna mismatch, atmospheric ducting, skywave, and additive noise/interference as distinct factors. Calibrate each with paired measurements at its own frequency/height/path class. Synthetic residual factors are experimental placeholders; no source here provides a distribution that covers all real weak-signal propagation.

The [appraisal protocol](../../../_appraisal/highFidelity-triple_Robust_Modem/experiment-protocol.md#groundwave-validation) specifies the staged validation procedures. A prediction or a successful simulated link does not establish the model’s applicability to another path, surface, antenna or time interval.

### Evidence coverage and unresolved limits

The source set includes classical flat-earth and spherical-earth theory; the active P.368 standard; measured 1-MHz mixed-path recovery; direct 25–32-MHz coastal/ocean observations; HF terrain data through 30 MHz; recent urban MF/HF data; and 2023–2026 marine/navigational observations that contradict oversimplified universal assumptions. The most useful contrary results are: urban measurements with greater loss than standard prediction; measured ocean conductivity with no significant seasonal loss effect in one long record; nonmonotonic wind/working-range response in a river plume; 32-MHz enhancement attributed to ducting; and NAVTEX models predicting service where measurements show unstable or absent decoding. Those results differ in frequency, route, antenna, output metric, and instrumentation and should remain so in the eventual appraisal.

There is no cited 2020–2026 field trial that establishes a universal 30.000-MHz groundwave loss distribution or a Robust-modem outage probability. The 30-MHz case therefore needs an explicitly bounded synthetic design until matched local field measurements exist. The literature does not provide a single defensible dB cutoff for “moderate,” “adverse,” or “worst.” Assign environmental labels from stated measured episodes or supported conditional received-level statistics, then report decoder outcomes separately. Preserve the path and mechanism provenance; do not define environmental severity retrospectively from whether this decoder succeeds. The strongest current prior for worst-case planning is not “maximum smooth-earth attenuation” alone: it is a set of separable hard conditions—poor conductivity, dry sand/rock/forest or obstructed urban terrain, shoreline transitions, surface roughness, height-induced multipath, local interference/noise, seasonal ground changes, and possible mode contamination—whose combination must be bounded and measured rather than extrapolated from one paper.
