# Sub-9 kHz link physics for a 200 ft top-loaded vertical

## Scope and decision summary

This note concerns weak-signal, high-fidelity channel experiments below 9 kHz, with a 200 ft (60.96 m) vertical wire and a capacitive top-hat as the lead geometry. The phrase “antenna” can mean either transmitter or receiver, and those cases have different limits: transmit efficiency and feed power depend on radiation and loss resistances; receive voltage depends on effective height, loading, matching, amplifier noise, and the local field/noise polarization. No site, soil, top-hat dimensions, operating frequency within the band, receiver input, transmitter power, or TX/RX spacing has been supplied. The calculations below are therefore scale checks and scenario guidance, not a link budget or antenna qualification.

Key implications for the channel model:

1. A 60.96 m vertical is electrically very short throughout this band: wavelength is 33.3 km at 9 kHz and 100 km at 3 kHz, so height is 0.0018–0.00061 wavelengths. A capacitive top-hat can increase capacitance, vertical current uniformity, effective height and radiation resistance relative to bare wire, but does not ensure resonance, transmit matching, bandwidth or efficiency. Hat and ground/counterpoise geometry are essential.
2. Antenna height does not specify link distance. Separate near-field coupling from distant Earth–ionosphere-waveguide propagation; the optional short-range loop and conductive-earth families below require their own geometry.
3. Long-path propagation is not a fixed scalar loss. Lightning measurements show frequency/direction, magnetic-dip, day/night ionosphere, ground-conductivity, and range-dependent mode effects. A recent inversion finds up to 12 dB/Mm ice-to-saltwater difference, but broadband integration is not an exact 3–9 kHz curve; “about 2 dB/Mm” hides conditional behavior. [VLF06](https://doi.org/10.1002/jgra.50533) [VLF07](https://doi.org/10.1029/2022JA030742) [VLF08](https://doi.org/10.1029/2024RS008150)
4. Natural noise is often lightning-dominated but impulsive, geographic, temporal and antenna dependent. The station-specific sub-9 kHz evidence is summarized once in Table 2; it is not a universal noise calibration. A fixed Gaussian PSD misses bursts and quiet intervals. [VLF04](https://www.itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-r/rec/p/R-REC-P.372-15-202109-I%21%21PDF-E.pdf) [VLF05](https://doi.org/10.1029/2020JA029101)
5. A *grounded electrode pair* (earth dipole/TTE) couples through conductive earth, not like a vertical with “worse ground.” A 2016 mine study measured ~5 Ω pair impedance on moist clay and short-message reception at 8 kHz with >180 W; it does not predict an above-ground link. A magnetic loop is a third option with orientation/area-dependent induction. [VLF10](https://doi.org/10.1155/2016/8601497) [VLF03](https://doi.org/10.2528/PIERC19090911)

No cited study establishes a range for this antenna in the modem's environment. State the propagation family and report input field or port SNR, receiver noise, outage and antenna assumptions separately; synthetic success does not qualify an installation.

## Geometry and scale checks

| Quantity | Value / relation | Meaning and limit |
|---|---:|---|
| Free-space wavelength | 33.31 km at 9 kHz; 99.93 km at 3 kHz | Standard $c/f$; below 3 kHz falls outside conventional VLF and spans lower radio bands (ELF/SLF/ULF, depending on frequency). |
| 60.96 m vertical / wavelength | 0.00183 at 9 kHz; 0.000610 at 3 kHz | Confirms electrically short behavior; this ratio alone does not give loaded antenna efficiency. |
| Quarter-wave vertical | 8.33 km at 9 kHz; 25.0 km at 3 kHz | Shows why high-power VLF aerials use capacitively top-loaded structures. |
| Frequency coverage | unspecified; $f_{min}$ matters | Antenna tuning, effective height, bandwidth, receiver filtering, and waveguide mode response change rapidly with frequency; define this before numerical calibration. |

For a short, unloaded monopole over ideal ground, effective height and radiation resistance are set by the vertical current distribution and fall rapidly with decreasing electrical length. Top-loading moves charge to the upper conductors, increases capacitance to earth and makes vertical current more nearly uniform. For an ideal symmetric horizontal hat, opposing horizontal current contributions can cancel while increasing the vertical electric dipole moment. Asymmetric or sloping loading conductors need their own current and radiation calculation; cancellation is not a general property of every hat. A T-hat, umbrella, capacity hat, and a tall vertical with radial counterpoise are not interchangeable: dimensions, wire count, radial slope/spacing, feed location, and support/ground geometry determine capacitance, effective height, current distribution, and loss. Mahmud’s numerical study compares top-hat wire counts and radial geometries against scaled model data; the older Gobbel VLF installation analysis relates capacitance, Q, bandwidth, resistance and installation losses. [VLF01](https://www.navy-radio.com/ant/vlf-ant-nps-1967.pdf) [VLF02](https://hdl.handle.net/10945/22519)

For a transmit monopole, a useful equivalent circuit has radiation resistance $R_r$, conductor/coil/ground losses $R_l$, and large capacitive reactance cancelled by loading inductance. At resonance, first-order radiation efficiency is $R_r/(R_r+R_l)$, before feed, matching, corona, and dielectric losses. This expression is not a model of received SNR. As a scale warning, Gobbel's real high-power Northwest Cape design table at 15.5 kHz specifies effective height 628 ft (191.4 m), static capacitance 0.164 μF, $R_r=0.154\ \Omega$, matching loss resistance 0.154 Ω for 50% efficiency, and 38.1 Hz antenna bandwidth (76.3 Hz including loss). This was a very large 1 MW-class installation. It is evidence that VLF systems are high-Q and loss-sensitive, not a transferable efficiency or resistance value for a 200 ft backyard wire. [VLF01](https://www.navy-radio.com/ant/vlf-ant-nps-1967.pdf)

In receive mode, model induced open-circuit voltage, effective height/area, source impedance, matching, and receiver noise. Matching trades transfer, bandwidth and interference pickup. The later loop discussion gives one measured receiver/antenna comparison; its result is not a 3–9 kHz vertical calibration. [VLF03](https://doi.org/10.2528/PIERC19090911)

## Propagation, noise, and possible failure modes

### Near-field versus Earth–ionosphere waveguide

At a transmitter–receiver spacing much smaller than wavelength, near electric and magnetic fields do not obey the inverse-distance field relation of a radiating far-field wave. Their relative magnitude and decay depend on antenna family, orientation, frequency, current/voltage and the surrounding conducting earth. A compact VLF loop study explicitly measures its 0.5 m diameter, 7.8 kHz high-power loop in the near field. At 120 m it reports 47.32 dBμA/m under approximately 1.2 kW input; its 1,446 m “communication distance” is an extrapolation from a selected 0.16 pT threshold, not a measured decoded link at that range. This is useful evidence for a distinct, measurable short-range magnetic near-field scenario, but not for a 60 m top-loaded vertical. [VLF09](https://doi.org/10.3390/nano15141062)

At long range, the ground and lower ionosphere form a waveguide. Measurements of lightning radiation yield a convenient empirical stress range, not a universal law. Hutchins et al. analyzed 8–18 kHz sferics observed at three island stations over four years: across all paths mean attenuation was 1.87 ± 1.06 dB/Mm; for paths beyond 4 Mm, mean attenuation was 1.29 ± 1.09 dB/Mm in day and 0.24 ± 0.48 dB/Mm at night. East/west direction changed attenuation by roughly 1.9–2.0 dB/Mm in their selected mostly-ocean paths. These are field-amplitude, path-specific slopes for lightning and 8–18 kHz, not a direct point-to-point modem loss formula below 9 kHz. [VLF06](https://doi.org/10.1002/jgra.50533)

Said et al.'s 2023 broadband global lightning-location inversion reinforces the directional dependence but also exposes limits: integrated broadband data mix source spectrum, receiver passband, and frequency-dependent propagation. Estimated land/ocean differences were roughly 0.5–1.5 dB/Mm for soils at or above 1 mS/m, and up to 12 dB/Mm between saltwater and low-conductivity ice categories. Westward paths near the magnetic equator can have much higher nighttime loss; however, the authors caution that in that regime the estimate entangles weak sky-wave reflection, ground-wave amplitude, destructive interference, and distance, such that the ordinary distance-invariant attenuation fit breaks down. This contradiction matters: “night always helps” is not safe for all magnetic directions and ranges. [VLF07](https://doi.org/10.1029/2022JA030742)

A 2025 LWPC modeling study focuses on the lower boundary: conductivity values at or below $10^{-3}$ S/m require better description, and path segments only tens of kilometers long can materially affect inferred VLF propagation. This supports separate ordinary-soil, conductive/coastal, and poor-ground path scenarios when a long-path claim is intended; conductivity under the antenna's ground system is additionally a local antenna-loss parameter and should not be confused with the conductivity along a remote waveguide path. [VLF08](https://doi.org/10.1029/2024RS008150)

### Atmospheric and local noise below 9 kHz

Lightning sferics are broadband transients that arrive in bursts, and global lightning fields can contribute at long range. Gu et al.'s measured noise and the study's summary of low-band propagation features are collected in Table 2; these findings do not set a universal level. [VLF05](https://doi.org/10.1029/2020JA029101)

| Source | Quantitative observation | Measurement context and limit |
|---|---|---|
| Gu et al. (2021), VLF05 | Seasonal noise variation about 10 dB; lightning within 3,500 km was the main measured contributor. The paper also summarizes attenuation rising toward ~3 kHz and waveguide resonances near ~2 kHz multiples. | Feb 2016–Feb 2019, Suizhou, China; two 7-turn orthogonal triangular loops with 7.29 m² equivalent area. CCIR captured long-term trend but overestimated this site's levels and missed short-term changes. One site is not a transferable noise calibration. [VLF05](https://doi.org/10.1029/2020JA029101) |

The inspected historical ITU-R P.372-15 remains a useful baseline catalog of atmospheric, man-made, and galactic noise and specifies a short vertical monopole over perfectly conducting ground as its reference antenna for global lightning-noise estimates. The Recommendation explicitly notes that lightning noise is generally non-Gaussian, and gives amplitude-probability-distribution parameters; its bandwidth-conversion guidance is cautioned for LF/VLF/ELF. It also says very low predicted atmospheric levels may reflect absent other noise sources. Therefore, use ITU curves to bound a climatological prior, not to assert a particular received voltage, Gaussian sample variance, or modem outage probability. [VLF04](https://www.itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-r/rec/p/R-REC-P.372-15-202109-I%21%21PDF-E.pdf)

P.372-18 (September 2026) is the current edition; see the [noise chapter](50-noise-interference-and-frontends.md) for verified edition status and access limits. The detailed statements above refer to the inspected 2021 edition, not a claimed comparison of every revision.

Receiver-site noise can differ from natural sky noise. Man-made sources named by ITU include electrical machinery, power networks, powerline communication, industrial/medical equipment, and imperfectly shielded cables. At sub-9 kHz, local mains harmonics and switching/drive components can be narrowband or impulsive; a grounded long wire can couple common-mode interference and earth-potential differences. On a quiet remote site, atmospheric transients or receiver noise may dominate instead. The model must preserve that distinction, rather than using “urban” as a single wideband noise scalar.

Ionospheric changes are frequency and path effects as well as noise changes. In 2025, Thomson et al. inferred dawn/dusk D-region parameters from transmitter amplitude and phase, with reflection heights near 70 km in daytime and 85 km at night under their path and model conditions. Solar forcing can change propagation on illuminated segments; lightning can perturb the D region; and long paths may cross both day and night. The date/path qualification matters: do not use a single fixed day/night gain offset or treat an extreme solar disturbance as guaranteed universal outage. [VLF12](https://doi.org/10.1029/2024JA033610)

Natural emissions other than lightning may also enter low-frequency data. A 2022 review of long-duration high-latitude records reports that auroral VLF emissions above 4–5 kHz can be masked by strong sferics, and some natural emissions are localized to stations within roughly 300–400 km of an ionospheric exit point. At the Kannuslehto station, orthogonal loops spanning 0.2–39 kHz recorded daytime bursts typically around 4–7 kHz and above 6–7 kHz; their source and propagation remain incompletely understood. This implies that “natural VLF noise” depends on site and desired bandwidth: an emission of interest may create co-channel interference in one setting, be absent in another, or be overwhelmed by lightning. This is not evidence for an ordinary terrestrial modem channel having those emissions everywhere. [VLF13](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10712-022-09741-0)

### Alternative antenna families

**Grounded vertical / top-loaded monopole.** This is the lead 200 ft interpretation. Transmit performance depends on hat geometry, loading coil, base feed, ground radial/counterpoise and soil. Receive performance depends on effective height, loss/noise, input impedance and common-mode rejection. The ORNL tree-loaded monopole study provides a useful caution: connecting a short grounded wire to a tree significantly changed input impedance and raised received power-line signal voltage by up to about 40 dB at its lowest tested frequencies. That is a specific measured dielectric/loading configuration, not a 40 dB benefit assigned to a capacity hat. [VLF11](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/1881163)

**Grounded electrode pair (earth dipole/TTE).** Two electrodes inject current into a conducting medium; coupling is by the electric field in soil/rock and can remain quasi-static over large distances. Xu et al.'s 2016 above-to-underground mine study used electrode arrays with 40 m spacing at surface and 80 m underground. It measured pair impedance of about 5 Ω in moist clay at surface, 13 Ω in mine rock, and used a layered earth model with conductivities of approximately 0.005–0.007 S/m; a digital short-message/voice demonstration received at 8 kHz for transmit power above 180 W. This is strong proof of possibility for a specialized through-earth path, but its geologic coupling, deployment, received sensor, and power level differ from a skywave modem. Model it as another path class only if modem use intends electrode transmission or reception. [VLF10](https://doi.org/10.1155/2016/8601497)

**Wire magnetic loop.** A loop couples to magnetic flux and can reject some electric-field/common-mode pickup depending on balance, placement, preamp and orientation. Received voltage scales as (N A\,dB/dt), so loop area, turns, orientation to horizontal (B), winding resistance, core losses, inductance, capacitance and preamp noise matter. A larger air-core loop can have better sensitivity but be cumbersome; compact ferrite/core systems trade size with permeability, loss and nonlinear behavior. Bolton and Cohen's field comparison found a 2.6 m × 1.3 m air-core loop was about 11–13 dB more sensitive than two 12-inch ferrite-core loops around 24 kHz, with 10–15 dB less between-sferic receiver noise. It is an actual antenna/receiver comparison, but the data are above the current target band and should guide an alternative family rather than a numerical 3–9 kHz correction. [VLF03](https://doi.org/10.2528/PIERC19090911)

Do not merge these into a generic “200 ft antenna” parameter: vertical electric-field, horizontal magnetic-field, and grounded-earth responses have different transfer functions and noise coupling.

## Candidate native C++ model and falsifiers

Keep the implementation native and separate seeded stages for antenna/receiver transfer, path, coherent modes/fades where warranted, colored receiver noise, sferics, local interference, front-end saturation/recovery, and declared observation loss. Preserve phase where the waveform requires it. Report input/output levels, noise, clipping, fades and outage cause; do not fold antenna loss into path loss. Begin with a fixed reference, moderate waveguide cases, adverse direction/soil/day-night cases, and burst-noise cases. Calibrate replaceable transfer/noise inputs from measurements; if only simulated level is known, label it as such.

Useful falsifiers for oversimplified choices:

* **Gaussian-only noise is falsified** if measured sub-9 kHz noise envelope/APD or burst timing differs materially from a Gaussian process at the selected site/bandwidth, or if reception failures cluster on transient intervals while median PSD is nearly unchanged. ITU explicitly warns lightning noise is non-Gaussian; validate the model with amplitude distribution, impulsive occupancy, and quiet-gap statistics, not only PSD. [VLF04](https://www.itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-r/rec/p/R-REC-P.372-15-202109-I%21%21PDF-E.pdf)
* **Static “night gain” is falsified** if matched path amplitude/phase records vary oppositely by magnetic azimuth, if dawn/dusk phase transitions occur, or if nighttime equatorial westward paths show loss increases. The observed direction and boundary conditions must be retained when the path is known; otherwise treat these as uncertainty branches. [VLF06](https://doi.org/10.1002/jgra.50533) [VLF07](https://doi.org/10.1029/2022JA030742) [VLF12](https://doi.org/10.1029/2024JA033610)
* **Single fixed ground-loss/path-loss values are falsified** by site-specific conductivity or path segments changing the measured amplitude/phase outside the selected tolerance. Keep local ground-system loss separate from distributed propagation ground conductivity. Recent modeling says path descriptions need tens-of-kilometers conductivity segments for certain inversion accuracy, and extremely low conductivity is particularly sensitive. [VLF08](https://doi.org/10.1029/2024RS008150)
* **A generic antenna efficiency/receive-gain parameter is falsified** if measured impedance, effective height/area, orientation, or frequency response differs across the actual top-hat wire/ground installation. At minimum measure input impedance versus frequency and one calibrated field/voltage transfer. Do not assign the high-power base-station efficiencies to a 200 ft structure. [VLF01](https://www.navy-radio.com/ant/vlf-ant-nps-1967.pdf) [VLF02](https://hdl.handle.net/10945/22519)
* **The wrong propagation family is falsified** if attenuation does not follow far-field spreading, phase changes with small separation, or field orientation follows induction. For near-field loop tests, compare field or terminal transfer vs range and angle; for long paths, compare continuous transmitter phase/amplitude and lightning/station statistics. These experiments answer different questions and cannot validate each other. [VLF09](https://doi.org/10.3390/nano15141062)

## Missing inputs before a physically anchored case

A quantitative scenario needs: exact carrier range and occupied bandwidth; the confirmed 60.96 m vertical height; top-hat type, span/radial count and wire size; ground radial or counterpoise layout; soil conductivity/moisture and nearby conductors; transmitter, receiver, or both; measured feed/port impedance and tuning network; transmit power or receiver input noise figure/voltage noise; receiver antenna orientation and height; TX–RX spacing; desired path (direct near field, above-ground ground wave, Earth–ionosphere waveguide, or through-earth electrodes); path length, land/sea and conductivity profile; geographic/magnetic location and date/local solar geometry; receiver bandwidth and clipping limits; and whether operation is continuous or bursty.

Until those inputs exist, an evidence-grounded high-fidelity exercise can compare alternative model structures and controlled levels, but should not call a specific SNR, antenna efficiency, or failure probability “realistic” for the user's unspecified installation.

## Bandwidth feasibility and the limit of small-antenna bounds

For an ideal tuned, lossless radiator inside a sphere of radius $a$, the Chu lower bound for a lowest-order dipole mode at $ka\ll1$ is $Q\gtrsim(ka)^{-3}+(ka)^{-1}$, where $k=2\pi/\lambda$. It is a spherical-mode stored-energy floor, not a predicted impedance curve, loaded Q, or modem bandwidth. The sphere encloses the full antenna source and included feed structure. [VLF14](https://dspace.mit.edu/bitstream/handle/1721.1/4984/RLE-TR-064-04706975.pdf)

As a scale check, an ideal-ground image of a 60.96 m vertical spans 122 m, giving minimum $a\approx61$ m before adding the hat. This gives $ka\approx0.0115$ at 9 kHz and $0.00383$ at 3 kHz, or ideal Q floors near $6.6\times10^5$ and $1.8\times10^7$. A hat or finite counterpoise can enlarge the sphere; an infinite ground plane cannot itself fit in it, so image theory is an explicit idealization. The larger $a$ lowers the bound but certifies no real antenna efficiency or transfer. [VLF14](https://dspace.mit.edu/bitstream/handle/1721.1/4984/RLE-TR-064-04706975.pdf)

Q only approximates fractional matched-VSWR bandwidth for a sufficiently isolated resonance and modest bandwidth/power variation; the chosen reference impedance and accepted-power criterion matter. Yaghjian and Best define this relation for one-port tuned antennas, including lossy cases. A high loss resistance can lower accepted-power Q and make a resonance look broader while also diverting accepted power from radiation. Thus passive matching cannot manufacture transmit gain or received SNR: test radiated efficiency and receiver noise/gain separately from port match. Bode–Fano constrains how broadly a passive network can match a reactive load; a proposed active parametric matching scheme reports simulated bandwidth extension by trading input noise figure, not a free improvement in antenna radiation. [VLF15](https://doi.org/10.1109/TAP.2005.844443) [VLF16](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.11683)

Active, nonlinear, or time-varying systems change assumptions behind passive linear time-invariant bounds and require their own stability, added-power/noise, sideband, and modulation analysis. Mechanical VLF devices are relevant as a distinct antenna family, but available demonstrations are not a bandwidth fix for this vertical: a 2023 magneto-mechano-electric transmitter used a high-Q piezoelectric resonator, showed 10 Hz ASK/FSK tests, used 11.22/11.42 kHz FSK carriers, and placed its receive loop 15 cm away. The paper also reports resonator settling as an ASK-rate limit. This supports near-field proof-of-principle only, not sub-9 kHz range, efficiency or native-modem compatibility. [VLF17](https://doi.org/10.1002/aelm.202300096)

## Evidence limits

Evidence is stronger for high-power transmitters, geophysical receivers, global lightning propagation and through-earth links than for an unspecified-site 60.96 m vertical. Recent work updates noise, path and ground-conductivity findings, but does not replace measurement of the actual antenna/receiver assembly. No 2026 study was used as evidence. No communications authorization, transmission legality, or specific power permission is inferred here.
