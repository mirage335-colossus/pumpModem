## Summary

The modem's simulator default (white Gaussian noise at −164 dBm/Hz, i.e. Fa = 10 dB above kT0) is below the selected P.372 reference outputs evaluated here. It is a numerical reference, not a measured quiet-site floor or a universal bound on all physical antennas. P.372's combined-noise quantile approximation and a literal sum of random component powers give different medians (§4). The latest edition, Rec. ITU-R P.372-18 (09/2026), still carries the 1960s atmospheric-noise maps (CCIR 322-3) and the 1970s US man-made medians unchanged; what changed since 2016 is structure (Parts 1–7, in -14 of 2019), taxonomy (Gaussian / impulsive / single-carrier components) and redrawn figures that no longer show the impulsiveness parameter Vd (both in -15 of 2021), and an indoor model above 80 MHz (-18). The ITU Radio Noise Data Bank collected for a revision holds 90,205 measurements, all from Europe, Japan and the USA, which one analysis finds too sparse and biased for an empirical update. With a lossless reference antenna the median noise density is about −120 to −97 dBm/Hz at 1 MHz by day (quiet rural to city), −114 to −74 dBm/Hz at 1 MHz at night (mid-latitude winter to tropical summer), −140 to −125 dBm/Hz at 10 MHz, and −155 to −138 dBm/Hz at 30 MHz, with decile spreads of 4–19 dB. Post-2000 campaigns disagree: Dutch amateur-site measurements put dense residential areas 7–13 dB above the P.372 residential curve below 4 MHz and 1–9 dB above it at 7–21 MHz, whereas German regulator measurements (2007–2012) lie between 3 dB below and 7 dB above P.372 at 5 and 12 MHz and 5–9 dB below it at 20 MHz with no upward trend, and Norwegian measurements at 30–203 MHz lie on average 5–8 dB below. A new 2024 Antarctic campaign, published as NTIA TR-25-579 in 2025, further demonstrates site/frequency variation: its off-route channel medians put the Black Island receiver site in a quiet-rural class while McMurdo in-town locations range rural–residential and bluff locations mostly residential, with 9.032 MHz on the bluff near residential–urban. That is a small, local campaign, not a revised global curve. The campaigns do not hold site selection, antenna factor, estimator, signal removal, geography or era fixed; those differences prevent attributing disagreement to a unique cause. The noise is impulsive in 0.25–3 kHz (Vd 4–11 dB; the envelope median lies 8–20 dB below the rms). Extrapolating the 1985 bandwidth conversion below its 6 Hz data limit gives about 1.1 dB Vd at 1 Hz for HF and MF winter-day cases, near the Gaussian Vd reference, but this does not establish Gaussian tails or independent samples; the same extrapolation leaves MF summer-afternoon noise at Vd ≈ 3–4.6 dB. The original 1962 paper includes a measured 0.64-Hz APD but acknowledges weak validation of its bandwidth transformation; neither that marginal nor the 1985 formula supplies temporal covariance. Below a hertz, slow power variation, carriers and spectral lines matter, while lightning-flash correlation can persist across the filter's integration memory. At HF the practical floor is often other signals; the structure of the Laycock–Gott congestion model was verified but its coefficient tables were not obtained. Measured occupancy was found for 1 kHz and 3 kHz channels, not for 250 Hz slots: in one Belgian example (November 2014) 3 kHz channels at 2–9 MHz were unavailable about a third of the time on average (8–66 % by hour and frequency), those at 16–29 MHz about 4 %.

## Physical mechanisms

**Three external sources plus interference.** P.372 treats (i) atmospheric noise from lightning, (ii) man-made noise, (iii) galactic noise, and explicitly excludes signals of radio services and single identifiable sources (P.372-18 §1.1 Notes 3–5) [S, read]. For a receiver the fourth component, co-channel and adjacent-channel signals, is often the largest at HF (Rep. ITU-R SM.2155 §3 a; SM.2158-3 Annex 1) [S, read].

**Atmospheric noise.** Lightning in the world's thunderstorm regions radiates impulses whose energy falls steeply with frequency; what reaches a site is set by propagation. At MF by day D-region absorption removes distant storms, so the 1 MHz level is low (10–50 dB above kT0) unless storms are local; at night sky-wave propagation brings in continental and tropical storm centres and the 1 MHz level rises by 10–30 dB (about 30 dB in winter at mid-latitudes, map readings in §2). Around 10–15 MHz the daytime curves show a secondary maximum (30–45 dB) from long-distance F-region propagation; above about 20 MHz the atmospheric component collapses (P.372-18 Figs 13b–36b) [S, read]. The CCIR maps describe "average background" noise with the effects of local thunderstorms removed from the data (NTIA Report 85-173, p. 174) [T, read]; nearby storms are therefore outside the model.

**Man-made noise.** Aggregated unintended radiation: historically power lines (dominant "at and below the middle of the HF band") and ignition (dominant above about 10 MHz) (OT Report 74-38 §4.1.2, §4.2) [T, read]; today switch-mode power supplies, LED drivers, PLT/VDSL, photovoltaic optimisers and inverters, and wireless power transfer (P.372-18 §1.1; SM.2158-3; SM.2451) [S, read]. Fockens, Zwamborn & Leferink's 2019 Netherlands campaign is now available in full: 54 amateur premises plus five other sites, 470 kHz–50.5 MHz, with a 500-Hz measurement bandwidth converted to 2700 Hz under an explicit Gaussian assumption. The paper separates narrowband EMI (≤500 Hz), broadband EMI, specific-source noise, cumulative MMN and radio-background noise; its daytime/site campaign found statistically significant increases over P.372-13, highest in dense city locations (14 dB mean and peaks over 20 dB), and itself urges equivalent measurements elsewhere rather than asserting a universal country effect [P, read]. Fockens & Leferink 2020 is also now full-text accessible: it correlates measurements at those 54 locations with dwelling counts in annuli out to 500 m; the greatest correlation is within 200–300 m, and it supports many weak residential sources rather than a single nearby emitter. The result is a spatially scoped association, not a universal source radius or a propagation cutoff [P, read]. P.372-15 onward reinterprets the "quiet rural" curve as man-made noise reflected by the ionosphere, observed below the extraordinary-wave critical frequency fxF2, giving way to galactic noise above it (P.372-18 §6.1.1) [S, read].

**What follow-up Dutch measurements add.** The full 2022 cumulation paper models residences as collections of independent sources coupled to wiring/mains radiation and ground-wave propagation, then compares the model with Dutch measurements. Its analytical spatial accumulation assumes perfect source-to-mains matching and uses a generalized measured apparent-isotropic gain; these assumptions are model inputs, not universal properties of household equipment. The same authors' 2022 propagation experiment used ten frequencies from 1.8 to 28 MHz and three deliberately distinct paths (open farm road, airstrip, decommissioned runway), separating direct and ground-reflected waves. It makes the important point that buildings/ground and geometry change the field along the path, and does not supply one transferable scalar attenuation law for arbitrary clutter. Fockens's 2023 University of Twente thesis is 212 pages and covers 500 kHz–50 MHz and 59 sites. The institutional record/abstract is accessible, but the linked 106-MB full PDF returned HTTP 403 on the renewed official-repository attempt; unique thesis synthesis/tables remain unverified and no additional numeric results are attributed to it [P/T, abstract/record].

**Galactic noise.** Synchrotron emission of the Galaxy, Fam = 52 − 23 log f(MHz); it is not observed below foF2 and is reduced up to about 3·foF2 (P.372-18 §4.1) [S, read]. Whole-sky average varies ±2 dB; a narrow beam at the galactic pole sees 3 dB less (P.372-18 Part 2) [S, read]. A Radio JOVE observing note confirms qualitatively that at 20–22 MHz the daily "galactic hump" is easily seen at night and is normally hidden by day except near solar minimum [G, read].

**Why impulsiveness depends on bandwidth.** A filter of bandwidth B responds to an isolated impulse with peak voltage ∝ B and duration ≈ 1/B, while Gaussian noise voltage grows as √B. Rec. ITU-R SM.1753-2 Table 2 states the scaling rules used by monitoring services: Gaussian-noise power ∝ B, impulsive-noise "spectral power level rises with square of bandwidth", single-carrier level independent of B [S, read]. Narrowing the filter can smooth individual pulse waveforms, but does not by itself make the output Gaussian: clustered arrivals, shared flash/storm activity, discrete tones, and slow power modulation can remain non-Gaussian or correlated over the observation window (see the lightning evidence below). Under an isolated-impulse and flat Gaussian-background comparison, relative to the Gaussian floor impulses lose 10 log(B1/B2) dB while carriers gain the same amount. [E] Going from 3 kHz to 1 Hz therefore moves impulses down by about 35 dB and steady carriers up by about 35 dB relative to the noise in the filter; this comparison alone does not establish which component dominates a particular 0.06–0.6 Hz mode.

**Units and conversions.** Pn (dBW) = Fa + 10 log b − 204 (P.372-18 eq. 6), so N0 (dBm/Hz) = Fa − 174. R.m.s. field strength in bandwidth b: En (dBµV/m) = Fa + 20 log f(MHz) + 10 log b − 95.5 for a short vertical monopole over perfect ground (eq. 7) and −96.8 for an isotropic antenna in free space (eq. 8) [S, read]. [E] Check: monopole effective height h/2 and radiation resistance 40π²(h/λ)² give E² = 640π² p/λ², i.e. the constant −204 + 38.0 − 49.5 + 120 = −95.5; the same method gives −98.9 for a free-space half-wave dipole of gain 1.64, a separate antenna assumption. P.341-7 Annex 3 eqs. (10), (12), (13) confirms the near-surface receive gain 0.75 for the total vertical field, despite transmit directivity 3; substituting transmit gain into the receiving-field conversion would introduce 6.02 dB. For measurements with a calibrated antenna: Fa = P(dBm) + AF − 20 log f − 10 log b + 202.5 (SM.1753-2 eq. 10) [S, read]. External noise only defines C/N0 if it exceeds receiver noise after antenna losses; with an electrically small lossy antenna signal and external noise are attenuated together.

### 4. Combination rule (P.372-18 §7.1, eqs 31–39)

The standard is a **quantile approximation**, not an exact distribution of the sum. NTIA 87-212 §4, pp. 30–33, now read from the official report, explicitly explains that a sum of lognormal powers is not lognormal, that convolution would give the distribution, and that the approximation is especially directed at the upper/10%-exceedance region. It corrects the older treatment of median powers as means. Its 1987 derivation predates the later large-deviation modification; Greg Hand's first-party [2007 modification note](https://www.greg-hand.com/noise/) records the undesirable cases and the subsequent proposal/software change. The note is provenance, not a normative definition.

For `c=10/ln(10)`, a side-specific normal-in-dB approximation uses `sigma_i=D_i/1.282`, `alpha_i=exp(Fa_i/c+sigma_i²/(2c²))`, `alpha=sum(alpha_i)`, `beta=sum(alpha_i² expm1(sigma_i²/c²))`, and `gamma=sum(exp(Fa_i/c))`. Ordinarily `sigma_T=c sqrt(log1p(beta/alpha²))`; when any component deviation on that side exceeds 12 dB the v14.3 code **replaces** this with `c sqrt(2 ln(alpha/gamma))`. This is not a maximum operation. Each side gives `Fam_side=c ln(alpha)−sigma_T²/(2c)`; the library returns the smaller upper/lower result and its separately computed deciles. The substitution makes that side equal the plain sum of median powers. Neither operation proves that value is the physical median.

**Independent discriminator.** For nonnegative powers, `sum(X_i)>=X_j` in every realization, so `P(sum(X_i)<=x)<=P(X_j<=x)` and the continuous physical median cannot be below a component median, under any dependence. ### 6. Time structure

- Man-made envelopes appear uncorrelated for lags under about 100 ms in most samples, but pulse-spacing distributions at high thresholds are not exponential, i.e. arrivals are not Poisson and the process is correlated over longer times (OT 74-38 §4.3; examples at 48, 102, 250 MHz in 4 kHz) [T, read].
- Rep. SM.2155 treats HF impulsive noise as bursts of very short pulses, characterised by level density (dBµV/MHz), burst length, repetition time and total burst time. Its examples show a dominant source with a 20 ms repetition (the 50 Hz mains period, although the report does not name the cause; the peaks at multiples of 20 ms arise from its counting method), total burst time 0.63 % in a "typical" record and 10.49 % in another (Figs 13, 14, 30) [S, read]. The sampling requirement (10 kHz bandwidth, ≥ 20 kHz sampling, 1 s records) limits detectable repetition rates to ≥ 2 Hz.
- MITRE wideband HF records (42 one-second complex I/Q records, 1.024-MS/s sampling and 800-kHz equivalent RF bandwidth, Bedford MA, March 1989, 3–30 MHz, at various times of day) were examined by Lemmon & Behm [T, Part I read; Part II selected methods/results read]. Part I (NTIA 91-277) proposed Gaussian noise + narrowband sine-wave interferers + filtered impulses, with Hall-family amplitude distributions; one modeled record included 50 filtered impulses. Part II (NTIA 93-293, pp. 2–5, 80–85) compared higher-order statistics for five selected cases (centers 5.936, 19.29, 13.666 twice and 23.862 MHz; Table 1 p. 11), not a population sample. It found uniform impulse arrival times inadequate: a burst model with within-burst arrivals and approximately 450–550-µs burst spacing generated qualitative features missing from uniform arrivals. The especially impulsive case showed measured widths around 2–10 µs, spacings on the order of ≤10 µs and a finite bump near 500 µs; these are one 23.862-MHz case's observed features, not a transferable atmosphere parameter set. The report argues the impulses were man-made because burst widths/spacings differ from atmospheric lightning, identifies nonstationarity and says its comparisons are qualitative; no atmospheric waveform was included [T, selected methods/results visually read from the full official scanned PDF]. Lemmon's 2000 URSI proceedings paper later addressed atmospheric HF noise separately; limits are summarized under waveform models [P, proceedings read].
- Slow variation: hour-to-hour and day-to-day deciles in §2; for man-made noise P.372's "variation with time" is interpreted by FFI, from the CCIR source, as the within-hour spread. OT 74-38 supports this: its Table 2 is titled "Expected variation, man-made radio noise levels, about the median value for a location, within the hour" [T, read], and its averages over 0.25–102 MHz agree with P.372 Table 2 to within about 0.3 dB [E, from the OCR text]. FFI measured only +0.8/−0.3 dB over 28 minutes and about 2 dB over 11.5 hours at a residential VHF site (FFI 16/00869 §4.3) [T, read], against 10.6/5.3 dB in P.372. A US study found business-area noise following working hours (NTIA TR-02-390 §5) [T, read].
- Lightning: in records of a representative cloud-to-ground flash at 3, 30 and 300 MHz (300 kHz bandwidth, Florida), the radiation is a sequence of many discrete impulses spread over about a second; the stepped leader and return strokes give large impulses, but most impulses are probably from intracloud processes, and a given impulse tends to appear at all frequencies. That review's composite amplitude spectra peak near 5 kHz and approximately follow 1/f over part of the range; source-specific return strokes and narrow bipolar events have materially different high-frequency spectra, as the renewed primary evidence below shows. A 250 Hz filter has an impulse response of about 0.25 s, so it merges multiple impulses from that flash (Le Vine, NASA TM-87788, 1986) [T, read]. This merging alone does not establish a Gaussian output: hundreds of hours of measured 10 Hz–60 kHz atmospheric noise show sferic interarrival clustering consistent with a clustered-Poisson model, and broadband VLF sferic bursts can last hundreds of milliseconds to 3 s (Chrissan et al. 2003; Marshall et al. 2007) [P, read]. A narrow filter can smooth within-flash waveforms while flash/storm activity and burst arrivals remain correlated across its memory; the measured distribution must be checked at the actual bandwidth and averaging interval. The hundreds-of-hours timing evidence is 10 Hz–60 kHz, not a validated 3–30 MHz HF burst-rate model. Separately, a three-year Suizhou VLF study found minute-sampled 4–27 kHz band levels correlated with lightning within roughly 3,500 km and reported short-term variation not represented by the hourly CCIR model; those site, bands and sampling do not set HF or sub-Hz parameters (Gu et al. 2021) [P, read].

### 8. Interference and occupancy at HF

**Congestion model (Gott, Laycock and co-workers; Economou et al. 2005).** Congestion Qk is the probability that a randomly chosen channel in ITU allocation k exceeds a field-strength threshold E. Qk = 1/(1 + e^(−yk)), yk = αk + Bk·E, Bk = B0 + B1 fk + B2 fk²; 95 allocations from 1.606 to 30 MHz; αk depends on filter bandwidth, sunspot number, week of year and location; 142 coefficients for "stable day" and 139 for "stable night" (three-hour windows at local noon and midnight); built from weekly measurements in northern Europe with a calibrated low-angle monopole (described in Mendieta-Otero et al. 2014) [P, secondary; primary not obtained]. Typical values quoted there: Bk ≈ −0.1 per dB, αk between −16 and −9; worked case (amateur allocation, Las Palmas, June 2011, midday) Bk = −0.0849, αk = −10.81. [E] With that slope the fraction of channels exceeding the median channel level by 10, 20, 30, 40, 50 dB is 30 %, 15.5 %, 7 %, 3 %, 1.4 %: channel levels spread over tens of dB above the floor. Tabulated model congestion by band, time and bandwidth was not obtained, and no figure specific to 250 Hz slots was found.

**Measured occupancy, 2012–2015.** In Cyprus, occupancy was measured hourly as the fraction of 1 kHz channels in each ITU allocation whose rms level exceeded a field-strength threshold, for low-elevation signals, with a 100 ms dwell (Haralambous & Mostafa 2015) [G, read]. At a threshold of 20 dBµV/m, hourly congestion in the 5.95–6.2 MHz broadcast allocation spread from nearly 0 to 1 between May 2012 and May 2013, with many hours at 0.8–1.0; in 25.67–26.1 MHz it stayed below about 0.1 in mid-2012 and reached 0.2–1 in winter 2012/13 [G, fig. reading]. Allocations in the lower part of the band peak at night, those in the upper part by day, and the lower part is busier in winter [G, read]. [E] 20 dBµV/m in 1 kHz is about 24 dB above P.372 rural noise at 6 MHz. The HFIA working group swept 2–30 MHz every minute with a Perseus SDR and an active whip, summed 488 Hz bins into 3 kHz channels, and counted a channel as occupied when it exceeded by 10 dB a floor taken from the ten lowest channels in each MHz; a channel counts as available for a minute only if it stays free for at least two minutes (Furman, Nieto & Batts 2015) [G, read]. Their Belgian example (November 2014) tabulates this availability by hour and MHz: [E, from the table] 0.68 on average at 2–9 MHz (0.34–0.92 by hour and frequency), 0.85 at 10–15 MHz (0.71 around midday) and 0.96 at 16–29 MHz (1.00 at night, 0.89 around midday). These results remain 3 kHz-channel statistics: they cannot be divided by 12 or otherwise converted into a general 250 Hz-slot occupancy bound. A toy estimate from uniformly placed single narrow emitters would require matched thresholds and observation windows, and would not cover wideband or multiple interferers. Mostafa, Tsolaki & Haralambous's *HF Spectral Occupancy Time Series Models Over the Eastern Mediterranean Region* is now identity-verified as *IEEE Trans. EMC*59(1),240–248,2017, DOI10.1109/TEMC.2016.2606130, by the [author institution](https://cyirg.frederick.ac.cy/monitoringmodelling-and-prediction-of-hf-spectral-occupancy-over-cyprus/). Its numerical coefficients/body were not exposed by the inspected DOI/author routes; it supplies no selected temporal coefficient here.

**Full-text 2022 Cyprus interference update.** Constantinides, Haralambous, Papadopoulos & Makrominas, *Models of HF Interference over Cyprus*, Appl. The one-year low-elevation dataset uses the calibrated monopole component of an HE016 antenna into an R&S EM 510; 1 kHz filter readings are spaced 2 kHz across 1.606–30.000 MHz, with 100 ms dwell. For each of 95 ITU allocations, congestion Q is the fraction of sampled channels above an explicitly selected incident-field threshold (−20 to 45 dBµV/m in 5 dB steps); the paper compares logit regression and a feed-forward neural network. The stated domain is typical allocation congestion over Cyprus during benign/quiet geomagnetic activity, with frequency, field threshold, allocation/user type, season and local time as predictors. **Separate Cyprus noise-characteristics record.** Haralambous, Constantinides & Paul, *HF Noise Characteristics over Cyprus*, Remote Sens. 14(23), 6040, DOI 10.3390/rs14236040 [P, read]. ### 10. Special and worst cases

- **Local thunderstorms:** excluded from the maps [T, read]. P.372's 0.5 %-of-hours curve (101 dB at 1 MHz, 41 dB at 10 MHz) plus the upper decile is the nearest documented bound; no verified near-storm HF spectral densities were obtained.

The timing of gap discharges within the mains cycle was not verified.
- **Precipitation static and corona on the receiving antenna:** no source was obtained, and no level is given here.
- **Solar radio bursts:** quiet-Sun brightness about 10⁶ K at 50–200 MHz with "large increases" when disturbed (P.372-18 §4.1) [S, read]; individual bursts "usually last for about half a minute" and are stronger than Jupiter's noise storms (Radio JOVE; no frequency stated) [G, read]. [E] A flux density S gives Ta = S·Gλ²/(8πk); for G = 1.5 at 30 MHz, 10⁻¹⁹ W m⁻² Hz⁻¹ (1 000 sfu) gives Fa ≈ 22 dB, 10⁻¹⁷ gives 42 dB, 10⁻¹⁶ gives 52 dB, against 18 dB galactic. ## How the literature models or simulates this

- **Level models.** P.372 / CCIR 322-3 with numerical maps (Fourier coefficients in NTIA 85-173) and the reference implementation in ITU-R-HF (GitHub release v14.3 cited in P.372-18) [S/T, read]; the IONCAP/VOACAP noise routine of NTIA 87-212 [T]. Validated domain: seasonal/4-hour medians and deciles from a 16-station network (about nine years of data) plus ten Soviet stations, 13 kHz–20 MHz, 200 Hz bandwidth, short vertical monopole. Known limits: local storms removed; sparse southern-hemisphere data; no directionality or polarisation (85-173 §5); man-made medians from US data of 1966–1971 (OT 74-38) [T, read]. Coleman (2002), now read in the original, supplies a direction-sensitive alternative with both successful and unsuccessful comparisons (below). Kotaki (1984) remains a contextual satellite-derived predecessor cited in 85-173, not a selected coefficient source.
- **First-order amplitude models.** CCIR three-section APD indexed by Vd (reproduces the measured family; says nothing about time structure). Hall (1966) model, used by Lemmon & Behm for interferer and impulse amplitudes [T, read]. Middleton Class A (narrowband relative to the receiver) and Class B (broadband) canonical models; P.2089 and SM.1753 use the simpler Gaussian plus Poisson impulses with a Weibull power law [S, read]. Giesbrecht et al. (2006) fit Bi-Kappa to selected 4-kHz HF-noise samples from one Adelaide site and sunrise period; fit quality varies materially by time and tail (e.g. the 7:00 case fit the central ±100 µV/m region while observed standard deviation was 100 µV/m against fitted σ=20 µV/m; by 7:45 fit σ matched the observed 46 µV/m). The 2008 Part II/thesis add selected 153.6-kHz Swan Reach records from a different rural site and use threshold-based signal rejection; the thesis explicitly notes that the threshold is subjective, a wide channel may contain multiple allocations, and sub-threshold signals can remain. Their modified Bi-Kappa fit is therefore a dataset-conditioned candidate, not a universal HF law. They explicitly allow Gaussian as an initial approximation, while noting possible inaccuracy [P, full texts read]. Alpha-stable and Gaussian-mixture models are signal-processing surrogates. Fitted Class B parameters for MF atmospheric noise appear in Middleton's OT Report 76-86 (1976, Figs 2.4 and 2.7): envelope APDs measured at 500 kHz in a 4 kHz bandwidth (near Laramie, Wyoming, February 1970, night; Boulder, Colorado, July 1969, afternoon; data of Espeland & Spaulding 1970), fitted with α = 1.2 and 1.0 and Aα = 1.0, and A_B = 0.005, Γ′_B = 0.008 for the second [T, read; values from figure annotations in OCR text]. No fitted Class A or alpha-stable parameters for MF/HF atmospheric noise were obtained; Middleton's Class A example for power-line interference (200 kHz, 6 kHz bandwidth, Denver 1972, same report) gives its parameters only in a figure that the text extraction does not reproduce.
- **Waveform models.** Lemmon & Behm's NTIA 91-277/93-293 model combines Gaussian noise, sinusoidal narrowband interferers and band-limited impulses with Hall-family amplitude distributions. Part II develops evidence that correlated burst arrival structure is needed for selected one-second Bedford records: a simple uniform-arrival model missed pulse-width/spacing details, while a burst process with approx. 450–550 µs between bursts and about 6.25 impulses per burst produced qualitative agreement. Part II explicitly limits itself to five cases, nonstationarity is present, comparisons are qualitative, and impulse events were judged man-made; no atmospheric waveform is provided. Exact 93-293 method/result locations: model and data scope pp. 2–5; record/case definition and autocorrelation pp. 7–12; pulse distributions/model comparison pp. 80–83; conclusions pp. 84–85 [T, scanned full text read]. Lemmon's 2000 URSI proceedings paper, “Wideband Model of HF Atmospheric Radio Noise,” describes a distinct 3–30 MHz atmospheric model whose cumulative envelope distributions match a large database, relates model parameters to Vd and mean noise power at lossless short-monopole terminals under NTIA 85-173/CCIR conventions, and includes temporal correlation/burst structure. Its Vd bandwidth dependence is only qualitatively similar to limited data; the proceedings text obtained here does not establish a parameter set for an arbitrary channel/site. The closely related 2001 Radio Science article is still represented here by its abstract, not full text [P, proceedings full text; journal abstract]. Markov-modulated (“Markov–Middleton”, Gilbert–Elliott-type) burst models exist in the power-line literature; no HF fit was found.
- **Interference models.** Laycock–Gott congestion (logistic in threshold), UK/northern Europe, extended to a sunspot cycle (Economou et al. 2005) and to 24 h neural-network forms (Haralambous & Papadopoulos 2009) [P, secondary]; Mendieta-Otero et al. (2014) turn it into a waveform simulator for the amateur allocations of 1.606–30 MHz with Poisson arrivals: their contest example takes 6.68 arrivals per second, the ARRL Field Day 2011 average QSO rate over 24 h spread over the amateur bands, and exponential durations of mean 10 s; they state that no comparison has yet decided whether their model or a narrowband sine-wave model fits reality better [P, read].
- **Measurement standards.** SM.1753-2 (types A/B/C; rms; 20 % method; rms + 13 dB impulse threshold; 100 Hz and 10 kHz bandwidths below 30 MHz), SM.2155 (HF practice, two-site correlation to remove sky-wave impulses), SM.2093 (indoor, above 30 MHz), P.2089 (Weibull analysis) [S, read].

## What a high-fidelity simulation would need

These are options with their limits, not a design; better approaches may exist.

1. **A level process rather than a constant.** Inputs: frequency, site category, location, season, local time, foF2. Median and deciles from P.372 (preferably computed with the reference code rather than read from figures), drawn per hour from split log-normal distributions. Limits: atmospheric deciles describe the spread of hourly values and man-made time deciles a within-hour spread (OT 74-38), but neither gives rates or correlation times; modern man-made medians are disputed by ±10 dB; an explicit site-offset parameter (−10 to +15 dB relative to P.372) is more honest than a category label.
2. **Component structure.** Sum of (a) a Gaussian background where supported, (b) an impulsive process, (c) discrete carriers and lines, (d) modulated co-channel signals, and (e) swept signals. Each scales differently with bandwidth, so they must be generated at the front-end bandwidth and passed through the modelled receiver, including AGC or limiter. Equal-power Gaussian replacement preserves only a second-moment reference for a linear path; it does not preserve APD tails, burst correlation, threshold exceedance or coherent-decoder error rates. Use it as a distributional approximation only after those observables have been checked for the stated bandwidth and time window.
3. **Impulsive process options.** (i) Envelope drawn to match the CCIR APD for a target Vd at 3 kHz: simple, matches first-order statistics only. (ii) Filtered-impulse train with clustered arrivals (Lemmon–Behm type) or a Markov-modulated Class A source: reproduces bursts; parameters for MF/HF are poorly constrained. (iii) Mains-locked burst trains (100/120 Hz) for power-line and converter noise. (iv) Replay of recorded wideband IQ: highest fidelity for one site and hour, no generality.
4. **Slow modulation of the Gaussian floor** (seconds to hours): storm passages, appliance switching, working-hours cycles, PV daylight cycle. For 0.06–0.6 Hz modes non-stationarity and potentially impulsive or correlated structure are both candidate departures from the white Gaussian assumption; their relative importance is not established by the cited measurements; there is almost no published statistical model for it.
5. **Tonal environment.** Carriers at broadcast rasters (5 kHz HF, 9/10 kHz MF) with sidebands; equipment lines on a comb with slow drift and mains sidebands; WPT harmonics. Line widths, drift rates and stability matter at sub-hertz resolution and are not documented in the sources read for this chapter.
6. **Occupancy.** A congestion-type draw of interferer levels per slot with on/off durations; swept radars with documented sweep parameters.
7. **Observables to validate against.** Fa (rms in a clear channel) by hour; APD and Vd at two or more bandwidths; pulse-duration and pulse-spacing distributions; burst-time percentage; spectrum of the envelope (to expose 100/120 Hz); spectrograms at about 1 Hz resolution over hours; fraction of slots above threshold versus threshold.

## Contradictions, open questions, and things not found

- **Has man-made HF noise risen?** Dutch amateur-site data: +7 to +13 dB in dense residential areas below 4 MHz and +1 to +9 dB at 7–21 MHz, but about equal to P.372 in the sparsest residential class and slightly below it in rural areas [G; P, read]. German regulator data: between 3 dB below and 7 dB above P.372 at 5 and 12 MHz, 5–9 dB below at 20 MHz (stated uncertainty ±5 dB), no trend 2008–2012 [S]. Norway (30–203 MHz): on average 4.7–7.9 dB lower by category, 6.5–11 dB at 30.45 MHz [T]. Canada 1993: equal or lower, buried cables quieter [secondary]. Sweden: lower except in urban areas and Stockholm, up to 15 dB higher [secondary]. SM.2158-3 concludes no major change; SM.2451 notes some higher city/residential measurements; P.372-18 keeps the 1974 values. P.372-13 and -14 described the 2006–2011 European and Japanese measurements as having "generally confirmed" the figures, although at 35–37 MHz they lie 6–13 dB below the city, residential and rural lines [S; E]. The ITU data bank meant for a revision holds no data from outside Europe, Japan and the USA and is dominated by Japanese measurements and German locations (Witvliet 2024) [P, read]; Fockens et al. 2019 reports a significant increase in this Dutch campaign; the 2023 thesis abstract additionally says generic conducted mains-port limits do not explain it, but thesis tables remain unobtained [P, read; T, abstract]. The source-conditioned matrix below reconciles the meanings of the estimators without identifying a unique cause for the regional differences. Contributing factors identified in the sources: (a) site classes are not comparable (addresses within 100 m versus regulator-chosen sites, including safety-service stations); (b) estimators differ: true rms, rms of the lowest 20 % with correction, the 37 % APD point of the Gaussian part (several dB below true rms when impulses are strong, FFI §3.2), median of 500 Hz samples, average detector with correction; (c) antenna factor of short or active antennas near ground (±5 dB stated in SM.2155); (d) treatment of narrowband signals and single sources, which P.372 excludes by definition but which receivers experience; (e) time of day (noon measurements suppress sky-wave but also miss evening domestic activity). CEPT's 2002 campaign was "ambiguous" and could not reproduce the 1970s method (SM.2158-3) [S, read].

### Directional and event-specific alternatives (S17–S19)

Coleman2002's [original directional model](https://digital.library.adelaide.edu.au/server/api/core/bitstreams/0d4a196e-f63b-4e3a-a423-925cf5271042/content), §§2–4, traces global lightning through the ionosphere then integrates against the receiving antenna pattern. It distinguishes local intermittent noise from distant background and explicitly limits its climatological inputs to typical behavior. Its Table1 has both improvements and failures: at2.5MHz Sweden the directional/CCIR/observed values are57/51/52dB, whereas Singapore gives63/68/61dB. It is not uniformly better. The assumed ground (`sigma=.01S/m,epsilon_r=15`) and low-elevation antenna response can change predictions by several dB. This supplies an implementable **directional power** alternative, not pulse arrivals. Cane1979's original polar-region measurements/model (§§2–7) verify low-frequency turnover and direction limits; its model is not suitable for a resolved Galactic-plane beam. Dulk2001 remains a secondary parameterization here because the original publisher PDF returned403; do not promote the1.3whole-sky correction to a universally measured constant.

[Pederick & Cervera2016](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/2015RS005842), §§3–5, supplies a more recent directional-noise alternative distinct from their scheduled-interference paper. It fits lightning radiated-power coefficients to eight representative months in2008/2012 at Longreach, 5–30MHz and20-minute model steps, with NEC antenna/mismatch corrections. Training RMS difference is4.2dB versus4.9dB for P.372. Transfer is assessed at Laverton,2160km away, including sixteen beams and a whip; that geographic check is stronger than a same-site fit. However, the plotted comparison uses monthly medians, while the modeled Galactic transit is for15June; temporal smoothing differs. IRI omits sporadicE, and the model represents climatological directional power rather than storm pulses or modem-band covariance. Its SiMIAN absorption changes low-HF results materially relative to the older empirical absorption. The15February2016 correction concerns an omitted affiliation, not physics. This is a justified candidate when antenna direction and background power matter; it does not calibrate N2 burst timing or solve low-HF disturbed conditions.

A consequential contrary mechanism is supported by [Berngardt et al.2022](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2021RS007338), §§2–3: four **poleward-looking** radars in Canada, USA, Kerguelen and Russia are modeled with distributed anthropogenic sources focused near the skip-zone boundary, rather than a universal tropical-lightning background. Sources are assumed homogeneous, stationary and incoherent; IRI-2012/NRLMSISE-00 and antenna patterns determine relative seasonal/diurnal power. The2013–2014 comparisons normalize yearly maxima and match truncated dynamic ranges, so reported agreement is not independent absolute-level calibration. Low measured levels stop following predicted absorption; instrument sensitivity, Galactic noise and back-lobe lightning remain possible causes. Fixed-frequency24-hour SAS/EKB records support interpretation more strongly than BKS/KER's partial-time measurements. This preserves a physical source/propagation/antenna alternative to the Australian model and a discriminator: directional, calibrated records with floor/censoring measurements. A quiet-day noise reduction alone is not a wanted-link loss measurement or a unique diagnosis of its cause.

Fernandez et al. **2015** (uploaded2025), §§2–7, measures a specific switching event with source-off/source-on records, a calibrated receiver/passive rod, sample detector and explicit burst-merging procedure. Its classroom example switches eight fluorescent tubes at630/1620kHz,8001samples/s,4–5s records. This is useful MF event evidence; it is not a modern PV/EV population. Its rms+13dB threshold and burst rules are detection conventions. For an ideal Rayleigh envelope the probability above rms+13dB is `exp(−10^1.3)≈2.16e−9`, not zero: Gaussian noise has no finite hard crest factor. Background contamination, finite sampling and amplitude/duty estimation remain relevant. Source-specific PV/EV/SMPS timing/amplitude is therefore an explicit sensitivity or measurement input, never inferred from this example.

Brown & Flagg's [2018 precipitation-static measurement](https://radiojove.net/SUG/Pubs/A%20Measurement%20of%20Precipitation%20Static%2C%20Brown%20%28SUG%2C%202018%29.pdf) gives a direct20.1MHz event example: a TFD array steered35°south,7-min before/during intervals,4203points each, and noise-source/attenuator matching imply about11.5dB increase.
