**Summary.** The Watterson model (tapped delay line, one tap per mode, independent zero-mean complex-Gaussian tap gains with Gaussian or double-Gaussian Doppler spectra) was validated on three samples of 10–13 minutes (36 minutes in total) selected as the most nearly stationary parts of about 17 hours recorded on one 1294 km mid-latitude path in November 1967, at 5.864 and 9.259 MHz, with measured spreads of only 0.005–0.4 Hz. Its authors limited it to bandwidths of about one quarter of the reciprocal of the within-mode time spread (2.5–12 kHz) and to intervals of about 15 minutes; ten years later Watterson restricted the validated model to a quiet ionosphere, which he called the usual case. Every test standard read (ITU-R F.520-2, suppressed in 2000; ITU-R F.1487, in force; MIL-STD-188-110C, -110D and -141B; DRM/ETSI ES 201 980 and ITU-R BS.1615-3) reuses that structure, mostly as two equal-power Rayleigh paths with white Gaussian noise of fixed power and no variation of the mean level, and extends it to 10–30 Hz spreads, to 24 kHz (MIL-STD-188-110C) and to 48 kHz (MIL-STD-188-110D) without citing new validation. Wideband and physics-based alternatives exist. The ITS work includes visual measured-scattering comparisons; Gherm et al. (2005) compare analytical model moments with their own simulated realizations, which is numerical consistency rather than field validation; HiCIRF cites sponsor validation not obtained here. Heitmann et al. (2018) match measured disturbance signatures qualitatively, while explicitly identifying limitations of those matches. Ray tracing through climatological ionospheres predicts mode structure, delay, absorption and Doppler shift, but not Doppler spread unless an irregularity model is added. Test-duration rules disagree: F.1487 asks for 3000 reciprocal Doppler spreads, whereas a 2011 Harris study found 4–5 hours necessary at 1 Hz. The nearest thing to a non-stationary generator located is Angling's replay simulator (2001 thesis), driven by high-latitude soundings taken every 10 minutes; it was checked against existing simulators, not against on-air modem results. No validated event-driven (flare, travelling disturbance, storm, MUF transit) generator, no machine-learning HF channel model validated against measurements, and no public STANAG test matrix were found. Published simulator-versus-air evidence is limited to one qualitative grey-literature comparison (Isode) and to availability studies that combine simulator characterisations of modems with measured channel statistics.

Evidence grades: [S] standard, [P] research paper or preprint (peer-review status checked separately), [T] technical report, [B] book, [G] grey, [E] this chapter's own derivation. Access status is given in the reference list ("read", "abstract", "secondary", "bibliographic only").

## Physical mechanisms

**What the models try to represent.** An HF skywave signal arrives by a small number of modes (1E, 1F, 2F, mixed "M" modes), each with low and high rays and with ordinary (O) and extraordinary (X) magneto-ionic components. Differences in group path give differential delays of up to several milliseconds; slow changes of layer height or density give each component its own Doppler shift; irregularities ("turbulence") give each component a Doppler spread and a small spread in delay (F.1487 Annex 1 §1 [S]; Watterson 1979, NTIA Report 79-29 §4.1, pp. 35–39 [T]). Watterson (1979, p. 37–39) separates a *quiet* ionosphere, in which turbulence spreads each mode in frequency but not significantly in time so that modes are "essentially discrete in time", from *spread-F*, in which each mode becomes a continuum of faster-fading paths. He gives shifts and spreads of about 0.01–1 Hz for the quiet case and "up to 10 Hz or more" for spread-F (p. 39) [T].

**Model structure (Watterson / "Gaussian scatter").** The channel is H(f,t) = Σᵢ Gᵢ(t)·exp(−j 2πτᵢf), with a few unequally spaced taps τᵢ, one per mode. Each tap gain is Gᵢ(t) = G̃ᵢₐ(t)·exp(j 2πνᵢₐt) + G̃ᵢᵦ(t)·exp(j 2πνᵢᵦt), where the two terms are independent zero-mean complex-Gaussian (Rayleigh-amplitude) processes for the two magneto-ionic components. Each has a Gaussian power spectrum with its own power, shift ν and standard deviation σ; the "frequency spread" is 2σ (ESSA ERL 112-ITS 80, App. E eq. 159–166, pp. 111–114 [T]; F.1487 Annex 1 eq. 1–2 [S]). Six numbers therefore describe one tap. One Gaussian term is enough when the O and X components have nearly equal shift and spread; two separate taps are needed when their delays differ appreciably (F.1487 Annex 1 §2 [S]).

**Why the discrete-tap model has a bandwidth limit.** A real mode is spread in delay; the report writes its effective time spread as 2ρ. A zero-width tap reproduces it only over a bandwidth B ≈ 1/(4·2ρ) (ESSA report abstract p. x, eq. 110 and §6 pp. 84–85 [T]). With 2ρ ≈ 20–30 µs by day and ≈ 100 µs at night, B is about 8.3–12.5 kHz and 2.5 kHz respectively (same source; the abstract rounds the three samples to 2.5, 8.0 and 12 kHz).

**Relations useful for a narrowband, long-integration modem [E].** For a Gaussian Doppler spectrum with standard deviation σ = D/2 (D = 2σ spread), the tap autocorrelation is exp(−2π²σ²τ²). Hence:

- 1/e coherence time τc = 1/(√2·π·σ) = 0.450/D. D = 0.01, 0.1, 1, 10, 30 Hz gives 45 s, 4.5 s, 0.45 s, 45 ms, 15 ms.
- Level-crossing rate of the Rayleigh envelope (Rice's formula with second spectral moment (2πσ)²): N(ρ) = √π·D·ρ·exp(−ρ²), ρ = level/rms. At 10 dB below rms N = 0.507·D per second; the maximum (3 dB below rms) is 0.76·D, consistent with Watterson's remark that the spread is "approximately the fading rate" (NTIA 79-29 p. 42 [T]).
- Average fade duration below level ρ: (exp(ρ²)−1)/(√π·D·ρ); 0.188/D at −10 dB and 0.057/D at −20 dB. For D = 0.1 Hz: a 1.9 s fade below −10 dB about every 20 s; for D = 0.01 Hz: 19 s about every 200 s.
- Width conversions for a Gaussian spectrum: rms Doppler = D/2; −3 dB full width = 1.18·D; 80 %-power width = 1.28·D; 90 %-power width = 1.64·D; −10 dB full width = 2.15·D. A Lorentzian spectrum (exponential autocorrelation) has no finite σ, so "2σ" is undefined for it and another estimator must be named.
- Two equal paths separated by Δτ produce spectral nulls every 1/Δτ: 2 kHz (0.5 ms), 1 kHz (1 ms), 500 Hz (2 ms), 143 Hz (7 ms), 50 Hz (20 ms). A ≈225 Hz signal is flat-faded for Δτ ≲ 1 ms and frequency-selective for Δτ ≳ 4 ms; the 0.06 Hz and 0.6 Hz variants are flat-faded for any millisecond-scale multipath.
- SNR in 3 kHz = C/N0 − 10·log₁₀(3000) = C/N0 − 34.8 dB. So 32 dB-Hz is −2.8 dB in 3 kHz; 8, 0 and −6 dB-Hz are −26.8, −34.8 and −40.8 dB. A 2.7 kHz reference (F.520-2) differs from 3 kHz by 0.46 dB.

**Weak versus strong scatter.** Physics-based treatments distinguish a strong-scatter regime (fully randomised field; equatorial night, auroral/polar) from a weak-scatter regime in which "a substantial portion of the signal comes through with minor decorrelation" (Nickisch et al. 2012, ¶21 [P]). They state that strong scatter "is not appropriate for most midlatitude propagation or daylight hours in the equatorial zone" (same paragraph). This is a physical reason to expect a coherent (specular-like) component on quiet mid-latitude paths, in tension with the purely Rayleigh taps of the standard tests (see Contradictions).

### B. Channel conditions prescribed by standards

**ITU-R F.520-2 (1978–1982–1992; withdrawn).** Two independent equal-power paths, equal frequency spreads, no shift: good 0.5 ms / 0.1 Hz; moderate 1 ms / 0.5 Hz; poor 2 ms / 1 Hz; flutter 0.5 ms / 10 Hz. F.520-2 itself does not define the spread estimator; F.1487 later defines its corresponding conditions as 2σ. Single-path flat fading at 0.2 and 1 Hz. A frequency offset of both components from 0 to 10 Hz, at 0.5 ms and 0.2 Hz without noise (Annex 1 §2.3). Comparative tests: spread 0.1–50 Hz (flat fading); delay 0.1–5 ms at 0.2 and 1 Hz; mean level ratio −40…0 dB at 5 ms and 0.2 Hz. Annex 3 lists desirable simulator ranges: fade depth 2–40 dB; fade duration 0.05–1.5 s; 5–40 fades per minute; delay 0–5 ms; selective-fade width 0.1–1.2 kHz moving at 0.5–2 kHz/s; "frequency drifts" of 0–7 Hz (a range of offset in hertz, not a rate); SNR 0–40 dB with white Gaussian noise in 2.7 kHz. It also suggests simulating radio effects such as automatic gain control and synthesiser phase jumps (Annexes 1–3 [S]).

**ITU-R F.1487 (05/2000, in force) Annex 3**, two independent equal-power paths, equal 2σ spreads, zero shift [S]:

Occurrence (Annex 3 §5): low latitude, 4 ms and 3 Hz each exceeded about 5 % of the time; high latitude, 5 ms and 25 Hz each exceeded about 5 %. No basis or data set is cited for these. Annex 2 comparative grid: delay 0–4 ms in 0.5 ms steps, 4–12 in 1 ms, 12–20 in 2 ms; spread 0.1 Hz, 0.5–4 in 0.5 Hz steps, 4–20 in 2 Hz, 20–40 in 4 Hz; S/N −10 to +50 dB, set with band-limited Gaussian noise of unstated bandwidth; BER window 0.5–2 × 10⁻³. **MIL-STD-188-110D (29 December 2017)** [S] supersedes 110C with Change 1. In the passages compared, its channel-test rules are unchanged: Appendix E still specifies the two-path Watterson simulator, cites Watterson et al. (1970) as the model, gives the same tap-gain, delay-rounding, SNR and validation rules, and still states its scope as channels up to 24 kHz; the "Poor" channel is still 2 ms and 1 Hz measured for at least 5 hours, AWGN at least 60 minutes, and Table D-LXIV repeats the SNR values of 110C Table D-LII. What changed is the bandwidth: the wideband waveforms now run from 3 to 48 kHz, and the static-multipath tests (Table D-LXV adds two- to four-path 48 kHz channels with paths at up to 4 ms), the ±75 Hz Doppler-shift test and the 3.5 Hz/s sweep test now include 48 kHz channels. The simulator model is thus applied at four times the bandwidth to which Watterson validated it, without a new validation reference (§D.6, App. E; two image-only pages read as rendered images).

### D. Wideband and disturbed-channel comparisons used to justify post-Watterson models

Vogler and Hoffmeyer (NTIA 90-255 §3 [T]) compared their model with measured scattering functions whose delay spreads ran "from a few microseconds to over 2 milliseconds" and Doppler spreads "from 0.1 to over 40 Hz": a 2158 km equatorial path (Truk–Majuro, July 1986, 11 MHz, late night; 0.88 ms and 4 Hz); a 1913 km polar path (Greenland, March 1985); a 126 km mid-latitude path (southern California, winter 1983, 5.5 MHz), where Doppler varied with delay across each O and X return; an 88 km auroral path (Alaska, May 1988) in severe spread-F with no mode structure and about 1.9 ms delay extent; and a 2300 km auroral path (April 1986, 12.5 MHz, quiet magnetic conditions). They also cite dispersion of a few tens of µs/MHz on undisturbed mid-latitude paths and 240 µs/MHz in mid-latitude spread-F (§1.2, citing Wagner et al. 1989 [T, secondary]). Angling et al. (1998, abstract [P]) report 5 %-exceedance Doppler spreads of 2–55 Hz and multipath spreads of 1–11 ms on four high-latitude paths. Angling's thesis on the same DAMSON data (2001 [T], read in part) defines both as composite quantities: the Doppler spread is the central 80 % of the power of the sum of the modes' Gaussian spectra, shifts included, and the multipath spread runs from the leading edge of the first mode's 80 % power-percentile spread to the trailing edge of the last mode's (§5.4.6). Neither is the 2σ width of one path or the separation of two paths. The thesis's 5 %-exceedance values by frequency band reach 64–73 Hz on the 194 km Harstad–Kiruna path at 14.4–21.9 MHz (Tables 6-1, 6-5 and 6-6; 1995 data), and its §6.6 summary gives 2–73 Hz, although its §11 repeats 2–55 Hz. Its Fig. 3-7 converts an 80 %-power width of a single Gaussian to 2σ by multiplying by 0.78 (= 1/1.28).

| GNU Radio `gr-channels` | Sum-of-sinusoids fader set by a normalised maximum Doppler and a Rician K factor (mobile-radio type), not a Gaussian Doppler spectrum | source `flat_fader_impl.cc` [G] |

The "disturbed 2 Hz / 4 ms" preset of codec 2 and IONOS is not an F.520-2 or F.1487 condition; it equals DRM channel 5.

### F. Reported simulator-versus-air differences

Isode (white paper [G], page text read) compared on-air SNR and data-loss records (Rockwell Collins modems; Cedar Rapids, Iowa to Las Cruces, New Mexico, described as a mid-latitude daytime channel; 26–29 August 2014; STANAG 4539 and 3, 12 and 24 kHz wideband waveforms; mostly five-minute runs and a few of thirty minutes) with standard-model simulation. On air the SNR varied greatly over tens of seconds to a few minutes and losses came in blocks. Some simulator records looked "very similar", with SNR variation over about 10 s of a somewhat smaller range (perhaps 10–15 dB against 15–20 dB on air), which Isode calls "a quite reasonable approximation"; others were "stripy", and the simulator produced none of the blocky loss patterns seen on air. Isode concludes that a classic simulator deviates significantly from skywave over tens of seconds to minutes. It quotes, without naming sources, a typical intermediate-term standard deviation of 4 dB, which it says its measurements match. No frequency or geomagnetic state is given; the great-circle distance between the named end points is about 1,700 km [E].

The other work found does not compare one modem's on-air and simulator results directly.

- Jodalen, Bergsvik, Cannon and Arthur (2001, Radio Science 36(6):1687–1698, abstract [P]) characterised two 75 bit/s modems (STANAG 4415- and STANAG 4285-compliant), a 2400 bit/s STANAG 4285 modem, Morse and voice over "a comprehensive range of simulated channel conditions", and combined these characterisations with measured high-latitude Doppler spread, delay spread and SNR to estimate availability. The 75 bit/s modems showed 60–75 % higher availability than the 2400 bit/s modem, and only the latter degraded during a geomagnetic disturbance. These availabilities are simulator predictions applied to measured channels, not on-air modem measurements.
- Angling's thesis (2001 [T], read in part) found that availability estimates of this kind, made from Watterson-simulator performance surfaces and composite DAMSON spreads by the method of Cotterill and Arthur (1998), "do not perform well in the presence of Doppler spread" when compared with replay simulation of a six-hour period containing quiet and disturbed conditions; a revised method agreed with the replay, and the thesis states that both still need on-air confirmation (§10.4–10.6).
- Cotterill and Arthur (1998), "Validation of a multi-dimensional technique for characterising the performance of HF modems, using channel probes and embedded modem transmissions" (Nordic Shortwave Conference, Fårö), and Arthur and Maundrell (1997), "Multi-Dimensional HF Modem Performance Characteristics" (7th IEE Int. Conf. HF Radio Systems and Techniques, IEE Conf. Publ. 441, pp. 154–158), were located only as references in that thesis [secondary]. The first title indicates an on-air check using embedded modem transmissions; its results were not obtained.
- Furman and Nieto (2012), "Latest on-air testing of U.S. MIL-STD-188-110C Appendix D wideband HF data waveforms" (IET IRST 2012, p. 58), was located bibliographically only; a search summary describes on-air tests over a 160 km near-vertical link and a 1,700 km skywave link (not verified).

## How the literature models or simulates this

### 1. The Watterson model: validated domain and stated limits

The validated domain is narrow: one path, one month, two frequencies, three samples totalling 36 minutes, spreads below 0.5 Hz, selected for stationarity. The authors' own caveats were explicit:

- The channel is "nonstationary in both frequency and time"; stationarity was assumed only for bandwidths of about 10 kHz and samples of about 15 minutes (§2.1 p. 4). Confidence in the model "decreases as the duration of an experiment increases beyond" that of the validating measurements (p. 5) [T].
- The recommended specifications were offered without implying "that improved ionospheric channel models are not possible" (App. E p. 111) [T].
- Ten years later Watterson wrote that the validated model "applies only to a quiet ionosphere (the usual case)", and that a spread-F version with an exponential delay profile "has not been validated" (NTIA 79-29 p. 44) [T].

Later critiques: Vogler and Hoffmeyer (NTIA 90-255 §1.1, pp. 4–5 [T]) list the limits as bandwidth ≤ 12 kHz, time and frequency stationarity, negligible delay dispersion (no spread-F) and low ray only. They complain that the restrictions "apparently have been forgotten", citing a simulator marketed with 24 kHz capability, and conclude that stationary models represent conditions "for only a small portion of the time" (§4 p. 30). They quote CCIR Report 549-2 (1986) as conceding that accuracy falls with bandwidth and that the Gaussian-scatter model "almost certainly is not valid for all HF ionospheric channels" [secondary]. Sutherland (NTIA 98-348 §1 [T]) calls the model "extremely restricted, as Watterson himself made quite clear". Gherm et al. (URSI 2005 [G]) add: fixed delay per mode, Gaussian Doppler shape only, low ray only, stationarity. An inference of this chapter [E]: with tap gains sampled at 3.125–6.25 Hz and spreads of 0.005–0.4 Hz, the experiment could not test the Gaussian-shape or Rayleigh hypotheses for spreads of several hertz or more; and for the narrowest modes (0.005–0.05 Hz) a 600–780 s sample holds only a few independent fades, so the Gaussian-amplitude test had little power there. The report itself notes that a small effective number of independent values limited accuracy for the strongest path of sample I2 (p. 76) [T].

### 2. Standards built on it

- **CCIR Report 549** and its revisions promulgated the simulator model. The earlier “no open copy” status is superseded: the official ITU historical collection supplies the editions, and 549-2 (1986), pp.59–67, has now been read in French. Its model-validity and dependent-input cautions are summarized in the completion-research section below and in [31](31-skywave-dispersion-midlatitude-nvis-longhaul.md); this does not make every edition fully inspected.
- **ITU-R F.520** became F.520-2 in 1992 and was withdrawn: the ITU status page lists F.520-2 (03/92) as "Withdrawn" with the note "Suppressed on 05/05/00 (RA-2000)", that is, on 5 May 2000 at the 2000 Radiocommunication Assembly; F.1487 carries the same month (05/2000). The PDF carries the withdrawn suffix. F.1487 refers to it as "the former Recommendation". Its good, moderate and poor conditions survive as F.1487's mid-latitude quiet, moderate and disturbed.
- **ITU-R F.1487** states that the model "is valid for use in 3 kHz channels, and may be applicable to bandwidths up to 12 kHz", and that it "can accurately represent the majority of typical HF ionospheric links" (Annex 1 §1, §3), without citing evidence beyond the "considering" clause that some administrations reported good correlation between simulator and operational tests. Annex 1 §3 allows a non-fading tap for a specular component or ground wave; §4 says extension beyond 12 kHz needs "more development and validation". The spread is defined as 2σ; filter shape is Gaussian; no tolerance on the filter, update rate or interpolation is given.
- **MIL-STD-188-110C Appendix E** tightens the implementation: sample rate ≥ 4× symbol rate; delay rounded to the nearest sample; tap gains from complex white Gaussian noise through a Gaussian filter with σ equal to half the fading bandwidth, truncated where the response falls to 1 % of its peak, computed at ≥ 32× the fading bandwidth and interpolated at least to the symbol rate; SNR referred to the long-term average input power in the waveform bandwidth; radio filters disabled; Hilbert transform permitted for real audio. It covers 3–24 kHz while still citing Watterson 1970 as the model [S]; MIL-STD-188-110D keeps the appendix, including that scope statement, while extending the wideband tests to 48 kHz (section B). The Harris slides behind it say plainly: "Assume model holds to 24 kHz", adding that even if it does not match propagation precisely it remains a useful comparison tool, and that it will not model partial-band effects such as MUF transitions (2010, slide 4 [G]).
- **STANAG 4285, 4415, 4539, 4538.** The NATO texts were not obtained, and no public copy of their performance requirements or test matrices was found. Public descriptions (signal wiki and decoder documentation [G]) give waveform characteristics only; they describe STANAG 4415 qualitatively, as a robust non-hopping 75 bit/s mode for "severely degraded HF channels with large Doppler and multipath spreads", equivalent to the 75 bit/s MIL-STD-188-110 serial mode. The nearest published envelope of that class found is a simulator surface for the MIL-STD-188-110A 75 bit/s waveform at 0 dB SNR (Bagwell 1996, as reproduced in Angling 2001 Fig. 3-7 [secondary]): BER below 10⁻⁵ for Doppler spreads below 20 Hz (80 %-power width, about 16 Hz as 2σ) and multipath below 4 ms, degrading towards 10⁻³ as the spread rises beyond 20 Hz, and failing abruptly at about 4 ms of two-path delay. Jodalen et al. (2001) characterised STANAG 4415- and 4285-compliant modems on a simulator, but the abstract gives no test matrix (section F). MIL-STD-188-110C §5.3.4 defers robust 75 bit/s performance to STANAG 4415; its Appendix C corresponds to STANAG 4539 and MIL-STD-188-141B Appendix C to STANAG 4538 (secondary descriptions [G]). The US tables above are therefore the nearest verified test matrices. Jorgenson and Moreland (1999 [T]) note that waveform standards generally specify only the transmitted signal, the US standards being the ones that add minimum performance figures.
- **DRM** justifies its stationary profiles by stating that results on real channels give BER curves "between best and worst cases found in the simulation" (ETSI Annex B.1 [S]); no data are cited.

### 3. Beyond Watterson: wideband empirical models

- **ITS model (Vogler & Hoffmeyer; NTIA 88-240, 90-255, 92-284; Radio Science 1993).** Each mode has a delay-amplitude shape factor T(τ) in place of a delta function, with parameters for peak amplitude, total spread and rise portion; mode delay follows from layer parameters (penetration frequency, height, thickness) so that low and high rays and dispersion are produced; the Doppler correlation factor is selectable as Gaussian or exponential (the latter giving a peaked, Lorentzian-type spectrum); Doppler shift may vary linearly with delay to give "slanted" scattering-function ridges; spread-F is produced by multiplying by a random factor (NTIA 90-255 §2 [T]). Validation is visual comparison with the scattering functions in section D; the authors say a statistical decision between exponential and Gaussian shapes "is reserved for future studies" (§3.5), and many input parameters were read off the very plots being matched (§2.1.3). The peaked spectrum was "a characteristic of many of the SRI data measurements" (§3.2) [T]. Part III (NTIA 92-284, abstract [T]) goes further: comparison with NRL prober data from an 80 km near-vertical auroral path near Fairbanks, Alaska, at 2.8–5.3 MHz over a wide range of spread-F conditions, indicated that the fading process "is best modeled" with an exponential autocorrelation, "a scattering function having a Lorentzian shape". Part I (NTIA 88-240, figure list [T]) compared model and measured ionograms and pulse responses on 126 km, 2200 km and 2600 km paths. None of this evidence concerns quiet mid-latitude narrowband channels.
- **Mastrangelo et al. 1997** (IEEE Trans. Commun. 45(1):26–34 [P, bibliographic and search summary only]) describes the resulting hardware and software system with propagation, noise and interference models, operating above 1 Msample/s. Gherm et al. criticise it as empirical, needing many parameters per mode that cannot be derived from path and ionosphere, based on little data, with unsupported assumptions that delay taps within a mode fade independently and that Doppler shift and spread can be chosen independently for different modes (URSI 2005 [G]).
- **Noise and interference (Lemmon & Behm, NTIA 91-277 [T]).** A waveform model with three components (Gaussian noise, narrowband interferers, impulsive noise), fitted to wideband records (equivalent RF bandwidth 800 kHz, for a model aimed at about 1 MHz) taken in March 1989 at Bedford, Massachusetts. The authors list its limits: no way to choose parameters for a given place and time, higher-order statistics (pulse widths and spacings, non-stationarity) not yet modelled, one site and month, eight-bit data (§4 pp. 83–84). Part II (NTIA 93-293 [T]) addresses higher-order correlations and pulse timing; full body remains unread here, while [50](50-noise-and-interference-mf-hf.md) distinguishes the summary pages inspected from a complete report reading. No body-only parameter is selected here. Lemmon (2001, abstract [P]) later gave an atmospheric-noise waveform model with burst correlations, checked against envelope distributions.

### 4. Physics-based stochastic simulators

- **Gherm, Zernov & Strangeways (2005), primary full text now read.** The extended complex-phase/Rytov method constructs frequency–time covariance of log-amplitude and phase from a three-dimensional background and moving anisotropic density fluctuations. Its weak/moderate-fluctuation limitation and independence of separated ray volumes matter; it is not an unrestricted strong-scatter solution. The journal example is **1,000 km westward from St Petersburg, 8.1 MHz, IRI July 0700 LT, spectral index 3.7, transverse outer scale 3 km, aspect ratio 5, fractional-density variance 3×10⁻⁶, longitudinal and latitudinal drift components each 0.5 km/s, 20-kHz pulse bandwidth** (§4.1 ¶35–36). These differ from the companion URSI example previously quoted here; neither is a measured fitted channel. Figures 4/5 compare theoretical moments with moments estimated from simulated signals, not observations. Background motion may add centroid shift, while random irregularities supply spread (§5); the worked random medium is stationary. Use as a physical modelling option, with measured input/holdout validation still required. [Primary article](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2004RS003093).
- **HiCIRF (Nickisch et al. 2012 [P], read).** Numerical ray tracing in a climatological ionosphere gives modes, delays, absorption, focusing and Doppler shift; the phase-screen/diffraction method (Nickisch 1992) with irregularity strength and spectral index from the WBMOD climatology and plasma-drift models gives delay, Doppler and angular spreading; signal realisations are generated at antenna-element level. Stated limits: mean path curvature is ignored in the stochastic calculation, which "seems adequate" only for reasonably oblique paths; reflection-caustic effects are ignored (¶13); the weak-scatter case is handled by a Rician split into a coherent and a decorrelated part, set by the S4 scintillation index (¶21; the authors describe it as "a smooth transition from the strong-scatter regime to the no-scatter regime"); and the coherent term carries no phase variation from large-scale electron-content changes, so a user "could get the wrong impression that extremely long coherent integration times" help (¶23). That last caveat bears directly on a modem that integrates for seconds to hours. Validation was said to be in interim sponsor reports, with extensive testing "in the next year" (¶5); those reports were not found.
- **Phase-screen and coherence-function theory.** Knepp (1983) for multiple phase screens; Dana and Wittwer (1991, abstract [P]) for a general model running between frozen-in drift (space and time fluctuations locked together) and full turbulence (uncorrelated); both were developed for transionospheric scintillation and are applied to the reflected HF channel by HiCIRF with the caveats above.
- **Measurement-driven model (Angling 1999, 2001).** A colloquium paper, "An assessment of a new ionospheric channel model driven by measurements of multipath and Doppler spread" (Crossref lists M. Angling as sole author [bibliographic only]), has a title matching the work in Angling's PhD thesis (University of Leicester, April 2001 [T], read in part). The thesis's SUEDE is a non-real-time replay simulator that uses the ITS (Vogler–Hoffmeyer) model in the narrow band, at 9600 samples/s, driven by DAMSON scattering-function parameters measured about every 10 minutes and interpolated between measurements, with modes tracked from one measurement to the next (§8.2–8.7). Its implementation was checked by showing BERs that agree with existing simulators. With it, a Lorentzian Doppler shape gave about three times the BER of a Gaussian for a 2400 bit/s MIL-STD-188-110A serial-tone modem and about twice for a 300 bit/s wide-shift FSK modem (§9.5). A single fading mode with a spread delay-power profile gave the serial-tone modem BERs many orders of magnitude worse than a Watterson pair of discrete taps at the same delay extent, because the profile's low-power wings fell outside the equaliser span; the size of the effect depends on the assumed profile, and the FSK modem was unaffected (§9.4). Fading at the delay taps within a mode is uncorrelated in SUEDE, and the thesis says a measured model of that correlation is needed (§9.5). It also states that there is "currently no model to indicate which Doppler shape should be used for particular ionospheric conditions", and that its methods still need on-air confirmation (§10.6, §11).

### 6. Physics- and geometry-driven prediction of the "slow" channel

- **Ray tracing.** Jones and Stephenson (OT Report 75-76, 1975, abstract [T]): three-dimensional Hamiltonian ray tracing with Appleton–Hartree or Sen–Wyller refractive index; outputs group path, phase path, absorption and "Doppler shift due to a time-varying ionosphere"; includes models for density perturbations; Fortran listing in the report. Coleman (1998 [P]) gives a fast two-dimensional scheme used for oblique and backscatter ionogram synthesis. PHaRLAP is the Australian defence toolbox with two- and three-dimensional magneto-ionic engines (described from Cervera & Harris 2014 and the PyLap repository; the distribution page refused the automated request). PyLap is an MIT-licensed Python interface that needs the separately downloaded PHaRLAP binaries and describes itself as experimental and "not meant for operational use" [G]. IONORT (Azzarone et al. 2012 [P, bibliographic only]) is a Windows ray-tracing tool. The vendor’s [Proplab-Pro version-3 manual](https://shop.spacew.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/08/Manual.pdf), now read in relevant sections, documents IRI2007, two-/three-dimensional tracing, O/X ray and ground-intercept analysis and oblique ionograms. This establishes implementation features, not measured fading-model validity. The current vendor page advertises version 3.2/build 47 (August 2025); the older manual cannot establish all current internals.
- **Background ionospheres.** IRI describes "monthly averages" of density from 60 to 2000 km and includes a spread-F occurrence probability (Bilitza et al. 2022, abstract [P]). NeQuick 2 is Nava et al. 2008 [bibliographic only]. E-CHAIM covers geomagnetic latitudes above 50°N; its hmF2 rms error is about 13 km against 16–22 km for IRI-2007, and its foF2 error in the polar cap is lower by up to 1.3 MHz (Themens et al. 2017, abstract [P]). Assimilative models reduce these errors: A-CHAIM cut foF2 error by 40–50 % at mid-latitude and auroral reference stations during 21–29 September 2021 (Reid et al. 2023, abstract [P]); others are IRI real-time assimilative mapping (Galkin et al. 2012), GAIM (Schunk et al. 2004) and AENeAS (Elvidge & Angling 2019).
- **Travelling disturbances.** Cervera and Harris (2014, abstract [P]) reproduced disturbance features on O and X traces of quasi-vertical ionograms by perturbing the ionosphere with atmospheric gravity waves and ray tracing in three dimensions (scales below 150 km, time resolution under a minute). Heitmann et al. (2018, full primary text now read [P]) compare parameterized disturbances and geometric ray tracing with ELOISE 2015 delay, Doppler, bearing and elevation observations. Their conclusion says broad features are reproduced qualitatively; some mirror-model parameters were matched by eye, the gravity-wave model struggles with some larger disturbances, and evolving superposed components remain unresolved. The acknowledgement states that Commonwealth-owned ELOISE data are available only by case-by-case request, and PHaRLAP by author request. This is meaningful measured-signature evidence, not a validated statistical event generator or a public replay package. [Primary article, conclusion and acknowledgements](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2018RS006613). Mitchell et al. (2017, abstract [P]) assimilated HF observations into an ensemble of physics-based disturbance models and validated angle-of-arrival predictions over two days in January 2014. Hooke's 1968 perturbation theory is the usual basis; that citation was not verified.
- **Absorption.** Pederick and Cervera (2014, abstract [P]) validated a semi-empirical absorption model against vertical-sounder returned power; Zawdie et al. (2017 [P, bibliographic only]); Warrington et al. (2016, abstract [P]) drive polar absorption from X-ray and proton flux for nowcasting.
- **What these predict and what they do not.** By construction and by the validations above, they give mode structure, group delay, angles, MUF, absorption and the deterministic Doppler shift (and its slow variation when a disturbance model is included). Their absolute accuracy is bounded by the background model, hence the hmF2 and foF2 errors quoted. They give no Doppler or delay spread from small-scale structure unless an irregularity climatology and scattering theory are added, which is what Gherm et al. and HiCIRF do. Hoffmeyer and Nesenbergs observed that ordinary ionograms give relative delays but seldom mode amplitudes, phases, spreads or their mutual dependence (NTIA 87-221 p. 40 [T]).

### 7. Data-driven and replay approaches

- **Sounders and public data.** Research probes used for model building: the NRL channel prober (1 µs delay resolution, ±30 Hz unambiguous Doppler; Wagner et al. 1988, abstract [P]), the SRI 20 kHz probe (Basler et al. 1988 [P]) and DAMSON (3 kHz scattering functions; Angling et al. 1998 [P]). Open data: ionosonde networks through GIRO (Reinisch & Galkin 2011 [P]); HamSCI Doppler records of time-standard carriers from late 2019 to early 2022, archived with a DOI (Collins et al. 2023, abstract [P]); SuperDARN (Nishitani et al. 2019 [P]); and amateur reporting networks (Reverse Beacon Network, WSPRNet, PSKReporter), which have shown flare blackouts, storm suppression and large-scale travelling disturbances with a period of about 2.5 hours (Frissell et al. 2014, 2019, 2022, abstracts [P]). The reporting networks give level and link-existence statistics, not scattering functions. A primary [KiwiSDR time-transfer repository](https://github.com/jmfriedt/kiwisdr_timetransfer/) describes GPS-timestamped recordings and processing at 77.5/100/162 kHz; those are below this HF scope and do not supply a calibrated HF scattering-function archive. The maintained KiwiSDR changelog also describes a waterfall-percentile “SNR” metric; it must not be treated as antenna-terminal modem C/N0. No suitable coherent HF replay package was obtained from these routes.
- **Replay as a methodological precedent.** In underwater acoustics, measured time-varying impulse responses are replayed directly or used to drive a stochastic generator (van Walree et al. 2008; Socheleau et al. 2011; Otnes et al. 2013, "Validation of replay-based underwater acoustic communication channel simulation"; van Walree et al. 2017, the Watermark benchmark [P, bibliographic only]). The Watermark documentation states that the realism of direct replay depends primarily on the quality of the channel measurement (search summary of FFI report 16/01378 [T]). The nearest HF equivalent found is Angling's SUEDE (2001 [T], section 4 above), which replays parameterised DAMSON measurements rather than raw channel soundings and was checked only against existing simulators; no HF replay simulator validated against on-air modem performance was found.
- **Machine-learning HF channel models (2019–2026).** A bounded search found no machine-learning, generative or "digital twin" HF channel model validated against measurements. The HF machine-learning work located uses the Watterson model to synthesise training data: Scholl (2019, arXiv:1906.04459 [G], read in part) trains signal classifiers on signals passed through F.520-2 channel conditions, and Kompella et al. (2024, arXiv:2410.18283 [G], read in part) augment Watterson-faded HF training data with a vector-quantised variational autoencoder, a generative model of received signals rather than of the channel. Generative models found in the ionospheric literature (for example ionogram forecasting) address ionospheric parameters, not channel synthesis (search summary only).

### 8. Implementation fidelity issues documented in the sources

- **Doppler filter shape.** The Gaussian is approximated by three poles in the ITS hardware, two poles in Johnson's software, and finite impulse response filters elsewhere; a low-order pole filter has heavier tails than a Gaussian [E]. MIL-STD-188-110C sets tolerances at −20 and −30 dB. Furman and Nieto (2001; primary technical text §§5–7 now read through a mirror [G]) compare two-pole Butterworth and Gaussian fading filters, update rates and interpolation. Their reported maximum differences at BER10⁻⁵ are about 0.5 dB for 2400L versus over 2.3 dB for 9600VL. Meeting a 32-times-spread update rate alone did not eliminate interpolation images. Their prescriptions normalize ensemble path power and interpolate gains ideally to the waveform sample rate. This is implementation-comparison evidence, not field validation. [Recovered paper text](https://www.yumpu.com/en/document/view/43230354/understanding-hf-channel-simulator-requirements-).
- **Update rate and interpolation.** 30× to 64× the spread, then interpolation (PathSim uses four cascaded 5× stages; codec 2 generates at 10× and resamples). The standard warns that poor interpolation puts discontinuities in the output.
- **Real audio versus analytic signal.** A finite Hilbert filter has a low-frequency band edge: PathSim's passes roughly 400–3400 Hz. At higher sample rates the edge rises, which is why Harris moved to an overlap-FFT transform (2010, slide 8 [G]). Tones near the band edges are distorted.
- **Delay quantisation.** One sample is 104 µs at 9.6 kHz and 125 µs at 8 kHz; MIL-STD-188-110C rounds to the nearest sample (0.5 ms becomes 0.52 ms at 9.6 kHz [E]); codec 2 truncates.
- **SNR definition.** 3 kHz (MIL-STD, PathSim), 2.7 kHz (F.520-2), the operating bandwidth (IONOS; wideband modes), or per hertz (F.339-8, codec 2). The reference power may be the long-term mean of the input (MIL-STD-188-110C notes this can take "several minutes" to measure), a pre-fading transmit power with noise set as a fixed density (codec 2), or a running RMS (PathSim). PathSim's guide is ambiguous about whether its RMS is taken before or after the fading paths (§2.7 versus §4.1.8). Its v 1.0 source settles this: `CIOCntrl::ProcessSimulator()` accumulates the RMS on the raw input buffer before the band-pass filter and the path calculations, smooths it over 0.256 s blocks with a first-order filter of coefficient 1/20 (time constant about 5 s [E]), and then scales the summed faded signal by a gain inversely proportional to that running input level, with the noise level fixed. Slow fades are therefore not normalised away. Input-level changes slower than a few seconds are, however: a level step in the input, including the end of a keyed silence or a quiet slot, changes the effective SNR for several seconds after it [E, from the code].
- **Fixed-delay artefacts.** A constant delay puts permanent nulls at fixed frequencies; MIL-STD-188-141B changed its test delays for this reason. Forrer notes users' complaints that the equal-power two-path notch is "not being realistic" (1999 [G]).
- **Repeatability.** Seeds and filter transients differ between implementations; Furman and Nieto ran independent seeds and still saw hours-long convergence. Forrer (1999 [G]) reports discrepancies when comparing his results with published ones from professional simulators, which he attributes to "some leeway in interpretation of the Watterson model" in proprietary implementations.
- **Radio filters and gain control.** Jorgenson and Moreland (1999 §8 [T]) found that raising the symbol rate above 2400 symbols/s through commercial radios with 2.7–2.8 kHz passbands improved AWGN results but not those on a 2 ms "CCIR poor" channel, and that "performance degraded markedly as delay spread was increased when the signal was passed through real radios", a result they say Nieto later confirmed. The US standards disable radio filters in the simulator and add SNR allowances instead (section B).
- **Grey-literature errors.** Forrer's paper places Watterson's path between Boulder and Washington, DC; the primary report says Long Branch, Illinois to Boulder.

### 9. Recent work, 2015–2026 (as far as verified)

Standards and profiles were republished without changing the channel model (ETSI ES 201 980 V4.2.1, 2021; ITU-R BS.1615-3, 2023). New channel measurements include narrowband and wideband sounding on a 12,760 km Antarctica–Spain link (Hervás et al. 2015 [P]) and polarisation-diversity studies for multiple-antenna HF (Gunashekar et al. 2009; Witvliet et al. 2015; Erhel et al. 2016 [P, bibliographic or abstract]). Ionosphere specification advanced (E-CHAIM 2017, A-CHAIM 2023, AENeAS 2019, IRI review 2022), as did disturbance-resolving ray tracing and assimilation (Mitchell 2017, Heitmann 2018, Cervera 2018) and citizen-science Doppler and link data (Frissell 2019, 2022; Collins 2022, 2023; Gibbons 2022). None of the items located proposes and validates a non-stationary or event-driven (flare, travelling disturbance, storm, MUF transit) channel *generator* for modem testing. The nearest are the disturbance-signature synthesis of Heitmann et al., the unpublished Harris variation stage and, earlier, Angling's DAMSON-driven replay (2001). MIL-STD-188-110D (2017) extends simulator testing to 48 kHz with an unchanged Watterson appendix (section B). No measurement-based validation of the Watterson model at 24–48 kHz was obtained. Le Nir and Becquaert (c. 2023, author copy [G], read in part) cite Furman, Nieto and Batts, "Evaluating the propagation characteristics of contiguous wideband channels" (Nordic HF Conference, 2019), as showing that the Watterson model "is still valid for higher bandwidths and wideband HF standards"; the official Nordic 2019 programme now confirms its authors and title, but linked presentations require participant login. ## What a high-fidelity simulation would need

These are options with their limits, not a recommended design; better approaches may exist that were not found.

For an implementable source-bound scenario set, see [83](83-scenarios-skywave.md). Keep a source-matched stationary Watterson/F.1487 comparator, but use a mode-structured channel as the physical representation: for each actual transmitted frequency `f`, form `h_f(t,τ) = Σ_m Σ_{q∈{O,X}} a_{m,q,f}(t) g_{m,q,f}(t,τ−τ_{m,q,f}(t))`, and apply that same physical propagation state to Helper and every DATA carrier. `m` identifies geometric modes; `q` identifies magneto-ionic components. Each component carries a measured/declared mean power, group delay, centroid trajectory, Doppler spectrum and availability state. This equation defines an interface, not a claim that all terms are jointly measured. For an unmeasured dependence, preserve separate marginals and expose a fully defined coupling assumption as a named sensitivity branch;83 specifies stationary and shared-latent alternatives, rather than leaving the law to an implementer. Do not mistake either for a measured distribution or rigorous performance bound. In particular, Male et al.'s O/X dependence is evidence from one single-frequency 5.4 MHz NVIS link, while frequency-to-frequency dependence among the actual modem carriers remains unresolved.

A sounder no-return is a threshold- and instrument-conditioned observation, not proof of exactly zero transfer. Preserve it as censored and retain compatible residual-channel alternatives. A zero received channel may be used as an explicitly synthetic complete-outage stress, followed by a declared recovery; do not label that precise zero as measured. Keep availability denominators and conditional dispersion statistics separately: the former include undetected intervals, while sounding spreads are only valid on the detection-conditioned interval and under each instrument's threshold, record length and resolution. Receiver loss, modem decode failure and ionospheric absence are separate outcomes. These requirements are compatible with continuous Helper/DATA observation and avoid assigning a fitted fading distribution to ionosonde MOF or a blackout record.

**Inputs.** (a) Path geometry, frequency, date, time, solar and geomagnetic indices. (b) A mode list with delay, mean power, O/X split, Doppler shift and its time derivative. (c) Per-mode small-scale statistics: spread, spectral shape, coherent fraction, within-mode delay spread. (d) Slow trajectories of all of these. (e) Noise and interference waveforms (covered by other notes; in the HF modelling literature only Lemmon and Behm and Lemmon 2001 offer waveform-level models). (f) Receiver effects: filters, gain control, oscillator drift.

**Model-structure options.**

1. *Stationary Watterson with standard parameters.* Cheap, repeatable and comparable with published modem results. Validated only for quiet mid-latitude conditions with spreads below 0.5 Hz; the 10–30 Hz and 6–7 ms conditions are conventions. Two equal Rayleigh paths with a fixed delay create a permanent comb and no coherent component.
2. *Extended Watterson.* More taps, unequal powers, bi-Gaussian O and X terms, optional non-fading term, selectable Gaussian or exponential-correlation spectrum, delay-dependent Doppler, within-mode delay spread (the ITS form). Reproduces the published scattering-function examples, but its parameters must come from measurement, and independence within and between modes is assumed.
3. *Parameter trajectories.* Extended Watterson driven by measured or explicitly hypothetical slow trajectories. Heitmann demonstrates qualitative disturbance-feature matching, not a stochastic event distribution. The source-derived availability/estimator gates are retained in 30–32;83 now defines exact synthetic transitions and a 4-dB/60-s OU sensitivity law. Its time constant is a design assumption and its 4-dB value is not promoted from Isode grey literature to a validated climatology.
4. *Physics-based stochastic simulation* (ray tracing plus irregularity climatology plus scattering theory, as in Gherm et al. or HiCIRF). Derives spreads from physical inputs and keeps modes mutually consistent. Limits: weak-scatter theory in one case, strong-scatter assumptions with a Rician blend in the other; no large-scale phase variation on the coherent term; climatological irregularity inputs; validation not publicly documented; neither code appears to be openly available.
5. *Measured-channel replay.* Direct or stochastic replay of sounder data (Angling's SUEDE replays sounder-derived parameters at 10-minute intervals). It inherits every real effect within the sounder's bandwidth, Doppler range and SNR, but is limited to the paths and times recorded; open data with full scattering functions are scarce (HamSCI data are single-carrier Doppler; reporting networks are level only).
6. *Hybrids.* For example, replayed Doppler-shift and level trajectories from carrier monitoring, with model-generated small-scale fading.

**Observables to validate against.** Envelope distribution per mode and composite (Rayleigh versus Rician); Doppler spectrum shape to at least −30 dB; fade-rate and fade-duration statistics at several depths; delay profile and frequency correlation across the occupied band; O/X separation; Doppler-shift trajectories over minutes to hours; phase-coherence time on the least-spread mode; minute-scale SNR statistics; and the fraction of time for which a stationary description holds.

**Test-length implications [E].** By F.1487's rule a 0.1 Hz spread needs 3×10⁴ s (8.3 h), and by the Harris finding roughly 40–50 h. A spread of 0.01 Hz, as Watterson measured on E modes, needs 3×10⁵ s (83 h) or several hundred hours. For a modem with a bit every 12–26 s, the error-count term 100/(BER × rate) at BER 10⁻³ is 1.2–2.6×10⁶ s (14–30 days); for a 96-minute bit it is about 18 years. Such tests have to run far faster than real time, or rely on semi-analytic methods. Stationary long runs also stop corresponding to any real channel once they exceed the stationarity interval of 10–15 minutes.

## Comparison with the modem's presets

Four HF and Aurora presets carry the numbers of F.1487 conditions, but not their meaning ([00](00-method-and-modem-context.md) §5). In every standard test the Doppler figure is the 2σ width of each of two equal-power, independently Rayleigh-fading paths with zero shift; in the presets it bounds the offset and wander of a two-path family whose direct path never fades out. The standards' structure corresponds to the simulator's lower-level option of independent Gaussian-spectrum gains of chosen 2σ width on both rays, not to its changing-channel realisation; whether that option also gives equal mean powers was not checked here. The third column reads each preset as the standard defines it, using the relations in "Physical mechanisms" [E]; for the 16.7 ms cells and 75 Hz carrier spacing of rows 4 to 8 see [00](00-method-and-modem-context.md) §4.1.

**Noise and level.** Every standard test adds white Gaussian noise of fixed power, in a stated bandwidth where one is given (3 kHz in MIL-STD-188-110C and -141B, 2.7 kHz in F.520-2, the waveform bandwidth for wideband modes, per hertz in F.339-8; none in F.1487), with MIL-STD-188-110C Appendix E referring it to the long-term average input power; and no standard test condition varies the mean level: the only level variation is the paths' Rayleigh fading. The simulator's white complex Gaussian noise of fixed variance and its constant gain from transmit power and path loss follow the same convention, as do MIL-STD-188-110C Appendix E and codec 2 `ch`; PathSim does not, because it rescales the signal to a running input level with a time constant of about 5 s. The difference lies in the fading: in the standard conditions the received signal is Rayleigh-faded at every frequency about its fixed mean, whereas in the preset family the direct path never fades. No standard condition includes the impulsive noise, interferers or intermediate-term level variation (Isode's 4 dB) that the ITS and Harris simulators offer as options. The standards' lowest SNR test points (about 25–29 dB-Hz, taking 3 kHz where no bandwidth is stated) lie just below the modem's 32 dB-Hz planning value and above the simulated C/N0 of all eight manual rows (−36 to +20 dB-Hz), so published simulator results say little about that range.

## Contradictions, open questions, and things not found

1. **Rayleigh or Rician at mid-latitude.** Watterson found no specular component and advised against simulating one for skywave. Nickisch et al. argue that weak scatter, typical of mid-latitudes, leaves a substantial coherent part; Wagner et al. (1988, abstract) describe the mid-latitude channel as usually "a single specular return per mode", but they use "specular" for a return that is discrete in delay, as opposed to diffuse, which does not by itself imply a non-fading component; DRM channel 2 ("Rice with delay") is Rician. A possible reconciliation [E]: a Gaussian process with 0.005–0.05 Hz spread is indistinguishable from a slowly wandering specular component over tens of seconds, and Watterson's 10–13 minute samples held few independent fades. The question matters for a modem integrating over seconds to hours, and no measurement that settles it was found.
2. **How representative the Gaussian-scatter model is.** F.1487 says it represents "the majority of typical" links, and Watterson (1979) called the quiet ionosphere to which it applies "the usual case"; Vogler and Hoffmeyer say stationary models hold for "only a small portion of the time"; Watterson analysed 36 minutes out of 17 hours.
3. **Test duration.** F.1487 (3000/D, "proved to be satisfactory in practice") against Furman and Nieto (4–5 h at 1 Hz; F.1487 "too optimistic") and MIL-STD-188-110C (5 h).
4. **Bandwidth of validity.** 2.5–12 kHz (Watterson); "3 kHz ... may be applicable to 12 kHz" (F.1487); assumed to 24 kHz (Harris, MIL-STD-188-110C) against the explicit objection of Vogler and Hoffmeyer, and applied to 48 kHz tests by MIL-STD-188-110D. A 2019 Harris paper is cited as showing validity at wider bandwidths, but its primary content remains unavailable through the participant-restricted conference route (section 9).
5. **Doppler spectrum shape.** Gaussian "has proven to be a good approach" (DRM), and was accepted by Watterson for spreads below 0.5 Hz; Lorentzian is "best" for auroral spread-F (Vogler and Hoffmeyer, Part III). The two may both hold in their own regimes; no statistical test of either against the other was found.
6. **Provenance errors.** Vogler and Hoffmeyer say the validating data were taken "during a single day"; the primary report shows 10 and 30 November 1967. MIL-STD-188-141B Table C-I prints "ITU-R F.250-2" for F.520-2. F.1487 Annex 3 equates its low-latitude moderate condition (1.5 Hz) with F.520's poor channel (1 Hz). MIL-STD-188-110D Appendix E still calls itself part of MIL-STD-188-110B and limits its scope to 24 kHz while Appendix D tests at 48 kHz. Angling's thesis gives the 5 %-exceedance Doppler range as 2–73 Hz in §6.6 and 2–55 Hz in §11; the 1998 paper's abstract gives 2–55 Hz.
7. **Simulator-versus-air agreement.** Standards assert good correlation without references. The one direct comparison read (Isode) is qualitative grey literature that found some simulator records reasonably similar to air and clear differences in loss structure at 10 s to minutes. The availability studies (Jodalen et al. 2001; Angling 2001) apply simulator characterisations to measured channels, and Angling found that the existing Watterson-surface method does "not perform well in the presence of Doppler spread" when judged against replay simulation; the on-air check suggested by the title of Cotterill and Arthur (1998) was not obtained.

**Remaining source and validation limits.** The source-by-source disposition and actual query ledger above replace the earlier undifferentiated “not found” list. CCIR 549-2, primary Gherm/Heitmann model passages, Furman/Nieto 2001 technical text, linsim and Proplab documentation have usable new evidence; they must no longer be listed as wholly unlocated. The original NATO matrices, participant-restricted 2019 wideband paper, several historical conference bodies, full 93-293 and sponsor-validation reports remain uninspected at the stated access grades. FFI and recent measured-channel findings are incorporated at their actual grades in 30–32. No selected numerical control depends on treating those unread sources as evidence. Empirical joint transition/covariance/replay and broad physical validity remain scientific/data gates, not omissions repaired by assigning a synthetic law.
