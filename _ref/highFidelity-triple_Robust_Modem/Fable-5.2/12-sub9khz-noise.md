## Summary

Below 9 kHz the external noise is dominated by lightning in many remote-site measurements, and can be impulsive when receiver bandwidth resolves sferics; it also varies with site, time and bandwidth, and may include local man-made signals. The cited evidence does not establish one universal distribution. The latest ITU-R Recommendation (P.372-18, September 2026) still describes this band with one figure of minimum and maximum expected hourly-median Fa. At 8.27 kHz those curves span Fa ≈ 148–175 dB (3.4–75 µV/m/√Hz, 11–250 fT/√Hz); near the Earth–ionosphere waveguide cutoff (2.5–5 kHz) the minimum drops to Fa ≈ 124 dB (≈0.1 µV/m/√Hz, ≈0.35 fT/√Hz) while the maximum stays at 170–176 dB. The Stanford eight-station survey (1985–1990s) fills in the geography: mean envelope densities at 3 kHz run from 0.3–1 fT/√Hz at polar sites to 3–27 fT/√Hz at mid- and low-latitude sites, with seasonal factors up to 4–5 and diurnal factors up to 10 in the 3–8 kHz range. A 1977–78 naval survey at 3 kHz found rms densities from a mean daily minimum of 0.3 fT/√Hz (Tromsø, March) to a mean daily maximum of 13 fT/√Hz (Italy, June). Hall, alpha-stable, Field–Lewinstein and Middleton fits are measurement-specific; most cited fitted ranges are not from below 9 kHz, and none licenses a universal 0.0625 Hz distribution. The approximately one-second impulse clusters are from a 26 kHz polar case. Some measured narrow-band cases approach Gaussian envelope statistics (Vd near 1–2 dB), but narrowing alone does not prove Gaussianity or erase temporal dependence. Wideband processing may exploit structure lost by filtering. Measured ELF results show that wideband clipping leaves a near-Gaussian residual 4–12 dB (typical) to 15–20 dB (high-level noise) below the raw density, with a spread of 1.4–4 dB. At 3 kHz, clipping in a 150 Hz band gained 3.3–6.7 dB on average (1–10 dB hour by hour) at a mid-latitude island site, little at an auroral site where the noise was near-Gaussian, and 19–26 dB during two local thunderstorms. Near 8 kHz the only figure obtained is amateur practice: blanking in a 3 kHz band gains about 20 dB with 25–35 % of samples blanked and leaves approximately Gaussian noise. Measured grid-frequency variability implies several-hertz RMS *offsets* for high-order mains harmonics near 8.27 kHz, but it does not establish their received amplitudes or crossing probabilities.

## Physical mechanisms

**Source.** Each lightning return stroke radiates a broadband pulse whose spectrum peaks near 5–10 kHz; the continuing current that follows radiates the ELF "slow tail". Maxwell & Stone summarise the stroke as leaders (~1 µs pulses every 25–100 µs, energy mostly at 30–100 kHz), a return stroke of ~100 µs with spectral peak near 9–10 kHz, and a slow tail of a few hundred amperes lasting up to ~0.5 s that supplies the ELF energy [T13, §3.5, read]. A grey summary gives about 100 strokes per second worldwide and a received spectral maximum near 5 kHz [G3, read].

**Propagation shapes the spectrum.** Below about 1.6–1.9 kHz only the quasi-TEM mode propagates; its attenuation rises with frequency, so the noise density falls roughly as f⁻² in power up to 2–3 kHz [T4 §1.1.1, read]. The first-order TE/TM modes cut off near 1.7–1.8 kHz (night) and are heavily attenuated just above cutoff, producing the noise minimum; density then rises towards 10 kHz and falls steeply above (faster than f⁻⁴) [T4 §1.1.1; T1 §2.2, read]. Because nothing propagates far in the cutoff region, a receiver there sees mainly local sources: nearby lightning and man-made fields [T4 §2.2.1; T1 §3.2, read]. Maxwell & Stone found the depth of the minimum varying from under 10 dB to about 40 dB and its frequency moving between 2 and about 7 kHz with season and time of day [T13 §3, read]. At Tromsø (70°N) a minimum near 3 kHz was always visible, its frequency most often near 3.6 kHz (about 30 % of the time); on Giglio Island, Italy (42°N), it was often not visible [T18 Summary, read]. For an ideal parallel-plate guide the nth cutoff is n·c/2h: 1.72 kHz for a night-time reflection height of 87 km and 2.14 kHz for a daytime 70 km [E]; measured night values are 1.68–1.80 kHz per mode number [P16].

**Why the noise is impulsive, and when it is not.** A receiver hears the superposition of very many weak distant sferics (a quasi-Gaussian background) plus fewer strong pulses from storms within a few thousand kilometres [T7 §III.E; T4 §2.6, read]. Whether the result is impulsive depends on the ratio of pulse rate to receiver bandwidth: a wide filter resolves individual pulses; a narrow one rings for longer than the pulse spacing and may tend toward a Gaussian limit when sufficient effectively independent finite-variance contributions overlap. Clustering, rare large events and changing intensity can invalidate that approximation. In measured cases Vd (20 log of rms/mean envelope; 1.05 dB for Gaussian noise) rises with bandwidth and with centre frequency [S1 §5.1 and Fig. 38; T1 §3.2, read].

**Dispersion.** At night, components just above each mode cutoff travel slowly, so a sferic acquires a "tweek" tail of 15–60 ms (up to ~100 ms) hooked onto n × ~1.7–1.8 kHz [P16, read]. At ELF the slow tail is delayed and smeared; Lincoln Laboratory measured pulse widths of 3–8 ms in a 10–350 Hz band [T7 Table I, read].

**Non-stationarity.** Global lightning peaks at roughly 08 UT (SE Asia), 16 UT (Africa/Europe), 20 UT (S America) and 00 UT (N America), and shifts hemisphere with season; higher frequencies are weighted towards nearer storms [T4 §2.2.2; T6, read]. Individual storms within range add bursts lasting seconds that can dominate minutes of data [T7 §III.C, read].

**Field components.** For distant sources the vertical E and horizontal H are related by about 377 Ω; Maxwell & Stone confirmed this over 30 Hz–50 kHz except for unexplained anomalies between 300 Hz and 3 kHz where E/H exceeded 377 Ω [T13 §3, read]. Inside the induction zone of a local source (r < λ/2π: 5.8 km at 8.27 kHz, 16 km at 3 kHz, 48 km at 1 kHz, 480 km at 100 Hz) the ratio is arbitrary [E: c/2πf], so E-field and loop antennas can see very different man-made interference at the same site.

### 2. Stanford global ELF/VLF noise survey

Instrument: eight "radiometers" installed 1985–86 at Arrival Heights (78°S), Dunedin (46°S), Grafton NH (44°N), Kochi (33°N), L'Aquila (42°N), Søndrestrømfjord (67°N), Stanford (37°N) and Thule (77°N); crossed loops; 16 channels at 10, 30, 80, 135, 275, 380, 500, 750 Hz and 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 4, 8, 10.2, 32 kHz, each 5 % wide; envelope sampled 10 times per second, with rms, mean, maximum and minimum formed each minute [T1 §1 and Table 1; T4 §1.2, read]. Each sample is the output of an rms detector whose rise time is short compared with the filter's response, so it follows the envelope in the 5 % band; the north–south and east–west loop outputs are combined as root-sum-square [T5 §2; T16 §2.4, §3.7, read]. The broadband ELF recording path (Stanford example) had notch filters at 60, 120, 180 and 300 Hz, and the broadband VLF path (Arrival Heights example) a single-pole 5 kHz high-pass, both to keep mains harmonics from using up dynamic range [T4 §1.1.1, read]. The 10.2 kHz channel is contaminated by Omega [T1 §2.2, read].

- Seasonal and diurnal variation become small below 1 kHz; the largest diurnal, seasonal and regional variation is between 1 and 10 kHz [T13 abstract and §3, read]. Rapid changes in rms-to-peak dynamic range occur between 3 and 8 kHz [T13 abstract, read].
- Maxwell (1967) extended the measurements to Malta, Point Barrow, Boulder, Tokyo, Hawaii, Miami and Central/South America, mostly as average-detector readings converted to rms through measured APDs, 20 Hz–30 kHz [P5, abstract]. Its figures were not read.
- Whitson (SRI, 1960), broadband 3–30 kHz, northern winter 1958–59: sferics exceeding 10 mV/m occurred about once per second at Fairbanks and Thule, falling to about 0.01 per second above 100 mV/m; at St John's, Newfoundland about 10 per second above 10 mV/m and 0.5 per second above 100 mV/m. The ratio of VLF to ELF peak-to-peak amplitude in a sferic was log-normal with a mean below 10 [T15 §II, read]. These imply exceedance rates falling as E⁻² (polar) to E⁻¹·³ (nearer storms) over that decade [E].
- Dinger, Meyers & Davis (NRL), 1–4 kHz, about 125 days in winter and summer 1977–78 at Giglio Island, Italy (42°N), and near Tromsø, Norway (70°N): air-core loops tuned to 3.0 kHz with a 150 Hz noise bandwidth, antenna-plus-preamplifier noise about −193 to −195 dBH0 (0.22–0.28 fT/√Hz) [T18, read]. Rms densities near 3 kHz ran from −192 dBH0 (mean daily minimum, Tromsø, March 1978) to −184 dBH0 (mean daily maximum), i.e. 0.32–0.79 fT/√Hz, and in Italy from −187 dBH0 (mean daily minimum, March) to −160 dBH0 (mean daily maximum, June), i.e. 0.56–12.6 fT/√Hz [T18 Summary, read; E conversion]. The night-maximum to day-minimum ratio was larger at 3 kHz than elsewhere in the ELF/VLF range and more repeatable from day to day; night-time propagation at 3 kHz was strongly anisotropic (east–west attenuation lower than north–south); several days of abnormally high daytime levels were traced to nearby thunderstorms [T18 Summary, read]. When a thunderstorm within about 100 km of Tromsø dissipated abruptly, the level fell by about 16 dB; a second local storm raised it to −165 dBH0 (7 fT/√Hz) at its peak [T18 Norway results, read]. The authors judged the attenuation-band minima of earlier surveys, Maxwell & Stone's 1963 paper among them, too high because of man-made contamination or insufficient receiver sensitivity [T18, read]; this conflicts with the low morning values above (see contradictions, item 11).
- Watt's *VLF Radio Engineering* (1967), as quoted for the New Zealand region at 1 kHz: about 10 µV/m/√Hz (33 fT/√Hz) on summer days and 0.3 µV/m/√Hz (1 fT/√Hz) on winter afternoons and evenings [P33 §4.1, secondary via Watt; E conversion].

### 4. Lincoln Laboratory and NRL ELF measurements (10–350 Hz)

- Recordings: Malta (winter 1968–69), Norway (1969), Saipan (summer–autumn 1969), plus Florida and New Hampshire; whip and two orthogonal loops; about 10–350 Hz [T7 §II, read].
- Raw spectral density at 45 Hz (1–5 minute averages): Norway median −134 dBH0 (250 fT/√Hz) with a spread of only ±5 dB; Saipan and Malta medians about 6 dB higher with ±15 dB spread; 1 % exceedance about −115 dBH0 at Saipan and Malta and −130 dBH0 in Norway; the highest Malta samples reached −111 and −116 dBH0 [T7 abstract, §III.C–D, read]. Spectral slope between 40 and 80 Hz: about 2 dB/octave at Saipan, 3–4 at Malta, 5 in Norway [T7 §VII, read].
- High levels come from rare bursts, not from a raised background: at Malta all levels above −122 dBH0 were caused by a few bursts shorter than 2 s; a single burst raised a 2-minute average by 5–30 dB; one event stood 35–40 dB above the background; between bursts the background rose by no more than 2–3 dB [T7 §III.C, read].
- Burst statistics in the 10–350 Hz band [T7 Table I, read]: pulse rate 7–13 s⁻¹, pulse width 3–8 ms, duty factor 0.02–0.07 (Norway 8–9 s⁻¹, 3–4 ms, 0.02–0.03). The authors caution that these depend on threshold definitions and pre-filtering. Florida intervals between pulses were more often short than a Poisson process predicts, with an exponential tail; return strokes about 50 ms apart are noted [T9, read].
- Mains interference at sites chosen to be remote: about −130 dBH (0.4 pT) at 60 Hz on Saipan; −105 to −125 dBH (0.7–7 pT) at 50 Hz on Malta; −100 to −120 dBH (1.3–13 pT) near Tromsø. At Malta the 50 Hz line and its third harmonic stood 10–25 dB above the noise in 0.5 Hz resolution on the east–west loop and 5–15 dB lower on the north–south loop [T7 §III.C, §X, read].

### 5. Statistical structure

**Amplitude distributions.** Chrissan fitted Hall, Field–Lewinstein and symmetric alpha-stable envelope laws to about 35 GB of survey time series (mostly 15–43 kHz in 400–2000 Hz bandwidths, plus 240 Hz and an 8 kHz sample), normalising each one-minute record to unit mean envelope [T4 §3.3–3.4, read]:

| Model | Form | Parameter range found | Typical | Fit quality |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hall | f(a) = (m−1)γ^(m−1) a/(a²+γ²)^((m+1)/2); infinite variance for m ≤ 3 (form reconstructed from a damaged scan and checked for normalisation) | m 2.2–11; γ 0.6–2.4 | m 3–4; γ 1–1.5 | 10–22 % average error; best under heavy sferic activity (low/mid latitude, storm season) |
| Alpha-stable | characteristic function exp(−γ\|ω\|^α); envelope by Fourier–Bessel transform | α 1.1–2.0; γ ≈ 0.3 | α 1.6–2.0 | 5–14 %; best far from storms (polar, off-season) |
| Field–Lewinstein | Rayleigh plus power-Rayleigh, three parameters | α 0.34–1.15; r0, r 0.1–1.0 | α 0.5–0.6; r0 0.7; r 0.25 | 29–60 %; tail decays too fast |

- Examples: Arrival Heights, 22.7 kHz, 600 Hz, May 06 UT: α = 1.844, γ = 0.293, Vd = 3.48 dB. Grafton, 17.5 kHz, 400 Hz, March 18 UT: Hall m = 3.05, γ = 0.665, Vd = 4.91 dB [T4 Tables 3.1–3.2, §3.5, read].
- At Grafton the F–L exponent moved from 0.5 in January to 0.9 in June with a further 0.2 diurnal swing; polar sites showed only mild dependence [T4 §3.4.4, read]. As Vd rose from 1.5 to 9 dB in one Arrival Heights set, Hall m fell from 10 to 2.8 and α from about 2.0 to 1.2 [T4 §3.5, read].
- 8 kHz, Arrival Heights, May 1995, bandwidth 25–1600 Hz: alpha-stable error stayed near 7.5 % at all bandwidths; Hall error grew from 9.5 to 21 % with bandwidth [T4 §3.4.1, read].
- 240 Hz, bandwidths 5–30 Hz, three polar months free of the 240 Hz mains harmonic: results did not differ from VLF; parameter ranges the same [T4 §3.4.5, read].
- Typical Vd in these analyses 1.5–6 dB, up to 10 dB at polar sites; Vd is not a reliable indicator of storm activity (Grafton: 2.5 dB in both January and July, 4.5 dB in spring and autumn) [T4 §3.5, read].
- Middleton Class A/B: Chrissan did not fit them because of their complexity, but notes that Class B (interference wider than the receiver) is the applicable case and contains Hall and alpha-stable as special cases [T4 §3.2, read]. At 3 kHz (150 Hz bandwidth), NRL fitted a three-parameter Middleton Class B envelope APD and the Field–Lewinstein model to Italian and Norwegian APDs with generally satisfactory agreement: the high-amplitude tail (exceedances below about 5–15 %) is a straight line on log–log axes, while the transition region sometimes differed by 2–3 dB. APDs were Rayleigh in low-noise periods at Tromsø and developed a pronounced tail in higher-noise periods at both sites; the fitted parameters are given only as plots [T18 Summary and APD section, read]. No tabulated Class A/B parameters were found for this band.
- Gaussian-plus-impulse models: Lincoln Laboratory reproduced ELF APDs, spectra and waveforms with a Gaussian background plus a filtered Poisson impulse process using the Table I rates and widths [T7 §III.E; T10 abstract, read]. Ginsberg reported the ELF level distribution as roughly log-normal between the 10 % and 90 % points, with local storms lifting the top 10 % [P8, secondary via T11].

**Impulsiveness versus bandwidth.** ITU Fig. 38 (MF/HF-validated) changes a 200 Hz Vd of 6 dB to about 3 dB at 2 Hz and about 11 dB at 200 kHz; a 200 Hz Vd of 10 dB becomes about 5 dB at 2 Hz [S1 Fig. 38, fig-read]. The survey's own ELF channels (0.5–4 Hz wide at 10–80 Hz) give Vd of 0.9–2.3 dB [T1 Fig. 34]. Lowering the symbol rate or widening the pre-detection bandwidth "can greatly improve performance" in sparse-impulse noise [T4 §5.4, read]. At 3 kHz the effective noise after clipping fell as the pre-clipping bandwidth was widened, with only marginal further improvement beyond about 140 Hz [T18 Noise processing, read].

**Background.** With impulses removed (usually more than 95 % of samples kept), Nyquist-spaced samples are well modelled as white Gaussian noise with clipped tails [T4 §2.6, read].

**Waveforms.** Kumar et al. (Fiji, 18°S): tweeks only at night; up to the sixth mode; first-mode cutoff about 1.8 kHz with fcn/n falling from 1.80 to 1.68 kHz for n = 1–6; durations 15–60 ms; earlier work gives 40–50 ms typical and up to about 100 ms; reflection heights 83–92 km; 90 % of sources at 1000–5000 km; quasi-TEM components are strongly attenuated above about 1 kHz [P16, read]. A 2010 waveform-bank geolocation method catalogues the dominant variation of received sferic waveforms with distance and day/night out to 6000 km [P22, abstract]. ELF slow-tail group velocity is used to sense the D region [P23, abstract].

**Temporal correlation over minutes to seasons.** Covered above: ±5 dB (Norway) to ±15 dB (Malta, Saipan) between recordings at ELF; decile deviations of 4–4.5 dB about the time-block median at 10 kHz; seasonal factors 2–5; diurnal up to 10. Year-to-year changes at fixed month are small compared with the seasonal cycle [T5 §4; T2 §2, read].

**Direction and spatial correlation.** Sferic sources located from the Arctic followed thunderstorm-day maps [T15, read], but the arrival directions of the larger ELF spikes in Norway and Malta were spread over a wider sector than storm maps suggested; subtracting correlated noise from an orthogonal sensor gained only 0.7–3 dB over single-sensor nonlinear processing [T7 App. A, read]. Amateur 8 kHz receivers synthesise a loop pattern that maximises signal-to-noise ratio, which may differ from the great-circle bearing in order to steer away from the prevailing noise [G4, read]. ITU notes up to ±5 dB directional variation for narrow beams at HF [S1 Part 2, read]; nothing equivalent is stated below 10 kHz. In 2019 data, lightning transients produced magnetic coherence between sites 1100–9000 km apart over 1–1000 Hz; above 200 Hz the night-time cross-spectral density between the two LIGO sites averaged about 180 % larger than by day [P17 §III, read]. Noise at separated sub-kHz receivers is therefore partly common, which limits what space diversity can average away [E].

Supporting detail from T7 (read):

- Residual: matched-filter outputs after the nonlinearity followed a Gaussian distribution and were nearly independent; the residual inter-sample correlation cost under 0.5 dB in the decoder (Fig. 4 and note).
- Insensitivity to raw level: effective noise rose about 0.1 dB per dB of input level at Saipan and Malta (§VI).
- Choice of nonlinearity: clipper 0.5–1.5 dB (typically about 1 dB) better than a hard limiter; hole-puncher slightly worse than the clipper (typically 0.4 dB better than a hard limiter, in several cases worse) and very sensitive to its threshold; best clip level near the wideband rms of the background, 1–4 µA/m (§V). Ideal blanking loss is 1/(1−k_p), under 1 dB for duty factors below 0.1 (§III.E). The recommended receiver was estimated to be within 1 dB of the optimum structure (§XI).
- Pre-clipping bandwidth: cutting the upper edge from 350 Hz to 200 Hz cost 0–0.5 dB; to 120 Hz 0.2–1.0 dB; to 100 Hz 0.5–1.5 dB; to 80 Hz 4.0–4.8 dB (Table II). Passing the noise through 100 m of sea water (low-pass smearing) raised effective noise by 4–6 dB on high-level tapes and not at all on quiet ones (§IV).
- Interaction with mains lines: a notch placed before the clipper rings on large spikes and cost 1–2 dB on high-level Saipan data; a clipper ahead of the notch removed that loss; 60 Hz interference up to −104 dBH (signal at 75 Hz) or 50 Hz up to −110 dBH (signal at 44 Hz) was tolerated with under 0.5 dB loss using a 26 dB notch (§X.A). The report assumed mains interference is roughly constant in amplitude and frequency over several minutes and suggested an estimate-and-subtract canceller as an alternative.
- Signal-level dependence: a 6 dB change in signal produced a 4.5–5.7 dB change in output SNR (§VIII).

Chrissan adds two cautions: applying the nonlinearity to a wide band that still contains strong man-made narrowband signals hurts detection, so those must be filtered out first; and, in his review of conventional VLF receivers, blankers perform worse than clippers because they discard samples that still carry signal [T4 §5.2.2, read]. NRL restricted its 3 kHz work to clippers because ELF results had shown them consistently better than hole-punchers and hard limiters [T18, read]. Amateur 8 kHz practice nevertheless blanks: the vlfrx-tools blanker (threshold a multiple of a moving mean, for example 12 × with a 100 s or 1 s time constant, zero dwell) is documented as essential before narrow-band detection, with its input band-limited to 2–3 kHz [G5, read via archived copy], and the EbNaut chain blanks at about 10 × the mean rectified floor in a 3 kHz band [G4]. A 1974 model study of ELF noise in polar-cap-absorption and nuclear-disturbed conditions found pulses three to four times longer than in ambient conditions but a processing-gain loss of no more than 1–2 dB; a clipper that adapts to clip a set fraction of the time (typically 20–60 %) kept near-optimum gain, adaptation being needed because the ratio of spike to background energy can change drastically [T19 Summary, read in part from a poor scan]. A modern illustration of the cost of over-blanking: removing 1–1000 Hz magnetometer data around every network-located stroke within 4000 km (each veto under 0.06 s) discarded 37 % of the time, and within 10 000 km 71 % [P17 §III.B, read].

Implications for a coherent receiver 0.06–1 Hz wide, integrating minutes to hours [E, reasoning from T4 and T7]:

- In the final bandwidth a linear receiver's noise sample is the sum of hundreds of sferic clusters per minute (about 2–5 clusters per second above 5–20 times the mean), but their number alone does not establish a Gaussian distribution because amplitudes and arrivals can be dependent or heavy-tailed. This matches the ELF measurement in which 13-minute coherent integration of a 76 Hz reference gave Gaussian matched-filter outputs whether or not clipping was used [T11 Conclusions, read]. Its variance is not stable, however: with power-law amplitudes (α below 2, Hall m near 3) the energy in an integration is dominated by the few largest pulses, which is what the Malta records show (one burst setting a 2-minute average). A Gaussian conditional on slowly varying intensity is one candidate model to validate, not a consequence of filtering. A finite physical power requires an explicit truncation if the proposed heavy-tail model has infinite variance.
- A bit lasting about 96 minutes spans a large part of the diurnal cycle; at 3–8 kHz the level can drift by several dB (up to about 10 dB at dawn or dusk in the most variable case) within one bit.
- Pulses last milliseconds against chips of seconds to a minute, so removing them costs only the blanked fraction of time, provided removal happens in a bandwidth wide enough to keep pulses short and after strong narrowband interferers have been taken out.

Bandwidth dependence, in one rule of thumb [E]: if the mean envelope is set by the Gaussian background, perfect impulse removal improves the density by about (Vd − 1.05) dB in the bandwidth where Vd is measured, less the blanking loss 10 log[1/(1 − k_p)]. When large impulses also raise the mean envelope the improvement is larger, since Vd − 1.05 dB = G − 20 log(m/m_b), where G is the improvement and m/m_b ≥ 1 is the ratio of the total to the background mean envelope; the rule is a lower estimate, not a bound. With Vd of 4.5–10.5 dB in 400 Hz at 8 kHz it gives 3.5–9.5 dB; with Vd of 1–2 dB in 4 Hz at 80 Hz it gives nothing, although the same noise yields 6–20 dB when clipped in a 300 Hz band. The 19–26 dB measured at 3 kHz during local storms [T18] and the 20 dB reported at 8 kHz in a 3 kHz band [G4] exceed the rule's values. This is a consistency argument, not a measurement.

### 7. Man-made interference

**Mains harmonics.** Levels at "remote" sites are in §4. Odd low harmonics dominate and even ones are generally absent in satellite observations above industrial regions; intensity rises with a geomagnetically-induced-current proxy at night; detection is rarer in summer, when ionospheric absorption is higher and lightning emissions obscure the lines [P27, abstract]. Lines with 50/100 Hz or 60/120 Hz spacing are seen from low Earth orbit (about 700 km in the 2015 survey), drifting very slowly if at all [P26, P27, abstracts]. On the ground, a high-latitude research receiver needed notches on odd harmonics of 50 Hz from about 950 to 4050 Hz, re-estimated every 5 s, and still showed local harmonic radiation below 0.5 kHz [P25 §2–3, read]. The survey's ELF time series were "severely contaminated" by harmonics every 50 or 60 Hz, and even slight leakage lowered Vd noticeably [T4 §3.4.5, read].

Scaling with distance [E, quasi-static, ignoring earth-return currents]: a net (unbalanced) current I gives B = μ0I/2πr, so 5 A gives 1000 pT at 1 km and 100 pT at 10 km; a balanced three-phase line with 5 m spacing carrying 500 A gives about 870 pT at 1 km and 9 pT at 10 km (1/r²). The measured 0.4–13 pT at remote sites is consistent with tens of kilometres from significant unbalanced current. Measured curves exist for 1980 Newfoundland transmission lines: near the Buchans line the 60 Hz field fell from about 30 nT at 100 m to about 0.4 nT at 0.9 km, 0.2 nT at 2 km and 6 pT at 5 km, and the highest plotted harmonics (0.9 and 1.86 kHz) from about 9 pT at 100 m to 0.2–0.35 pT at 0.9 km and 0.1–0.2 pT at 2 km [B1 Fig. 4.13, fig-read]. Unbalanced harmonic currents in 2.7–3.7 kHz were 0.3–3.2 mA rms, with ground skin depths of 240–800 m [B1 §4.5, read]; such a long rural line carried harmonic content of order one part in 10⁶, about 2500 times less than is typical of New Zealand distribution networks [P33 §2, read]. No measured curve of field against distance above 2 kHz was found.

**Mains frequency wander.** Continental Europe, 2015: frequency histogram spanning about 49.90–50.10 Hz (density falling from about 20 Hz⁻¹ at 50.00 Hz to 10⁻²–10⁻³ Hz⁻¹ at those edges), heavy-tailed (stable fit α = 1.898 ± 0.002; q-Gaussian q = 1.20 ± 0.01), with autocorrelation decaying over minutes and recurring peaks every 15, 30 and 60 minutes caused by trading intervals [P18 Figs 2–3, read]. From the plotted peak density (about 20 Hz⁻¹) the standard deviation is about 20 mHz [E]. The measured standard deviations of f − f_ref in the P19 recordings are 18.8 mHz for Continental Europe (Karlsruhe), 12.4 mHz Baltic, 48.9 mHz Nordic, 62.3 mHz Great Britain, 16.4 mHz Russia, 18.7 mHz US Western Interconnection, 17.8 mHz Texas, 93.4 mHz South Africa and 56–133 mHz for four islands; the kurtosis is 3.95 for Continental Europe and 4.05 for the Nordic area, against 3 for a Gaussian [P19 Suppl. Table II, read]. Across twelve synchronous areas: Continental Europe within about ±100 mHz at the 10⁻⁶ level; Great Britain and Nordic within about ±250–300 mHz; islands (Faroe, Iceland, Gran Canaria) to ±300–500 mHz; one-second increments up to ±20 mHz; hourly dispatch ramps about ±2 mHz/s in Continental Europe [P19 Figs 2 and 6, fig-read]. Island grids have strongly non-Gaussian increments (excess kurtosis 10–100) [P19, read].

- Relative to nominal grid frequencies, 8270 Hz is crossed by the 165th 50 Hz harmonic at 50.1212 Hz (+121 mHz) and by the 166th at 49.8193 Hz (−181 mHz). For a nominal 60 Hz system it is crossed by the 138th at 59.9275 Hz (−72.5 mHz); the 137th would require about 60.365 Hz (+365 mHz). These are kinematic equalities, not measured crossing rates. P19 reports non-Gaussian frequency distributions, and RMS alone does not assign probabilities to these offsets or tell whether a received line is strong enough to matter.
- On the measured grid records, high-order harmonics inherit frequency deviations and drift rates multiplied by harmonic number. Thus their center-frequency variability can span many 0.06–1 Hz receiver-channel widths; a fixed comb notch can miss an offset line, but interference depends on measured line amplitude and filter/canceller response. If a Gaussian-distributed frequency offset is deliberately used as a *synthetic approximation*, a constant-amplitude line A gives the illustrative peak density A/(√(2π)·σ_n)^½; this is a model equation, not a claim that measured grid offsets are Gaussian or line amplitudes are known.
- Only integer harmonics are locked to the grid. Interharmonics (ripple-control tones, converter products) do not scale this way.

**Ripple control.** Audio-frequency tones "usually between 100 and 1600 Hz" superimposed on the mains, sent as on–off-keyed telegrams 30–180 s long with pulses about 1 s; used in Germany, the Czech Republic, the Netherlands, the UK, Australia, New Zealand, South Africa and elsewhere; one cited Czech transmitter used 1050 Hz [G1, read via extraction]. In the 1050 Hz system of Dunedin, New Zealand, 18 substation injectors each typically drive 42 A; a telegram has a 5 s start tone and lasts 170 s in 7.5 s windows, and an injector may run at most three times an hour. Modelling with an 8 % phase imbalance and 10 % ground return (0.3 A radiating) gave about 4 µW total radiated power and a radiation field below the natural noise at every distance; only the induction field near the network (within about one wavelength, 300 km at 1050 Hz, by the authors' criterion) is observable, and the tone was recorded on a loop at Swampy Summit, Dunedin. The carrier was to be replaced by 317 Hz from about 2006 [P33 §2, §4, read]. Ripple tones therefore matter to receivers within or near the network that injects them.

**Electric railways.** The survey authors attribute L'Aquila's tenfold ELF excess to the Italian railway [T1 §2.2, read]. Systems at 16.7 Hz and 25 Hz and their harmonics fall in or just below the band [recollection, unverified].

**Intentional transmitters below 9 kHz.** US Navy Project ELF: 76 Hz with 45 Hz alternate, 2.6 MW input, about 8 W radiated, operational 1989, closed 2004 [G2, read via extraction]. Russian 82 Hz transmitter: its signals were used in a Stanford propagation test [T3, read]. India and China are listed as operating ELF facilities without details [G2]. The 10.2 kHz Omega signals just above the band contaminated the survey until Omega closed [T1, T4]. Geophysical controlled-source survey transmitters also work in this range [recollection, unverified].

**Other local sources.** Switching power supplies, variable-speed drives, electric-fence energisers and vehicle ignition are commonly blamed in practice; no quantitative source for their levels below 9 kHz was obtained. Amateur VLF practice names power-line harmonics and digital electronics as the dominant local interference [G4, read]; the AWESOME description notes that power-line harmonics can extend to many kilohertz, be orders of magnitude stronger than natural signals, and be partly tracked and subtracted in post-processing if they have not saturated the receiver, and it provides a 7 kHz high-pass for urban sites [P21 §III, read]. A 2025 ELF study describes non-propagating near fields of power infrastructure as the dominant noise in ELF measurements [P34 §1, read]. The ITU text warns that its noise data exclude energy entering through cables, structures, poor screening or feeder imbalance [S1 §1.1 Note 4, read], which is the dominant route for hum in an audio-frequency receiver.

### 8. Magnetospheric and other natural emissions

- At the polar stations the commonest non-sferic noise is polar chorus, concentrated at 300 Hz–2 kHz; auroral hiss is rarer but can cover the whole band to 16 kHz or more and mask most sferics [T2 abstract and §4, read]. Both reduce Vd in the affected band, because they are smoother than sferics [T2 §3, read]. One chorus event began as bursts every ~6 s at 500 Hz–1.3 kHz, widened to 2.5 kHz within about three hours and lasted more than six [T2 §4, read]. Chrissan's APD statistics exclude such noise [T4 §1.1, read]. Near Tromsø, broadband emissions identified as polar chorus appeared in the 1–4 kHz band on about 20 % of measurement days (none on Giglio Island); their APDs were Rayleigh and clipping lowered the effective noise by at most 3 dB [T18 Summary, read].
- With sferics filtered out, emissions above 4–6 kHz (to 10–15 kHz) appeared on 165 of 210 days of five winter campaigns (2006–2016) at Kannuslehto, northern Finland, mostly by day, as 1–3 minute bursts with ~1 s fine structure. The sferics were removed by zeroing 20 ms intervals of a 0.6–16 kHz filtered signal, and odd harmonics of 50 Hz from 950 to 4050 Hz were notched at frequencies re-measured every 5 s; the receiver threshold was about 0.1 fT/√Hz [P25 §2–3, read]. So a blanked high-latitude receiver may be left with a non-stationary natural residual.
- Whistlers follow sferics along field lines and return dispersed [T4 §1.1]. Schumann resonances (fundamental near 7.5–8 Hz) and geomagnetic pulsations lie below 30–50 Hz; the ITU curve labels micropulsations as dominant only below about 0.5 Hz [S1 Fig. 1; G3]. Amplitudes for chorus and hiss in fT/√Hz were not extracted (the survey shows them only in plots).

### 9. Receiver-system noise and site practice

- Instrument floors: the full 2018 LF AWESOME paper reports small-loop self-noise below 1 fT/√Hz over roughly 6–330 kHz and a larger-loop minimum near 0.03 fT/√Hz around **30 kHz**; the latter is not an 8-kHz floor. Its injected-current/network calibration and low-frequency degradation are discussed in [11](11-sub9khz-antennas-links-and-practice.md) [P20 §§II–III, Fig. 2, read]; Kannuslehto loop receiver about 0.1 fT/√Hz [P25]; the 2010 AWESOME about −10 dB re 1 fT/√Hz (0.3 fT/√Hz) input-referred at 18–30 kHz with the larger loop, enough to detect about 1 fT in 1 Hz at ELF [P21 §V, read]; the NRL 3 kHz tuned loop −193 to −195 dBH0 (0.22–0.28 fT/√Hz) [T18, read]; the Stanford radiometer's VLF loop and preamplifier well below the 10⁻⁵–10⁻⁷ V/m/√Hz typical of the background [T16 §2.2, read]; a wideband E-field receiver covering ~4 Hz–400 kHz at 1 MHz sampling with ~35 µV amplitude resolution and ~12 ns timing [P30, abstract, not re-checked]. These examples support externally noise-limited operation at some measured sites/frequencies, not a universal claim throughout the band. A particularly small measured margin occurs in the polar cutoff notch: the Tromsø minima of 0.32 fT/√Hz were within 1–3 dB of the NRL receiver's own floor [T18; E].
- Survey antennas: ELF coils of 1164 turns, buried or enclosed against wind-induced motion in the Earth's field; VLF single-turn triangular loops 18 m wide and 9 m high [T4 §1.2, read]. Coil movement from nearby construction or farming produced huge ELF increases and those data were discarded [T5 §3, read]. The ELF coils are sensitive enough that footsteps are audible and a light breeze (about 5 knots) saturates the preamplifier unless the coil sits in a wind-proof, usually buried vault on a damping suspension [T16 §2.3, read].
- Motion sensitivity [E]: a loop tilting by δθ in a 50 µT field sees δB = 50 µT × δθ, so 1 fT/√Hz corresponds to 2×10⁻¹¹ rad/√Hz. A vertical wire in the fair-weather field (roughly 100 V/m [T3]) whose effective height is modulated by a fraction ε sees an equivalent field of 100 V/m × ε, so 1 µV/m/√Hz corresponds to ε = 10⁻⁸/√Hz. Aeolian shedding from a 2 mm wire in a 5 m/s wind is near 500 Hz (Strouhal number 0.2), inside the band. No measurements of wind, rain-static or space-charge noise on E-field antennas in this band were found. NRL chose loops over whips at 3 kHz because the absolute calibration of a whip is hard to maintain there "because of the variable electrostatic effects of precipitation, wind, and humidity" [T18, read]; titles in the 1974 ELF issue show that motion- and stress-induced antenna noise was a recognised problem [P-set A, bib].
- Published site criteria actually read: 2–3 miles from even small power lines, where 60 Hz was still visible but its harmonics were not in a 7 Hz bandwidth; or 60 Hz about 15 dB below atmospheric noise in 1 Hz [T13 §2]; "as far away from power line interference as possible", yet 50, 150 and 250 Hz remained pronounced in Norway and Malta [T7 §II].
- Sound-card and preamplifier noise floors, ground-loop practice: no measured comparison with natural noise was obtained. A builder's note on the INSPIRE VLF-3 receiver gives an internal noise of about 100 µV with its input low-pass filter in (about 1 µV without it) against sferics of about 1–10 mV on a 1.5 m whip, bandwidths not stated [G6, read]. The 16-bit AWESOME digitiser sampling at 100 kHz leaves 53–75 dB, depending on gain, between the input-referred noise integrated over 1–47 kHz and the clip level, while sferics from beyond 500 km reach 1–100 pT against an rms natural density of 1–100 fT/√Hz [P21 §V, read]. For scale [E]: an antenna of effective height h_e converts 1 µV/m/√Hz to h_e µV/√Hz open-circuit, so with tens of metres of wire even the ITU minimum at 8.27 kHz (3.4 µV/m/√Hz) is tens of µV/√Hz before capacitive division, far above the nV/√Hz-class noise of audio preamplifiers; the practical limit is hum and dynamic range, not thermal noise.

Two accessible studies sharpen the geographical and statistical limits. Gu et al. analyzed Suizhou, China, measurements from February 2016 to February 2019 across 3–30 kHz and found seasonal differences up to about 10 dB. The physical receiver cadence is 10 s on followed by 50 s off in daytime, or 10 s on followed by 10 s off at night; for their analysis, the authors selected the first 10 s of each minute from this duty-cycled acquisition. The system uses orthogonal magnetic loops (7 turns, 7.29 m² equivalent area) and a 16-bit, 250 ksample/s digitizer. Welch estimates use 4096-sample segments and 2048 overlap (about 61 Hz resolution). The paper reports narrow-band amplitude levels at 12 frequencies, including 4, 6, and 8 kHz with 2 kHz bands, and day/month variation. Its lightning comparison says the ≤3,500 km rings dominate while more distant lightning contributes less; the analysis bins daily lightning occurrence against monthly averaged band amplitude, and the authors note that stroke intensity is not included. The linked Figshare v1 deposit was retrieved and fully enumerated again in the completion pass: the 22,627,532-byte RAR, posted 7 January 2021 by Sunny Li under CC BY 4.0, matches its published MD5 `993657aa0ff2a9f87bf09671e853a7f1`. Its 28 members include substantially more than the small figure-series samples inspected earlier. The large Figure1 MATLAB workspace contains minute-band amplitudes and one day's two-component spectral arrays as well as the published monthly products. There is still no high-rate waveform. The completed numeric audit below establishes what can be used without inventing calibration or a sub-hertz law [P47, P50].

Reuveni et al. analyzed 386 days at Sde-Boker, Israel, sampling one-minute records every 15 minutes. Their 4 and 7.5 kHz Vd values use 75 Hz bandwidth; hourly and seasonal histograms differ, and for the whole-period Vd CDF at 4 kHz the two-Gaussian fit had R² 0.985 versus 0.884 Poisson, 0.801 Rayleigh and 0.896 lognormal. These fits describe Vd (20 log₁₀[H_rms/H_avg]), not the field-amplitude or PSD distribution [P48]. Wang et al. (2025) process four years of Suizhou data (April 2018–November 2021) to identify over 1.7 million tweeks and study occurrence, propagation distance and cutoff frequency. Their 250 ksample/s records are acquired 10 s on/10 s off; only detections passing a −15 dB spectrogram threshold are included. This demonstrates a substantial event-labelled source for *selected dispersive tweeks*, but the paper does not provide an ambient-noise APD, calibrated field series, or arrival process for all sferics [P49]. None of these studies measures the target 0.06-Hz channel, gives a matched long-aperture outage distribution, or licenses independent draws of maximum lightning, path attenuation and interference. Use them as evidence that site, time and bandwidth condition the noise process, not as a universal generator law [P47–P49].

## How the literature models or simulates this

- **Level models.** ITU-R P.372 Fig. 1 gives only a min/max envelope below 10 kHz; the full lightning model (maps, frequency curves, Du/Dl, Vd-indexed APD family) and its software (ITU-R HF noise code, linked from S1) begin at 10 kHz and rest on CCIR 322 measurements at 13 kHz–20 MHz in 200 Hz bandwidth [S1; T14]. The Warber & Field TE/TM long-wave noise prediction model computes noise from storm distributions and propagation [P12, secondary; original not read]. Raab's full-text low-frequency through-the-Earth model includes impulsiveness, arrival direction, local-ground and six-component covariance effects, and is reported checked against Vermont field tests; it addresses a different link/receiver geometry and is not a narrowband sub-9-kHz field calibration [P31, read]. Just above the band, a thunderstorm-day-driven model (the Westinghouse COMPWR and NOISLAN programs, extended by NRL as HORNS to horizontally and vertically polarised noise at elevated receivers) converts thunderstorm-day statistics into lightning source powers and propagates them in waveguide modes for 10–30 kHz [T20, read in part]; VALERIE (2007) combines several years of noise measurements with satellite lightning data for 10–80 kHz; in cross-validation it was more accurate than the ITU model, and it has been validated for the Atlantic area only [P35, abstract].
- **First-order amplitude models.** Hall (generalised-t), Field–Lewinstein, alpha-stable, Middleton Class B, log-normal and mixture models; validated domain and parameter ranges in §5. Known failures: Field–Lewinstein tails too light; Hall degrades with increasing bandwidth and in quiet conditions; alpha-stable degrades under heavy local activity; all are first-order only and say nothing about time structure [T4].
- **Time-structure models.** Gaussian background plus filtered Poisson impulses (Modestino; Evans) [T7, T10]; Giordano & Haber and Furutsu & Ishida dependent-pulse models [P11, secondary]; Chrissan's clustering Poisson model, which reproduces the interval histogram, yields an alpha-stable envelope, and leaves the spectrum unchanged [T4 ch. 4]. Its assumptions (sources uniform in direction and stationary in time) are admitted not to hold.
- **Replay of recordings.** The ELF receiver designs were sized by replaying hundreds of wideband tapes through simulated receivers, on the grounds that a 1 dB error mattered and that worst-case statistics could not be had from models [T7 §II]. Chrissan likewise used recorded noise for all error-rate simulations [T4 §5.4].
- **Performance metric.** "Effective noise level" N_eff = 2H_s²T/SNR_out lets a nonlinear receiver be treated as a linear one in Gaussian noise of that density [T7 eq. 2].
- **Source catalogues.** Global lightning networks give stroke time, place and peak current that can drive a propagation code; P17 used GLD360 this way for coincidence, not for noise synthesis, and P34 uses WWLLN locations to time ELF impulses. No published network-driven noise simulator for this band was located.
- **Mains frequency.** An Ornstein–Uhlenbeck swing-equation model with non-Gaussian driving noise and deterministic steps at trading intervals reproduces measured grid-frequency statistics [P18, P19]. This constrains the frequency process only; it does not provide the line's magnetic/electric field strength at an antenna or its interference-to-background ratio.
- **Multicomponent receiver model.** Raab (2010) is now full-text read, not merely bibliographic. It develops a through-the-Earth communication noise model with impulsive statistics, arrival direction and local-ground effects, and six-component covariance; it reports comparison with measurements at three Vermont sites in summer and winter. Its application is that link geometry, field-component covariance and local ground can matter to processing. It does not supply a calibrated sub-9-kHz/0.0625-Hz distribution for this modem [P31].

## What a high-fidelity simulation would need

These are options with their limits; none is offered as the definitive design, and better approaches may exist.

**Inputs**
1. Site latitude and distance to storm regions; season; local time; day or night along the dominant paths.
2. Antenna type (vertical E or loop, loop azimuth) and distance to power infrastructure.
3. Grid nominal frequency and synchronous area.
4. Receiver chain: pre-detection bandwidth, sample rate, order of hum removal and nonlinearity, clip or blank threshold.

**Model-structure options**
- *A. Replay of wideband recordings* made with the intended antenna type, at the intended latitude and season. Highest fidelity for clustering, tweeks, hum and their interaction with the receiver; limited by availability, by recorder dynamic range (which hid part of the gain in T11), and by the impossibility of sampling rare worst cases.
- *B. Factorized sensitivity generator.* Independently specify (i) a measured or declared band PSD, (ii) a proper complex-Gaussian stationary background, (iii) optional deterministic/specified event waveforms and times, (iv) an independently declared line-frequency trace and line amplitudes, and (v) slow level modulation. This prevents unidentified layers from being presented as an empirically joint distribution. P.372 can set a standards comparison level, and Gu et al. can inform a Suizhou 4/6/8 kHz *regional band-level* comparison; neither yields the target bandwidth law. Rates, event amplitudes, cross-layer correlations, and local mains field strength remain uncalibrated below 9 kHz.
- *C. Physics-driven synthesis* from a lightning catalogue and a waveguide propagation code. Could reproduce geography, direction and day/night structure, and E/B differences near cutoff; limited by catalogue detection efficiency for weak strokes (which form the background) and by computing cost. Not verified against the literature for this chapter.
- *D. Reduced model for a receiver that already clips or blanks:* white Gaussian noise at the effective density, with a slow log-normal level process (σ 1.5–4 dB, diurnal swing 4–10 dB at land sites and up to 23 dB at sea) and residual mains lines. Justified at ELF by T7, T8, T11 and T17, and its Gaussian effective-noise premise at 3 kHz by T18; unverified near 8 kHz. It cannot test the nonlinearity itself.
- A plain white Gaussian model at the raw rms density is pessimistic by the processing gain for a well-designed receiver and, for a linear receiver, misses the burst-driven outages; at the ITU minimum it is optimistic for any populated site.

**Observables to validate against**
- Spectrum: ITU min/max envelope; station spectra in §2; notch depth and frequency statistics [T13].
- Vd against bandwidth and frequency [T1; S1 Fig. 38 with its caveat]; envelope APD against Hall and alpha-stable fits [T4].
- Interval histogram: exponential beyond 1 s, excess below; interval correlation 0.15–0.20 [T4].
- Exceedance rate against threshold [T15]; pulse rate, width and duty factor after the intended front-end filter [T7 Table I].
- Effective noise after the intended nonlinearity: insensitivity to raw level (about 0.1 dB/dB), Gaussian matched-filter outputs, loss against pre-clipping bandwidth [T7]; at 3 kHz, gain against pre-clipping bandwidth (little beyond about 140 Hz), season, hour and clip level [T18].
- Level statistics over hours: deciles, diurnal and seasonal factors.
- Mains comb: line wander proportional to harmonic number; dwell time in the signal bin.

**Conditional control patterns for the separate experiments.** These patterns still require the explicitly named spectrum, recorded waveform/grid trace and sweep coordinates below; this paragraph alone is not an executable specification. Fully defined numerical comparisons are in [81](81-scenarios-sub9.md) and [85](85-scenarios-noise-and-equipment.md). None of these patterns establishes natural event probability or prevalence. Use the receiver's actual complex sample rate `Fs` and declared two-sided complex-baseband PSD `N0`; the Gaussian control is `n[k] ~ CN(0, N0·Fs)`, so `E|n[k]|²=N0·Fs` under that PSD convention. Set `N0` to the chosen P.372 hourly comparison point or to an explicitly measured receiver/site spectrum, and record which. Then run: (1) stationary Gaussian only; (2) the same Gaussian with a deterministic triangular level offset `L(t)` of 0, ±6, and ±12 dB over a 24 h cycle (sweep each level separately; these are sensitivity coordinates, not a claimed observed distribution); (3) the Gaussian plus one measured-and-calibrated wideband event waveform at scheduled offsets 0, 30, and 60 s, with event peak scaled to background ratios +10, +20, and +40 dB (stress sweeps; no occurrence probabilities); and (4) the Gaussian plus a comb with `f_n(t)=n f_grid(t)`, using an explicitly sourced/saved grid-frequency trace where available, while sweeping each injected line amplitude independently (e.g. −60 to 0 dB relative to integrated in-band noise power in named fixed steps). If an event waveform cannot be calibrated below 9 kHz, label the pulse as a synthetic impulse-response probe, not a sferic. Do not combine the independently swept maxima into a “natural worst-case” probability. These simple controls separate noise variance, slow level changes, transient sensitivity, and tonal rejection; an empirically coupled layer should replace them only when matched field recordings support its joint parameters.

Row 1's −36 dB-Hz needs a received field of 0.054–1.2 µV/m against the raw ITU range, 0.16 µV/m against a moderate 10 µV/m/√Hz, and about a tenth of that after a 20 dB blanking gain [E; S1, G4] |
| Noise statistics | White complex Gaussian of fixed variance | Useful null/control only. The literature does not establish a universal amplitude law for 0.0625 Hz below 9 kHz. Hall/alpha-stable fits, narrow-band Gaussian approximations, and post-clip effective-noise laws remain conditional on their measured bandwidth, site, statistic, and receiver [T4, T7, T18, P48]. Do not sample those fit parameters as target-band truth. |
| Impulses | Single-sample impulses of one amplitude at a Bernoulli rate, at most one per sample | Does not reproduce physical event waveform or tested clustering. The cited 3–8 ms duration is after a 10–350 Hz front end; 15–100 ms tweek tails concern propagation/event observations; 1 s/5–10 pulse clustering is a 26 kHz case, and the broad St John's count is not a sub-band law [T7, P16, T15, T4]. Until target-band marked records exist, use deterministic injected probe pulses or explicitly labelled borrowed waveforms, not a synthetic natural event probability. |
| Interfering tone | One receiver-coordinate tone of fixed amplitude and frequency (with Shift 0, receiver coordinates are the radio frequency) | Candidate stressor is a 50/60 Hz harmonic comb, with amplitude and geometry explicitly declared. Harmonic frequency offsets scale by harmonic number; the derived RMS values do not specify line strength, sweep rate, or occupancy of 0.0625-Hz bins. Ripple-control telegrams (100–1600 Hz) and high-latitude chorus/hiss are separate context-specific effects, not a single universal mixture [P19, P18, P33, T2, T18] |
| Doppler and delay of Sub9 presets | Wander and offset bounds of 0.001–0.05 Hz; echo delay to 5 ms | Not noise parameters. A mains harmonic can move across a narrow receive channel if its center frequency enters the signal search, but the occurrence and interference impact require the measured grid trace plus field strength, antenna coupling, filter and modem response. RMS grid statistics alone do not establish a 4–6σ crossing probability or reception failure [E; P19] |
| Front end | Optional quantisation, clipping, one-pole low-pass, one gain step, overload recovery | Dynamic range is a real constraint: sferics from beyond 500 km reach 1–100 pT against an rms background of 1–100 fT/√Hz, and a 16-bit digitiser leaves 53–75 dB between its noise and its clip level [P21]; recorder clipping hid part of the measured ELF gains [T11, T12] |

The fixed-variance white complex Gaussian is a useful receiver-noise control; it is not thereby an evidence-backed post-blanker approximation for every site. It cannot test the nonlinearity, mains comb, or burst-driven outage mechanism. In the original Shift 0/audio configuration, Helper pilots at 550 and 950 Hz coincide with the 11th and 19th 50 Hz harmonics. The original high pilot at 2,400 Hz is also harmonic-aligned (48×50 or 40×60 Hz). This frequency statement is configuration-specific: high-passband Robust DATA operation can set the high pilot dynamically above the passband, so the 2,400 Hz statement does not apply to that path. For line offsets, multiply the fundamental grid-frequency deviation by harmonic number; this gives a frequency offset, not calibrated interference amplitude or a probability of a line falling in the modem channel [E; P19].

## Contradictions, open questions, and things not found

1. **ITU statement versus measurements.** P.372-18 says variation with season, time and place is very small below 10 kHz, while conceding more variability above 100 Hz. The Stanford survey found seasonal factors of 2–5, a diurnal factor near 10 at 3–8 kHz, and station-to-station factors of 10–50 at 2–8 kHz; Maxwell & Stone located the largest variations at 1–10 kHz. The ITU wording is adequate below about 100 Hz only.
2. **ITU curves are not bounds.** (a) Arrival Heights at 8 kHz (mean envelope 1.6–2.0 fT/√Hz; perhaps 3–6 rms) lies below the ITU minimum (11 fT/√Hz); Søndrestrømfjord (3–5.5 mean; perhaps 8–18 rms) straddles it. (b) Mid-latitude means at 30 Hz (400–650 fT/√Hz) exceed the ITU maximum (285 fT/√Hz), although local interference may contribute. (c) Lincoln Laboratory medians at 45 Hz for Saipan and Malta (about −128 dBH0) sit above the ITU maximum (−133 dBH0), and the 1 % levels are 18 dB above it. (d) Fraser-Smith & Bowen give the natural background at 50/60 Hz as typically 150–600 fT/√Hz [P15, abstract], up to about twice the ITU maximum at 50 Hz (274 fT/√Hz). Part of this is definitional (hourly median of what statistic, in what bandwidth), which the Recommendation does not specify.
3. **Shape of the notch.** ITU's minimum curve bottoms at 4–4.5 kHz and is 30 times lower in amplitude than at 8 kHz; polar survey spectra are much flatter (0.3–1 fT/√Hz at 2–4 kHz, 1.6–5.5 at 8 kHz). Receiver floor or local interference in the survey's 2–4 kHz channels may explain it; this is unresolved. The NRL Tromsø minima near 3 kHz (0.32 fT/√Hz) match the ITU minimum at 3 kHz but lie within 1–3 dB of that receiver's own floor, and the Tromsø spectral minimum was most often near 3.6 kHz rather than 4–4.5 kHz [T18].
4. **Mean versus rms.** The survey's long-term figures are means of a short-time envelope detector. The rms figures derived here apply a one-minute Vd and ignore minute-to-minute variance, so they are low-biased for long averages. The NRL 3 kHz figures are rms densities, and the ITU curves hourly medians of rms values.
5. **Blanker versus clipper.** Lincoln Laboratory found the hole-puncher slightly worse than the clipper and much more sensitive to its threshold; Chrissan's review and NRL both treat clippers as better than blankers; an adaptive clipper clipping 10–40 % of the time was judged near-optimum at ELF [T7, T4, T18, T17]. Amateur 8 kHz practice nevertheless blanks, at 10–12 × the mean in a 2–3 kHz band, blanking 25–35 % of the signal and reporting about 20 dB [G4, G5]. The difference is probably pre-detection bandwidth and pulse duty factor, not a true conflict, but no source compares the two directly near 8 kHz.
6. **Gain near 8 kHz has no controlled measurement in the sources read.** The quantitative effective-noise data are for 40–80 Hz signals in a 100–350 Hz band, for 3 kHz in a 150 Hz band [T18], or error-rate curves at 18–45 kHz; the 8 kHz figure is a practitioner's statement with one worked example [G4].
7. **Assumption of stable mains frequency** (T7, 1972: constant over several minutes) is contradicted by modern grid data showing step changes at trading intervals and heavy tails.
8. **Primary-source access register (status inherited from the first pass; the targeted November follow-up did not re-open every historical item).** Watt, *VLF Radio Engineering*: used only through the named secondary discussions in P33/T18, not as a directly checked source. Evans & Griffiths (1974): paper not obtained; relevant conclusions retained only where supported by the read NRL/Stanford summaries T17/T11/T4. Bernstein et al. (1974), Ginsberg (1974), and Ingram (1984): bibliographic records only; no claim here depends on their un-read details. Field & Lewinstein (1978): the original paper was not directly read; model characterization is attributed to T4/P31 rather than represented as a check of its original derivation. Raab (2010): **resolved in follow-up**; publisher full text read, but its through-the-Earth and multicomponent model is not a sub-9-kHz narrowband calibration [P31]. 9. **Evidence gaps after the dated follow-up search:** tabulated Middleton Class A/B or Gaussian-mixture parameters for 30 Hz–9 kHz (3 kHz fits exist only as plots, T18); target-band cluster statistics at 1–9 kHz; APDs for local-storm conditions beyond one 3 kHz example at a polar site (T18); measured harmonic field strength against distance above 2 kHz; measured (not modelled) radiated levels of ripple-control tones; quantitative wind, rain-static or space-charge noise on E-field antennas (only a qualitative statement, T18); published sound-card noise comparisons beyond a builder's note (G6); chorus and hiss densities in fT/√Hz at ground level; and a new global calibrated noise survey. Recent tweek-detection/occurrence papers were found, but not a new marked sferic-clustering model for this band. These outcomes apply only to the recorded search/access routes. Topics not followed in depth in this pass: noise beside large ELF facilities and quantitative rain-static studies on E-field antennas.
10. **Combinations not observed together.** The "worst case" row combines summer-night maximum levels, nearby storms and a mains harmonic crossing the signal; no source documents all three at once.
11. **Earlier attenuation-band minima.** NRL judged the 1–4 kHz minima of earlier surveys, including Maxwell & Stone's 1963 paper, too high because of man-made noise or receiver sensitivity [T18]. Yet the 1962 DECO California morning points reach about −23 dB re 1 µV/m/√Hz (0.24 fT/√Hz) at 2–3 kHz [T13 Fig. 6, re-checked], below the ITU minimum (0.32–0.41 fT/√Hz) and as low as NRL's own receiver floor (0.22–0.28 fT/√Hz). The DECO points were read by eye from an average-reading meter on a 7 Hz analyser (T13 §2), and the NRL remark may refer to the summary curves of the 1963 paper; the discrepancy is unresolved.
12. **Measured gains against the Vd rule.** The 19–26 dB measured at 3 kHz during local storms and the 20 dB reported at 8 kHz exceed the 3.5–9.5 dB that the Vd rule of §6 gives from the survey's 400 Hz Vd values. The rule is a lower estimate, and Vd measured in a 5 % band understates the impulsiveness seen by a blanker working in 2–3 kHz, since Vd rises with bandwidth [S1 Fig. 38; T1; E].
